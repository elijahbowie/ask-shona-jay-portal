# Estimated Tax Payment System

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The federal tax system operates on a pay-as-you-go basis: if you earn income that is not subject to employer withholding -- such as self-employment income, S-corporation distributions, partnership income, rental income, or capital gains -- the IRS expects you to remit taxes in four quarterly installments throughout the year rather than in one lump sum at filing. Missing those installments, or underpaying them, triggers a penalty even if you pay the full balance by April 15. This playbook shows you exactly how to calculate, schedule, and pay quarterly estimated taxes for 2026, how to choose the method that fits your income pattern, and how to avoid the underpayment penalty entirely through one of two IRS-approved safe harbor routes.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy applies to you if any of the following describe your situation for tax year 2026:

- **Self-employed individuals (sole proprietors, single-member LLCs):** All net business income flows to your personal return with no employer withholding, so you are almost certainly required to make quarterly payments.
- **S-corporation shareholders who receive distributions in addition to (or instead of) a salary:** The W-2 salary portion is subject to withholding, but pass-through profit distributions are not. If those untaxed distributions are large enough, quarterly payments are required.
- **Partners in a partnership or multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership:** Partnership income passes through to your Form 1040 without withholding. You pay estimated tax on your allocable share.
- **1099-NEC / independent contractor income earners:** Any taxpayer who receives Form 1099-NEC, 1099-MISC, or 1099-K for services without withholding.
- **Investors and rental property owners:** Capital gains, dividends, and net rental income not covered by withholding count toward the estimated tax threshold.
- **Employees with insufficient withholding:** If you had significant side income, a large bonus with under-withheld tax, or exercised stock options, your W-4 withholding may not cover your full liability.
- **Any individual who expects to owe $1,000 or more in federal income tax after subtracting withholding and refundable credits** when their 2026 return is filed.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **W-2 employees with only wage income and adequate withholding:** If your employer withholds enough tax from each paycheck to satisfy one of the safe harbor tests, quarterly estimated payments are not required. You may still owe at filing, but you are not required to make installments.
- **Taxpayers who expect to owe less than $1,000 after withholding:** The $1,000 threshold is a hard floor. Below it, no estimated tax payments are required and no underpayment penalty applies.
- **Farmers and fishers:** Special rules apply that reduce the number of required installments to one. This playbook does not address those rules; consult your advisor.
- **Prior year with zero tax liability:** If your 2025 tax return showed $0 in tax owed AND you were a U.S. citizen or resident for all of 2025, you owe no estimated tax for 2026 regardless of this year's income. (This exception does not apply if your 2025 return covered less than 12 months.)
- **Taxpayers expecting a large refund from over-withheld wages:** If your W-2 withholding will cover 100% (or 110%) of your prior-year tax liability, you satisfy the safe harbor regardless of what you earn elsewhere.

## Key Terms

**Estimated tax:** Tax you pay to the IRS in advance, in quarterly installments, on income that is not subject to employer withholding. Governed by IRC Section 6654 and the regulations under it.

**Pay-as-you-go:** The IRS principle, established in IRC Section 6654, that taxes must be remitted throughout the year as income is earned -- not just at the end of the year when the return is filed.

**Safe harbor:** A set of IRS-defined payment thresholds that, if met, guarantee you will owe no underpayment penalty regardless of how much tax you ultimately owe when you file. There are two safe harbors for individuals (described in "The Numbers" section below).

**Underpayment penalty:** A penalty assessed under IRC Section 6654 when a taxpayer fails to pay enough tax during the year through withholding and/or estimated payments. It is calculated as interest on the shortfall for each quarter, not a flat fee.

**Withholding credit:** The income tax withheld from your W-2 wages (or backup withholding from investment accounts) that counts toward your total prepayment of tax, reducing or eliminating the amount you need to pay as estimated tax.

**Prior-year tax liability:** The "total tax" shown on last year's Form 1040, Line 24. This figure is computed after all nonrefundable credits (such as the child tax credit and education credits) have already reduced the tax, but before subtracting withholding, estimated tax payments, or refundable credits. It is the anchor figure for the prior-year safe harbor calculation. (Form 2210 also references this amount through its own internal line structure, but the source number is always 1040 Line 24.)

**Adjusted gross income (AGI):** Your total gross income minus specific above-the-line deductions such as the deduction for half of self-employment tax, IRA contributions, and student loan interest. Reported on line 11 of Form 1040. Your prior-year AGI determines whether you must pay 100% or 110% of last year's tax to satisfy the safe harbor.

**Self-employment (SE) tax:** The 15.3% tax that self-employed individuals pay in lieu of the FICA taxes an employer and employee would each pay. It consists of 12.4% for Social Security (on net earnings up to the wage base) and 2.9% for Medicare (on all net earnings). You include projected SE tax in your estimated tax calculation.

**Net earnings from self-employment:** Gross business income minus deductible business expenses, multiplied by 92.35%. The 92.35% multiplier removes the employer-equivalent portion of SE tax before applying the SE tax rate, mirroring what an employee would pay.

**Annualized income installment method:** An IRS-approved alternative to equal quarterly payments that lets you calculate each installment based on how much income you actually earned through that point in the year, rather than spreading your projected annual liability evenly. Used when income arrives unevenly (for example, a business with a strong Q4). Calculated on Form 2210, Schedule AI.

**Form 1040-ES:** The IRS form used by individuals (including sole proprietors, partners, and S-corp shareholders) to calculate and mail estimated tax payments. Each quarterly payment coupon identifies the period it covers and the payment due date.

**Form 2210:** IRS form used to calculate whether you owe an underpayment penalty and, if so, how much. Also used to request a penalty waiver and to use the annualized income installment method. For tax year 2025, the IRS released the 2025 Instructions for Form 2210; 2026-specific instructions will be released before the 2026 filing season.

**EFTPS (Electronic Federal Tax Payment System):** The IRS's free electronic payment system at eftps.gov, historically used by individuals and businesses to schedule federal tax payments online. As of 2026, the IRS has discontinued new individual enrollments in EFTPS; individual taxpayers should use IRS Direct Pay or their IRS Online Account instead. Businesses and existing EFTPS users may still use EFTPS.

**IRS Direct Pay:** A free IRS web tool at directpay.irs.gov that lets individuals make estimated tax payments directly from a bank account with no enrollment required. Payments can be scheduled up to a year in advance. A confirmation number is issued for each payment.

**IRS Online Account:** The individual taxpayer portal at irs.gov/account where you can make payments, view payment history, see prior-year tax information, and manage estimated tax payments.

**Federal short-term rate:** An interest rate published by the IRS quarterly based on U.S. Treasury bill rates. The underpayment penalty rate for individuals equals the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, updated each quarter.

**Schedule AI (Annualized Income Installment Method):** A schedule attached to Form 2210 that allows taxpayers with uneven income to calculate each quarterly installment based on income actually received through each cutoff date, rather than using equal amounts.

**Social Security wage base:** The maximum amount of earned income subject to the Social Security portion (12.4%) of the SE tax in a given year. Earnings above this threshold are still subject to the 2.9% Medicare portion of SE tax. For 2026, the Social Security wage base is $184,500.

**Additional Medicare Tax:** An extra 0.9% Medicare tax on wages, compensation, and self-employment income that exceeds $200,000 (single or head of household), $250,000 (married filing jointly), or $125,000 (married filing separately). Employers withhold this only when wages exceed $200,000 from that single employer; you must account for the remainder through estimated tax or additional withholding.

**W-4:** The IRS form an employee submits to their employer to direct how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. S-corp owner-employees can adjust their W-4 to increase withholding on their salary, which may reduce or eliminate the need for separate quarterly payments.

**Refundable credit:** A tax credit that can reduce your tax liability below zero, generating a refund. Examples include the Earned Income Tax Credit and the refundable portion of the Child Tax Credit. Refundable credits count as prepaid tax and reduce your estimated tax requirement.

**QBID (Qualified Business Income Deduction):** A deduction of up to 20% of qualified business income for eligible pass-through entity owners under IRC Section 199A. Beginning in 2026, a minimum QBID of $400 is available if you have at least $1,000 in qualified business income from a qualified trade or business in which you materially participate (within the meaning of IRC Section 469(h)). Passive owners who do not materially participate do not qualify for the $400 minimum. QBID reduces your taxable income and therefore your projected tax, which affects your estimated payment calculation.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these items before beginning your estimated tax calculation for 2026:

1. **Your 2025 federal tax return (Form 1040):** You need (a) your total 2025 federal income tax liability (line 24 of the 2025 Form 1040), (b) your 2025 adjusted gross income (line 11), and (c) any refundable credits you claimed.
2. **Projected 2026 gross income by source:** Business income (net of expenses), W-2 wages, rental income, investment income (dividends, interest, capital gains), partnership or S-corp K-1 income.
3. **Projected 2026 deductions:** Standard deduction or estimated itemized deductions, projected self-employment tax deduction (half of SE tax), IRA/HSA/SEP contribution deductions, QBID estimate, and any other above-the-line deductions.
4. **Projected 2026 withholding:** How much will be withheld from W-2 wages and investment accounts for the full year? Your most recent pay stub and brokerage tax-withholding election will show this.
5. **Your 2026 filing status:** Single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, head of household, or qualifying surviving spouse. This affects your standard deduction, tax brackets, and the Additional Medicare Tax threshold.
6. **Prior-year AGI to determine which safe harbor applies:** If your 2025 AGI exceeded $150,000 ($75,000 if you will file married filing separately for 2025), you must use the 110% safe harbor instead of the 100% safe harbor.
7. **State tax obligations:** Each state with an income tax has its own estimated payment schedule, thresholds, and safe harbor rules. Gather your most recent state tax return and confirm your state's quarterly due dates with your advisor.

## Required Documents And Records

**Documents to collect before making payments:**

- Copy of 2025 Form 1040 (for prior-year liability and AGI)
- 2025 Schedule SE (self-employment tax calculation, if applicable)
- Most recent profit-and-loss statement or bookkeeping report for 2026
- Current-year W-2s or pay stubs showing year-to-date withholding
- Brokerage statements or 1099-DIV/1099-INT estimates for 2026

**Records to keep after making each payment:**

- IRS Direct Pay confirmation number (screenshot or email confirmation) or EFTPS payment confirmation
- Date, amount, and tax period covered by each payment
- Copy of each Form 1040-ES voucher if paying by mail
- Bank statements showing the debit for each estimated tax payment

**Retention period:** The IRS generally has three years from the filing date to audit your return. Keep payment records for at least four years from the due date of the return to which the payments apply (longer if your return has a significant understatement of income, which extends the statute to six years). Retaining records indefinitely for returns that involve business asset basis is advisable.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

**Step 1: Retrieve your 2025 tax return.**
Pull your 2025 Form 1040. Record three numbers: (a) Line 24, "Total tax" -- this is your prior-year tax liability for safe harbor purposes. (b) Line 11, "Adjusted gross income" -- this determines whether your safe harbor is 100% or 110% of (a). (c) Line 25 through 32 combined, or simply the total withholding/credits already paid -- this shows what you already prepaid in 2025.

**Step 2: Calculate your prior-year safe harbor amount.**
If your 2025 AGI (from step 1b) was $150,000 or less (or $75,000 or less if you will file 2026 married filing separately), multiply your 2025 total tax (from step 1a) by 100%. That product is your safe harbor target. If your 2025 AGI exceeded $150,000 (or $75,000 for MFS), multiply your 2025 total tax by 110% instead. Example: 2025 total tax was $40,000 and AGI was $200,000. Safe harbor = $40,000 x 110% = $44,000.

**Step 3: Determine your projected 2026 tax liability.**
Use the Estimated Tax Payment System Implementation Worksheet (see "Using The Download(s)" below) or work through Form 1040-ES Worksheet 1. Project your 2026: (a) gross income from all sources, (b) above-the-line deductions (including half of SE tax if self-employed), (c) taxable income after standard or itemized deductions, (d) income tax using the 2026 tax brackets, (e) self-employment tax if applicable (net earnings x 92.35% x 15.3%, subject to the wage base for the Social Security portion), (f) Additional Medicare Tax if applicable, (g) minus any expected credits. The result is your projected 2026 tax liability.

**Step 4: Subtract projected 2026 withholding.**
From your projected 2026 tax liability (step 3), subtract your estimated total W-2 withholding and any other withholding for the full year. The remainder is the additional tax you must cover through estimated payments.

**Step 5: Choose your payment target.**
Pick the lower of:
- (A) 90% of your projected 2026 tax liability (from step 3), or
- (B) 100% or 110% of your 2025 tax liability (from step 2).

The lower amount is your "required annual payment." If your projected withholding already covers this amount, you may not owe any quarterly estimated tax -- but confirm this with your advisor.

**Step 6: Divide into four equal installments.**
Divide your required annual payment (from step 5) by 4 to get the equal quarterly amount. This is the standard (equal installment) approach and is the simplest method for most taxpayers.

**Step 7: Consider the annualized income installment method if your income is uneven.**
If your income is heavily weighted toward later quarters (for example, a consultant who closes large contracts in Q4, or a real estate investor who closes sales in November), equal installments may cause you to overpay early quarters. In that case, use Form 2210, Schedule AI to calculate installments based on actual income earned through each quarter's cutoff. This method requires more recordkeeping but can lower your early payments without penalty. Flag this option for your advisor before using it.

**Step 8: Set up your payment method.**
Individuals: go to directpay.irs.gov and select "Estimated Tax" as the payment type, or log in to your IRS Online Account at irs.gov/account. Businesses and existing EFTPS users: log in at eftps.gov. If paying by mail, complete the appropriate voucher from Form 1040-ES and mail it to the address on the form for your state. Confirm the mailing address on the current Form 1040-ES; addresses vary by location.

**Step 9: Schedule all four 2026 payments in advance.**
IRS Direct Pay allows payments to be scheduled up to a year in advance. After calculating your amounts in steps 1-7, schedule all four installments at once. This eliminates the risk of a missed deadline. Record each confirmation number.

**Step 10: Review and adjust mid-year.**
After Q2 ends (roughly mid-year), compare actual income and expenses to your projections. If you earned significantly more or less than projected, recalculate steps 3 and 4 and adjust the remaining Q3 and Q4 payments. You do not need to "make up" underpaid earlier quarters with a later payment to avoid the penalty -- but you must pay the penalty for the quarters that were short. Consult your advisor before significantly reducing a remaining payment.

**Step 11: Reconcile at filing.**
When you prepare your 2026 Form 1040, report all four estimated tax payments on Schedule 3 (Additional Credits and Payments), which flows to the main return. The return will show whether you paid enough, owe a balance, or are due a refund. If you underpaid for any quarter, Form 2210 will calculate any penalty owed.

**Step 12: Adjust withholding for next year.**
If you consistently owe large balances or receive large refunds, the more efficient fix is adjusting W-4 withholding (for W-2 income) or recalibrating your quarterly estimates. If you are an S-corp owner-employee, you can increase your W-4 withholding on your salary to reduce or eliminate quarterly estimated tax payments. Review your process each January.

## Timing And Deadlines

Estimated tax payments for calendar-year taxpayers are due on the following dates for the 2026 tax year:

| Payment | Income Period Covered | Due Date |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | January 1 -- March 31, 2026 | April 15, 2026 |
| Q2 | April 1 -- May 31, 2026 | June 15, 2026 |
| Q3 | June 1 -- August 31, 2026 | September 15, 2026 |
| Q4 | September 1 -- December 31, 2026 | January 15, 2027 |

**Important notes on timing:**

- The Q4 payment due January 15, 2027 is optional if you file your complete 2026 Form 1040 and pay any remaining balance in full by February 1, 2027. Most taxpayers keep the January 15 payment to avoid a gap.
- If a due date falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or federal legal holiday, the payment is on time if received on the next business day.
- Payments must be received (not merely postmarked) by the due date for electronic payments. For mailed payments, the postmark date controls.
- The penalty is computed separately for each quarter. An overpayment in Q2 does not retroactively cure an underpayment in Q1.
- Underpayment penalty accrues from the due date of the missed installment through the earlier of: (a) April 15 of the following year, or (b) the date the underpayment is actually paid.
- IRS Direct Pay is available Monday through Saturday 12:00 a.m. to 11:45 p.m. ET, and Sunday 7:00 a.m. to 11:45 p.m. ET. Do not wait until the deadline evening to pay, as the system may be unavailable.

**Recurring calendar reminder:** After completing this playbook, enter all four due dates (plus a two-week-prior reminder for each) in your calendar for both the current year and the following year.

## The Numbers

All figures below are verified for tax year 2026 against the sources listed in the "Sources" section.

**Minimum threshold to owe estimated tax:**
$1,000. If your expected tax liability for 2026, after subtracting all withholding and refundable credits, is less than $1,000, no estimated tax payments are required and no underpayment penalty applies.
Source: IRS Form 1040-ES (2026); IRS FAQs on Estimated Tax (irs.gov/faqs/estimated-tax/individuals/individuals).

**Safe harbor -- Option A (Current-year test):**
Pay at least 90% of your 2026 actual tax liability through withholding and/or timely quarterly payments. You must meet this threshold by the time each quarterly payment is due, not just by April 15.
Source: IRS FAQs on Estimated Tax; Instructions for Form 2210 (2025), page 2.

**Safe harbor -- Option B (Prior-year test):**
Pay at least 100% of your 2025 tax liability (shown on your 2025 Form 1040, Line 24) if your 2025 AGI was $150,000 or less ($75,000 or less if your 2026 filing status is married filing separately). Pay at least 110% of your 2025 tax liability if your 2025 AGI exceeded $150,000 ($75,000 for married filing separately). The prior-year tax return must cover a full 12-month year.
Source: IRS FAQs on Estimated Tax; Instructions for Form 2210 (2025), page 2.

**AGI thresholds for the 110% prior-year safe harbor:**
- $150,000: threshold for single filers, married filing jointly, head of household, and qualifying surviving spouse.
- $75,000: threshold for married filing separately.
Source: IRS FAQs on Estimated Tax; Instructions for Form 2210 (2025).

**Underpayment penalty rate:**
The penalty rate equals the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, reset quarterly by the IRS.
- Q1 2026 (January through March): 7% annualized. (Rev. Rul. 2025-22, Internal Revenue Bulletin 2025-48.)
- Q2 2026 (April through June): 6% annualized. (Rev. Rul. 2026-5, Internal Revenue Bulletin 2026-08, February 17, 2026.)
- Q3 2026 (July through September): 7% annualized. (Rev. Rul. 2026-10, Internal Revenue Bulletin 2026-22, May 26, 2026.)
- Q4 2026 (October through December): [confirm with your advisor -- not yet published as of June 2026; check irs.gov/payments/quarterly-interest-rates in late August 2026 when the IRS typically announces Q4 rates].
Source: IRS, Underpayment of Estimated Tax by Individuals Penalty (irs.gov/payments/underpayment-of-estimated-tax-by-individuals-penalty); IRS Quarterly Interest Rates page (irs.gov/payments/quarterly-interest-rates); Rev. Rul. 2025-22 (IRB 2025-48); Rev. Rul. 2026-5 (IRB 2026-08); Rev. Rul. 2026-10 (IRB 2026-22).

**Self-employment tax rate:** 15.3% on net earnings from self-employment (net business income x 92.35%).
- Social Security portion: 12.4%, applied only up to the Social Security wage base.
- Medicare portion: 2.9%, applied to all net earnings with no upper limit.
Source: IRS, Self-Employment Tax (Social Security and Medicare Taxes), irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes; Topic No. 751 (updated January 20, 2026).

**Social Security wage base for 2026:** $184,500. Net SE earnings above this amount are still subject to the 2.9% Medicare SE tax but not the 12.4% Social Security SE tax.
Source: IRS Topic No. 751, Social Security and Medicare Withholding Rates (updated January 20, 2026).

**Deduction for half of self-employment tax:** You may deduct the employer-equivalent portion of your SE tax (half of the total SE tax you owe) as an above-the-line deduction on Form 1040. This deduction reduces AGI and therefore reduces your income tax base. Include this deduction in your estimated tax calculation.
Source: IRS, Self-Employment Tax page; IRC Section 164(f).

**Additional Medicare Tax:** 0.9% on wages, compensation, and net self-employment income exceeding:
- $200,000: single filers and heads of household
- $250,000: married filing jointly
- $125,000: married filing separately
Employers are only required to withhold this tax once wages exceed $200,000 in a calendar year from that single employer. If you have multiple income sources that together exceed the threshold (for example, a $180,000 salary plus $80,000 in SE income for a single filer), you must account for the 0.9% shortfall through estimated tax payments.
Source: IRS Publication 505 (2026); IRS Topic No. 751.

**Standard deductions for 2026:**
- Single / Married filing separately: $16,100
- Married filing jointly / Qualifying surviving spouse: $32,200
- Head of household: $24,150
Source: IRS Publication 505 (2026).

**QBID minimum for 2026:** Beginning in 2026, taxpayers with at least $1,000 in qualified business income from a qualified trade or business in which they materially participate (IRC Section 469(h)) may claim a minimum QBID of $400. Passive owners who do not materially participate do not qualify for the $400 minimum. Phase-in and phase-out rules under IRC Section 199A apply at higher income levels; work through your QBID calculation in your estimated tax projection to avoid over-projecting your tax.
Source: IRS Publication 505 (2026); One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), IRC Section 199A(i) as amended.

**Form 1040-ES:** 2026 edition is available at irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1040es.pdf. Use the Worksheet on page 8 of the form to calculate your required estimated tax.

**Form 2210:** Used to calculate underpayment penalties. 2025 instructions available at irs.gov/instructions/i2210; 2026 instructions will be released during the 2026 filing season. Part II of the form contains checkboxes, including Box C, which you check to elect the annualized income installment method. Schedule AI is a separate schedule (not Part II) that you complete to carry out that calculation; the results flow into Part III of the main form.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**S-corporation owner-employees:** The W-2 salary you receive from your S-corp is subject to income tax withholding. You can increase withholding on that salary by submitting a revised W-4 to your S-corp's payroll provider, which may eliminate the need for separate quarterly estimated payments. However, the profit distributions (sometimes called shareholder distributions) you receive from the S-corp are NOT subject to withholding. The income tax on those distributions must be covered by estimated payments if the total owed exceeds $1,000 after withholding. S-corp profits also do not incur SE tax directly (unlike sole proprietors), but the IRS requires that you take reasonable compensation (a W-2 salary) before taking distributions, precisely to ensure FICA taxes are collected on a meaningful portion of your income. The reasonable compensation requirement is a separate issue from estimated tax; discuss your salary level with your advisor.

**Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (disregarded entities):** 100% of your net business income flows to Schedule C and is subject to both income tax and SE tax. Your SE tax bill is often the largest unexpected component of your quarterly payment. Make sure your projections include the full 15.3% SE tax on your net earnings (after the 92.35% multiplier), minus the deduction for half of SE tax.

**Partners in a partnership and multi-member LLC members:** Partnership income flows through to your Schedule E without payroll tax withholding. Unlike S-corp wages, there is no paycheck from which to withhold. All income tax and SE tax (for general partners and active LLC members) must be paid through estimated tax.

**C-corporation shareholders (who are also employees):** A C-corp pays its own corporate income tax and makes its own estimated tax payments on Form 1120-W. As a C-corp shareholder receiving dividends, your personal estimated tax obligation applies to dividend income not covered by withholding. This playbook addresses individual (Form 1040) estimated tax only.

**State estimated tax:** Every state that imposes an income tax has its own estimated tax system with separate due dates, thresholds, and safe harbor rules that may differ from federal rules. California, for example, uses a 30%-40%-0%-30% installment schedule rather than equal quarters. New York has different income thresholds. Do not assume federal rules apply to your state payments. Confirm your state's requirements with your advisor and with the official state Department of Revenue (DOR) or Franchise Tax Board (FTB) website each year.

**New York and California residents:** These two states are especially likely to have meaningful estimated tax obligations that diverge from the federal schedule. If you live or operate a business in either state, treat state and federal estimated tax as two separate systems running in parallel.

**Current-year caveats for 2026:**
- The TCJA (Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017) provisions were set to expire after 2025. Legislation enacted in 2025 extended or modified several provisions affecting 2026 tax rates and thresholds. The figures in this playbook reflect provisions as understood at the time of writing. Confirm 2026 tax rates, brackets, and QBID rules with your advisor and verify any changes at irs.gov before finalizing your estimated payment calculations.
- The Social Security wage base adjusts annually for inflation. The 2026 figure is $184,500 per IRS Topic No. 751. Confirm this figure at the start of each year as it changes.
- Underpayment penalty interest rates reset quarterly. The Q3 2026 rate (7%) was published on May 26, 2026 (Rev. Rul. 2026-10, IRB 2026-22). The Q4 2026 rate is not yet published as of June 2026; check irs.gov/payments/quarterly-interest-rates in late August 2026.

**What estimated tax does NOT cover:**
Estimated tax payments satisfy your income tax and SE tax prepayment obligation. They do not cover payroll tax deposits (which S-corp and other employer entities make separately on a different schedule), sales tax, or other business taxes.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Ignoring SE tax in the projection.** Self-employed individuals and partners often project only their income tax and forget to add SE tax (15.3% on net earnings up to the wage base, 2.9% above it). This causes chronic underpayment. Always project SE tax separately and add it to your income tax projection before dividing by 4.

**2. Using gross income instead of net income.** The SE tax and income tax base is net income from self-employment (gross income minus ordinary and necessary business expenses). Overpaying early because you estimated on gross is wasteful; underpaying because you forgot your expenses is also common (though less harmful).

**3. Missing Q2's compressed window.** Q2 covers only two calendar months (April and May income) but still requires a full quarterly payment by June 15. Taxpayers who think of it as a "three-month quarter" are surprised to find the Q2 due date arrives quickly after Q1.

**4. Paying four equal amounts based on last year's income without checking whether the 90% test is also satisfied.** The prior-year safe harbor guarantees no penalty regardless of what you earn this year. The 90% current-year test is often lower for taxpayers whose income grew significantly from last year. Using only the prior-year number without checking the 90% option means you may over-pay estimated tax unnecessarily. Run both calculations and use the lower target.

**5. Not adjusting after a major income event.** Selling a business, closing a large real estate transaction, or receiving a settlement in Q3 can instantly create a large underpayment for prior quarters. The penalty for those quarters is not avoidable after the fact (unless you qualify for a waiver), but you can avoid further underpayment by adjusting Q4. Review your situation with your advisor any time a major income event occurs.

**6. Assuming a refund from prior years carries forward automatically.** If you received a refund from your 2025 return and asked the IRS to apply it to 2026 estimated tax, that credit is applied to Q1. Verify the credit appears in your IRS Online Account and confirm the amount. Do not rely on it if you did not explicitly elect to apply the refund to 2026.

**7. Missing the state payment.** Business owners often pay the federal estimated tax on time but forget state estimated taxes, generating a separate state underpayment penalty. Build state payments into the same calendar reminder system you use for federal.

**8. Underpaying early quarters because income was low, then over-correcting late.** The penalty applies quarter-by-quarter. Paying a much larger Q4 installment to "catch up" does not erase the Q1, Q2, or Q3 shortfalls. Match your payments to the schedule.

**9. Assuming the S-corp entity itself owes the estimated tax.** The S-corp is a pass-through entity and generally does not pay federal income tax at the entity level. The individual shareholder pays income tax on their share of S-corp income via their personal Form 1040. Estimated tax is a personal obligation, not a corporate one, for S-corp income.

## Advisor Review Gates

Consult your advisor before acting if any of the following apply:

1. **Your projected 2026 income differs from 2025 by more than 20% (higher or lower).** Large income swings make both the 90% current-year test and the 110% prior-year test potentially useful; only one will be optimal for your situation.

2. **You had a major one-time income event in 2026** (business sale, real estate closing, large capital gain, stock option exercise, settlement, inheritance). These events often push you into higher brackets, trigger the Additional Medicare Tax, and may affect the QBID phase-out. Your quarterly payments need immediate recalculation.

3. **You plan to use the annualized income installment method (Form 2210, Schedule AI)** to reduce early-quarter payments. This method requires careful calculation and should be reviewed before you rely on it to skip or reduce a payment.

4. **You operate in two or more states**, or recently moved. State residency rules for estimated tax can be complicated, and some states assess penalties differently than the federal system.

5. **You believe you qualify for a penalty waiver** (casualty, disaster, retirement after age 62, or disability). Waiver requests are filed with Form 2210 and require supporting documentation.

6. **You are an S-corp owner considering using increased W-4 withholding in place of quarterly estimated payments.** The math works only if your salary is large enough to cover the withholding needed; your advisor can verify the calculation before you change your withholding elections.

7. **Your 2025 return covered less than 12 months** (for example, you established a fiscal year entity, moved from a foreign jurisdiction, or filed a short-period return). The prior-year safe harbor requires a full 12-month prior return; without one, you can only rely on the 90% current-year test.

8. **You are subject to Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT).** AMT liability must be included in your estimated tax projections; if your situation could trigger AMT, have your advisor confirm whether the safe harbor still applies as calculated.

## Before You Act

Before you make any estimated tax payment, change any withholding election, or finalize any payment amount:

Confirm your specific income projections, filing status, prior-year liability figures, and state obligations with your advisor. The calculations in this playbook use general federal rules that apply to most taxpayers, but individual circumstances -- entity structure, state of residence, multiple income streams, AMT exposure, QBID applicability, and recent income changes -- can affect the correct amount. An error in your estimated tax calculation does not result in criminal liability, but it can result in an underpayment penalty or in unnecessary overpayment of tax. Your advisor can verify your calculation before you commit to a payment schedule.

If you are uncertain about any number, mark it with "confirm with advisor" on the worksheet rather than guessing. A conservative overpayment is always safer than an underpayment that triggers a penalty.
