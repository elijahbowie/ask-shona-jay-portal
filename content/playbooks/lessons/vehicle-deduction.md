# Vehicle Deduction

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

When you use a vehicle for legitimate business purposes, the tax code allows your business to deduct the related costs -- either by tracking actual expenses and depreciation, or by multiplying business miles driven by the IRS standard rate. For 2026, the business standard mileage rate is 72.5 cents per mile (IRS Notice 2026-10). Heavier vehicles (over 6,000 pounds gross vehicle weight) can produce first-year deductions equal to their full purchase price when 100 percent bonus depreciation applies. Proper setup -- choosing the right method, logging every trip, and meeting the business-use percentage tests -- is what converts a vehicle you already need into a meaningful tax deduction.

## Who This Applies To

- **Self-employed business owners (sole proprietors, single-member LLC owners)** who drive for client visits, job sites, supply runs, or other business activities and report income on Schedule C (Form 1040).
- **S-corporation and C-corporation owners** who want the business -- not themselves personally -- to own or reimburse the cost of a vehicle used for business.
- **Partnership and multi-member LLC owners** using a vehicle for partnership business.
- **Business owners purchasing a new or used vehicle** in 2026 and weighing whether to deduct it immediately (Section 179 or bonus depreciation) or recover the cost over several years (MACRS depreciation).
- **Owners who already own a vehicle** used partly for business and want to optimize the annual deduction through the standard mileage or actual expense method.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Commuting miles are never deductible.** Travel from your home to your regular, principal place of business is a personal expense under IRC Section 262, regardless of how far you drive. If the only business driving you do is commuting, there is no vehicle deduction available.
- **Vehicles used less than 50 percent for business** fall under "listed property" (defined in Key Terms). Section 179 and bonus depreciation are both unavailable, and depreciation must use the slower Alternative Depreciation System (ADS) straight-line method. The economic value of the deduction drops substantially.
- **Employees (W-2 workers) who are not owner-operators** cannot deduct unreimbursed vehicle expenses on their own tax return under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) rules in effect through 2025. For 2026, the TCJA itemized-deduction suspension for unreimbursed employee business expenses remains in effect [confirm with your advisor -- the TCJA sunset and OBBBA interactions should be reviewed for your specific filing year]. Employees should instead ask their employer to establish an accountable plan reimbursement arrangement.
- **Businesses in a net loss position** may find that Section 179 accelerated deductions (which are capped by business taxable income) cannot be fully used in the current year and will carry forward. Bonus depreciation, however, can create or deepen a net operating loss (NOL) and may still be strategically useful.
- **Vehicles owned personally and never titled to the business** can still generate a deduction via the standard mileage method for self-employed individuals, but are generally not eligible for Section 179 or bonus depreciation unless placed in service as a business asset.
- **Luxury passenger cars (GVWR 6,000 lbs or less)** are subject to hard annual depreciation caps under IRC Section 280F, limiting the first-year deduction regardless of cost (see The Numbers section).

## Key Terms

**Gross Vehicle Weight Rating (GVWR):** The maximum total weight of a vehicle as determined by the manufacturer, including the vehicle itself plus passengers and cargo. This number is printed on a label inside the driver's side door jamb. GVWR determines whether a vehicle is treated as a "passenger automobile" (6,000 lbs or less, subject to stricter limits) or a heavier vehicle (over 6,000 lbs, subject to higher or no annual caps).

**Standard mileage rate:** A per-mile amount set each year by the IRS that you can multiply by your business miles to compute your vehicle deduction, instead of tracking and adding up every actual cost. The 2026 rate is 72.5 cents per mile (IRS Notice 2026-10). This rate covers depreciation, gas, oil, insurance, and most other operating costs. Parking fees and tolls related to business travel are deductible separately on top of the standard rate.

**Actual expense method:** Instead of using the standard rate, you track every real cost (gas, oil, tires, repairs, insurance, registration, lease payments, and depreciation) and multiply the total by your business-use percentage. This method often yields a larger deduction for heavier or more expensive vehicles, but requires more detailed recordkeeping.

**Business-use percentage:** The fraction of total miles driven in a year that were for business. If you drove 20,000 total miles and 14,000 were for business, your business-use percentage is 70 percent. Only this fraction of actual expenses is deductible. Business-use percentage must be supported by a contemporaneous mileage log.

**Listed property:** A category of assets the IRS scrutinizes because of their personal-use potential. Passenger automobiles (GVWR 6,000 lbs or less), certain aircraft, and similar assets are "listed property" under IRC Section 280F. If listed property is used 50 percent or less for qualified business use, Section 179 and bonus depreciation are unavailable, and depreciation must use the ADS (Alternative Depreciation System) straight-line method over a longer recovery period.

**50-percent business-use test:** The minimum business-use threshold for listed property to qualify for Section 179 and bonus depreciation. If business use exceeds 50 percent, full accelerated deductions are available (subject to the annual dollar caps described in The Numbers). If business use is 50 percent or below, the deduction is restricted to ADS straight-line only.

**MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System):** The standard U.S. depreciation system for assets placed in service after 1986, governed by IRC Section 168. MACRS assigns each asset a recovery period (number of years) and uses an accelerated method that produces larger deductions in early years. Passenger automobiles have a 5-year MACRS recovery period.

**Section 179 deduction:** A provision of IRC Section 179 letting a business elect to expense (deduct in full) the purchase cost of qualifying property in the year it is placed in service, up to an annual dollar limit. For 2026, the overall Section 179 limit is $2,560,000. For SUVs (defined below), a separate lower cap applies. Section 179 cannot exceed the business's taxable income from active trade or business operations for the year; any excess carries forward.

**SUV cap (Section 179):** A special limit on the Section 179 deduction for "sport utility vehicles" -- vehicles with a GVWR over 6,000 lbs that are primarily designed to carry passengers and have seating for fewer than nine passengers behind the driver. For 2026, the maximum Section 179 deduction for an SUV is $32,000 (IRC Section 179(b)(5), inflation-adjusted via Rev. Proc. 2026-15 / RP-25-16 as announced by the IRS). Full bonus depreciation may still be applied to the remaining basis after the Section 179 amount.

**Bonus depreciation (additional first-year depreciation):** Under IRC Section 168(k), an extra first-year deduction on top of regular MACRS. As of 2026, the rate is 100 percent for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025, a permanent change made by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025). Unlike Section 179, bonus depreciation is not limited to business taxable income and can create a net operating loss (NOL). For passenger autos under 6,000 lbs, the Section 280F cap applies and limits the first-year amount regardless of bonus depreciation.

**One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA):** Federal legislation (Public Law 119-21) signed July 4, 2025, that among other things permanently restored 100 percent bonus depreciation under IRC Section 168(k) for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025. IRS interim guidance is provided in Notice 2026-11.

**Section 280F limits (luxury auto caps):** Hard annual dollar caps on depreciation deductions for passenger automobiles (GVWR 6,000 lbs or less), set by the IRS each year and adjusted for inflation. These limits apply regardless of the vehicle's actual cost or the depreciation method used. For 2026, see the detailed figures in The Numbers section. Vehicles with GVWR over 6,000 lbs are not subject to Section 280F caps.

**Placed in service:** The date a vehicle is ready and available for use in your business. Depreciation begins on this date, and the tax year in which it falls determines which year's rules (mileage rate, caps, bonus depreciation percentage) apply.

**Accountable plan:** An employer reimbursement arrangement that meets three IRS requirements: (1) expenses have a legitimate business connection, (2) the employee provides adequate documentation (receipts, mileage log) within a reasonable time, and (3) any excess reimbursement is returned to the employer. Reimbursements made under a qualifying accountable plan are not included in the employee's taxable wages. Governed by Treasury Regulation Section 1.62-2.

**Recapture:** If you claim accelerated depreciation (Section 179 or bonus) and later drop the vehicle's business-use percentage below 50 percent, or sell the vehicle before the end of its MACRS recovery period, the IRS may require you to include some of the previously deducted amount back in taxable income in the year of the change. This is called depreciation recapture.

**Contemporaneous mileage log:** A mileage record created at or near the time of each trip (not reconstructed weeks or months later). IRS regulations under Section 274 require that business vehicle expenses be substantiated by adequate records, including date, destination, business purpose, and miles for each trip. A log created from memory at year-end is far more vulnerable in an audit than daily or trip-by-trip records.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The federal tax form used by sole proprietors and single-member LLC owners to report business profit or loss. Vehicle expenses are entered on Line 9 (Car and truck expenses).

**Form 4562 (Depreciation and Amortization):** The IRS form used to claim depreciation deductions, Section 179 elections, and bonus depreciation. Required for any vehicle on which you claim depreciation (not required if using only the standard mileage method for all vehicles). Part V covers listed property, including the vehicle business-use percentage and substantiation questions.

**ADS (Alternative Depreciation System):** A slower depreciation method under MACRS that uses straight-line depreciation over a generally longer recovery period. Required for listed property used 50 percent or less for business. Taxpayers may also elect ADS voluntarily.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before beginning implementation:

1. **Vehicle identification:** Year, make, model, and VIN of each vehicle you intend to deduct.
2. **GVWR:** The gross vehicle weight rating from the manufacturer's label inside the driver's side door (needed to determine whether the Section 280F caps or the heavier-vehicle rules apply).
3. **Date placed in service:** The date the vehicle was first available and used for business in 2026 (or the prior year if already in service).
4. **Purchase price or basis:** The cost you paid for the vehicle (or its adjusted tax basis if you converted a previously personal vehicle to business use).
5. **Financing:** Whether the vehicle is purchased outright, financed, or leased (lease payments are deducted differently than purchase depreciation).
6. **Total miles driven for the year:** Track all miles driven (business and personal) from January 1 through December 31 (or from placed-in-service date if acquired mid-year).
7. **Business miles:** The number of those total miles driven for legitimate business purposes (excluding commuting).
8. **Actual expenses (if using actual expense method):** Receipts or statements for gas, oil, tires, repairs, insurance, registration fees, and lease or loan interest.
9. **Entity structure:** Whether the vehicle is owned personally, by an S-corporation, C-corporation, partnership, or sole proprietorship (this affects which forms to use and whether a reimbursement plan is needed).
10. **Prior-year method election:** If this vehicle was in service in a prior year, whether you previously elected the standard mileage rate (affects 2026 choices).

## Required Documents And Records

**To establish and claim the deduction:**

- Contemporaneous mileage log for each vehicle, containing for every business trip: date, destination (city or name of business), business purpose (what you did or who you met), and miles driven.
- Odometer reading at the start and end of each year (or placed-in-service date) to establish total miles.
- Purchase documentation: bill of sale, dealer invoice, or financing agreement showing vehicle cost and date of purchase.
- Title or registration showing the vehicle owner (individual or business entity).
- If using actual expenses: receipts for all operating costs (fuel, oil, repairs, tires, insurance, registration).

**For depreciation (actual expense method):**

- Form 4562 completed by your preparer or yourself, showing the Section 179 election, bonus depreciation, and MACRS computation.
- GVWR documentation (photo of door label or manufacturer spec sheet).

**For S-corporations and C-corporations using a vehicle reimbursement arrangement:**

- Written accountable plan policy (a one- to two-page document establishing the plan before expenses are incurred).
- Monthly or per-trip reimbursement requests submitted by the owner-employee to the corporation, each attached to the mileage log entries for that period.
- Corporate check or ACH record showing the reimbursement payment.

**Record retention guidance:**

Keep all vehicle records for at least **3 years** after the due date of the return on which the deduction is claimed. If you claim Section 179 or bonus depreciation, keep records for at least **3 years after the year you dispose of the vehicle** (in case of recapture). The IRS generally has 3 years to audit a return, but 6 years if income is understated by more than 25 percent, so retaining records for 6 years provides a comfortable margin. Keep purchase documents and depreciation schedules for the life of the vehicle plus 6 years.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Identify And Classify The Vehicle

**Step 1. Confirm the vehicle is used for a qualified business purpose.**
Only miles and expenses connected to your trade or business are deductible. Commuting (home to your regular workplace) is never deductible. Client visits, site inspections, picking up supplies, driving between business locations, and travel to temporary work sites all qualify. Document the business purpose for each trip.

**Step 2. Check the GVWR.**
Look at the manufacturer's label inside the driver's side door jamb. Record the GVWR.
- GVWR of 6,000 lbs or less: the vehicle is a "passenger automobile" subject to Section 280F annual caps.
- GVWR over 6,000 lbs and up to 14,000 lbs: the vehicle is a heavy SUV, truck, or van. SUVs in this range are subject to the $32,000 Section 179 cap but not the Section 280F annual depreciation caps. Pickup trucks with a cargo bed of at least 6 feet in interior length (not readily accessible from the passenger compartment) are not subject to the SUV cap and may qualify for full Section 179 up to the overall $2,560,000 limit.

**Step 3. Determine business-use percentage.**
Divide total annual business miles by total annual miles driven. If the result is 50 percent or below, stop and consult your advisor -- standard depreciation rules are restricted and the deduction may be minimal.

### Phase 2: Choose Your Deduction Method

**Step 4. Decide between the standard mileage rate and the actual expense method.**

The choice must be made in the first year the vehicle is placed in service for business. Key rules:
- If you own the vehicle and want the option to switch methods in future years, you must elect the standard mileage rate in the first year. Once you use actual expenses and MACRS depreciation in year 1, you cannot switch to the standard mileage rate in a later year.
- If you lease the vehicle, you must use the standard mileage rate for the entire lease period (including renewals) if you elect that method.
- If you operate five or more vehicles simultaneously in your business, you cannot use the standard mileage rate and must use actual expenses.

Run a quick comparison (your advisor or the Vehicle Quick Reference Guide download can help):
- Standard mileage estimate: business miles x 72.5 cents
- Actual expense estimate: (business-use % x total operating costs) + depreciation

**Step 5. (If using actual expenses) Select the depreciation approach.**

For the actual expense method, choose one of the following:
- **Section 179:** Deduct up to the full purchase price in year 1, subject to the caps in The Numbers. Requires a taxable income from active business operations to absorb the deduction. Enter the election on Form 4562, Part I.
- **Bonus depreciation (100% in 2026):** For qualifying heavy vehicles (GVWR over 6,000 lbs) acquired after January 19, 2025, deduct 100 percent of the remaining basis in year 1 (after any Section 179 amount). Can create an NOL. Enter on Form 4562.
- **MACRS with no Section 179 or bonus:** Spread the deduction over 5 years using the standard MACRS percentages, subject to the Section 280F caps for passenger autos.

For most business owners buying a qualifying heavy vehicle, the typical approach is to elect Section 179 up to the allowable cap, then apply 100 percent bonus depreciation to any remaining basis.

### Phase 3: Build Your Mileage Log

**Step 6. Set up a mileage tracking system -- starting today.**
The log must be contemporaneous. Options:
- A mileage tracking app (MileIQ, Everlance, TripLog, or similar) that uses GPS to log trips automatically.
- A paper mileage log kept in the vehicle's glove box.
- A calendar or day planner with trip entries made at the time of travel.

For each trip, record:
- Date
- Starting location and destination (city name or business name)
- Business purpose (for example: "client meeting with ABC Company" or "supply run for inventory")
- Miles driven

**Step 7. Record odometer readings.**
Write down your odometer reading on January 1 (or the placed-in-service date if mid-year) and on December 31. The IRS expects total miles to be accounted for between business miles, personal miles, and commuting miles.

### Phase 4: Set Up The Entity Structure (S-Corp and C-Corp Owners)

**Step 8. Decide whether the vehicle will be owned by the business or by you personally.**

Option A -- Business owns the vehicle: The corporation or partnership purchases, titles, and insures the vehicle. All business operating costs and depreciation flow through the business return (Form 1120-S, 1120, or 1065).

Option B -- You own the vehicle personally and get reimbursed: The business establishes an accountable plan (Step 9), you submit mileage logs and expense reports, and the business pays you a tax-free reimbursement. This is often simpler and avoids payroll tax complexities.

**Step 9. If using an accountable plan: establish it in writing before expenses are incurred.**
The plan document should state that:
- Reimbursements are for legitimate business expenses.
- Employees (including owner-employees) must submit documentation (date, destination, business purpose, miles) within 60 days of each expense.
- Any advance in excess of actual expenses must be returned within 120 days.

Your advisor can provide or review an accountable plan template. Without a written plan, reimbursements may be treated as taxable W-2 wages.

**Step 10. Submit reimbursement requests monthly.**
Attach the mileage log for the period to a simple expense report. The corporation writes a check or initiates an ACH payment to you. File the documentation with the corporate records.

### Phase 5: Report On The Tax Return

**Step 11. Report the vehicle deduction on the correct form.**

- **Sole proprietors / single-member LLCs:** Line 9 (Car and truck expenses) on Schedule C. If using the actual expense method or claiming depreciation, also complete Form 4562.
- **S-corporations (Form 1120-S):** Deduct vehicle expenses on the corporate return. If the vehicle is personally owned and reimbursed via an accountable plan, no additional reporting is needed on the shareholder's individual return for the reimbursed amount.
- **Partnerships (Form 1065):** Report on the partnership return; expenses flow to partners on Schedule K-1.

**Step 12. Complete Form 4562 if claiming depreciation.**
Your tax preparer will complete Form 4562. Key sections:
- Part I: Section 179 election (enter the vehicle cost and the elected amount, subject to the annual limit and taxable income cap).
- Part V: Listed property. For every vehicle, answer the substantiation questions (Was the vehicle used for commuting? Do you have evidence to support the business-use percentage claimed?). Enter the business-use percentage.
- Part VI: For vehicles using the standard mileage rate, the form still asks for total miles and business miles.

**Step 13. Retain all documentation with your tax file.**
Keep the mileage log, odometer records, purchase documents, and any accountable plan records together in a folder labeled with the tax year. Do not discard these after filing -- retain them for the periods described in Required Documents And Records above.

## Timing And Deadlines

| Event | Deadline or Timing |
|---|---|
| Vehicle placed in service | Must occur during the tax year for which you claim the deduction (January 1 through December 31, 2026 for the 2026 tax year). |
| Method election (standard mileage vs. actual expenses) | Must be made on the original timely filed return (or extended return) for the first year the vehicle is used for business. |
| Section 179 election | Made on Form 4562 filed with the tax return (original or extended). The election can be revoked only with IRS consent. |
| Bonus depreciation election-out | If you want to opt out of bonus depreciation (to spread deductions over multiple years), the election not to apply bonus depreciation must be made on the timely filed return, including extensions. |
| Business tax return due date | Sole proprietors: April 15, 2027 (or October 15, 2027 with extension). S-corps and partnerships: March 15, 2027 (or September 15, 2027 with extension). C-corps (calendar year): April 15, 2027 (or October 15, 2027 with extension). |
| Accountable plan documentation | Mileage logs and expense reports should be submitted to the business within 60 days of the expense. Excess reimbursements must be returned within 120 days (or within 120 days of a quarterly statement). |
| Record retention | See Required Documents And Records. At minimum, 3 years from return due date; 6 years for larger deductions. |

**Recurring annual rhythm:**
- Monthly: Submit mileage log and reimbursement request to business (if using accountable plan).
- Quarterly: Review year-to-date mileage and estimated deduction; confirm you are still above 50 percent business use.
- At year-end (December 31): Record final odometer reading. Download or print mileage log for the year. Gather actual expense totals.
- January to March: Provide mileage summary and expense totals to your tax preparer.

**Important clarifications:**

The Section 280F caps (Year 1 through Year 4+) apply only to passenger automobiles with GVWR of 6,000 lbs or less. Vehicles over 6,000 lbs GVWR are NOT subject to these caps, which is why a qualifying heavy SUV or pickup truck used 100 percent for business can potentially be deducted in full in year 1.

The $32,000 SUV cap under Section 179 is separate from the Section 280F caps. It applies to heavy SUVs (GVWR over 6,000 lbs, designed for passenger use, fewer than 9 seats behind the driver). The remaining cost after the $32,000 Section 179 deduction can still qualify for 100 percent bonus depreciation with no additional cap.

Trucks and vans (including pickup trucks meeting the 6-foot cargo bed test) may be fully deductible in year 1 under Section 179 (up to the $2,560,000 overall limit) plus bonus depreciation on any remainder, with no separate vehicle cap.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure:**

- **Sole proprietors / single-member LLCs (Schedule C filers):** The vehicle deduction reduces self-employment income, which in turn reduces self-employment tax (the 15.3 percent tax on net self-employment income). This is a meaningful secondary benefit beyond just income tax savings.
- **S-corporation owners:** S-corp owners taking salary must decide whether the business or the individual will own the vehicle. If the S-corp owns it, the deduction flows through the corporate return and reduces the shareholder's basis. If personally owned and reimbursed via accountable plan, the reimbursement is a deductible business expense for the S-corp and tax-free to the shareholder. Using the vehicle for personal purposes in an S-corp context without proper documentation can create a taxable fringe benefit. Consult your advisor before placing a personal vehicle in an S-corp.
- **C-corporations:** C-corps can own vehicles and deduct all associated costs. Personal use of a company vehicle by a shareholder-employee can be a taxable fringe benefit that must be included in W-2 wages unless an exception applies (for example, the working condition fringe benefit rule). The vehicle must be available and used primarily for business.
- **Partnerships and multi-member LLCs:** Vehicle expenses paid by the partnership reduce partnership taxable income allocated to partners. Partners using personally owned vehicles for partnership business may deduct the expenses on their individual returns (Schedule E, unreimbursed partnership expenses), or the partnership can establish an accountable plan to reimburse them.

**Payroll caveats:**

S-corporation and C-corporation owners who use a business vehicle for any personal purpose must either include the value of that personal use in their W-2 wages or be reimbursed by the business only for the business-use portion. The IRS has specific valuation methods (cents-per-mile, commuting, fleet-average) for computing the taxable fringe benefit value. Failure to account for personal use can result in wage underreporting and associated payroll tax issues.

**State tax caveats:**

State conformity to federal depreciation rules varies. Many states have their own Section 179 limits and do not automatically conform to bonus depreciation. For example, California does not conform to the federal bonus depreciation rules and limits the Section 179 deduction to lower amounts. Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor before counting on a first-year state deduction equal to the federal amount. New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and several other states also decouple from federal bonus depreciation.

**OBBBA and current-year caveats:**

The 100 percent bonus depreciation rate under the OBBBA applies to property acquired after January 19, 2025. Property acquired under a binding written contract entered into before that date may remain subject to the prior phasedown schedule. For vehicles ordered or under contract before January 20, 2025, confirm the acquisition date rules with your advisor before claiming 100 percent bonus depreciation.

**Recapture risk:**

If you claim Section 179 or bonus depreciation in 2026 and your business-use percentage drops below 50 percent in any subsequent year during the MACRS recovery period (5 years for vehicles), you will be required to include a recapture amount in gross income in that year. Track business-use percentage annually. If you sell the vehicle, report any gain attributable to depreciation as ordinary income (Section 1245 recapture), not capital gain.

**Lease vs. purchase:**

If you lease a vehicle instead of buying it, you cannot claim Section 179 or bonus depreciation (those apply only to purchases). Under the actual expense method, lease payments are deductible proportional to business use, but the IRS requires you to add back a "lease inclusion amount" (an income add-back from IRS tables) to prevent an unfair advantage over buyers of luxury vehicles. The standard mileage rate is generally simpler for leased vehicles and avoids the inclusion amount issue.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Not logging trips contemporaneously.** The most common audit failure for vehicle deductions is a mileage log reconstructed at year-end from memory or bank statements. Create the log in real time -- apps make this automatic.

2. **Including commuting miles as business miles.** Travel from home to your primary office or workspace is personal and never deductible. Many business owners, especially those who work from home, miscount commute-like trips as business travel. The home-office exception (regular work location is your home office) is a recognized exception but must itself be substantiated.

3. **Claiming 100 percent business use when the vehicle has personal use.** If you drive the vehicle on weekends, for personal errands, or for family use at all, business use is less than 100 percent. Overstating business-use percentage is an audit trigger and can result in penalties.

4. **Choosing the actual expense method in year 1 without confirming it produces a larger deduction.** Once you use actual expenses and MACRS depreciation, you cannot switch to the standard mileage rate in a later year. Run the comparison first.

5. **Failing to establish an accountable plan in writing before expenses occur.** An oral agreement or practice is not an accountable plan. If the written plan does not exist before the reimbursements are made, the IRS will treat the reimbursements as taxable wages.

6. **Assuming a heavy vehicle (GVWR over 6,000 lbs) has no limits.** Heavy SUVs still have a $32,000 Section 179 cap (bonus depreciation covers the rest, but Section 179 alone is capped). Pickup trucks with 6-foot beds avoid even that cap. Know your vehicle category.

7. **Missing the first-year method election.** The standard mileage rate election must be made on the original or timely extended return for the first year. You cannot go back and change from actual expenses to standard mileage after the fact.

8. **Not accounting for state conformity.** A federal first-year deduction of the full vehicle cost may translate to a much smaller or zero state deduction in non-conforming states. Budget accordingly for estimated state taxes.

9. **Neglecting recapture planning.** Business owners who deduct a vehicle in full in year 1 and then sell the vehicle or reduce business use below 50 percent in later years will owe ordinary income tax on the recaptured depreciation. Factor this into the decision to accelerate.

10. **Forgetting parking fees and tolls.** These are separately deductible business expenses on top of the standard mileage rate (or on top of actual expenses). Do not omit them.

## Advisor Review Gates

Pause and review with your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

1. **You are placing a vehicle in service for the first time and choosing between the standard mileage rate and the actual expense method.** The choice is effectively permanent for that vehicle when owned (not leased). Run the numbers with your advisor before filing.

2. **You are considering purchasing a vehicle over 6,000 lbs GVWR specifically for the tax deduction.** Confirm that you have the business-use documentation to support greater than 50 percent business use, and that the vehicle is genuinely needed for business. Vehicles purchased primarily for the deduction with minimal business use can be disallowed and can trigger fraud-related penalties.

3. **Your S-corporation or C-corporation will own a vehicle that you also use personally.** Personal use creates taxable fringe benefits that must be reported on your W-2. The valuation and payroll implications require coordination with your advisor before the vehicle is placed in service.

4. **Your business-use percentage may be close to or below 50 percent.** This threshold determines whether you qualify for Section 179 and bonus depreciation at all. If you are near the line, document carefully and confirm with your advisor before claiming accelerated deductions.

5. **You operate in a state that does not conform to federal bonus depreciation or Section 179 rules (for example, California, New York, New Jersey).** Confirm the state-level deduction and plan for any state tax due that is not offset by the federal deduction.

6. **The vehicle was acquired under a contract signed before January 20, 2025.** The 100 percent OBBBA bonus depreciation rate may not apply; confirm the acquisition date rules with your advisor.

7. **You plan to sell, trade in, or substantially reduce business use of a vehicle on which you claimed Section 179 or bonus depreciation within 5 years of placing it in service.** Recapture rules will apply; model the tax cost before transacting.

8. **The vehicle is a leased vehicle and you are unsure whether to use the standard mileage rate or actual expense method, or how the lease inclusion amount affects your deduction.** Confirm with your advisor.
