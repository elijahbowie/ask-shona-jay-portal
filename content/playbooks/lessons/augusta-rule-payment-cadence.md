# Augusta Rule Payment Cadence

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The Augusta Rule (named after Augusta, Georgia, home of the Masters golf tournament) is a provision in the federal tax code -- IRC Section 280A(g) -- that lets a homeowner rent their personal residence to a separate business entity for up to 14 days per year. The rental income the homeowner receives is completely excluded from federal gross income, meaning the homeowner pays zero federal income tax on it. At the same time, the business entity that pays the rent can deduct that payment as an ordinary and necessary business expense. The result is a legal income-shifting arrangement: money leaves the business as a deductible expense and arrives in the owner's personal account as tax-free income.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is designed for business owners who meet all three of the following conditions:

1. **You own or co-own your primary residence.** The property must qualify as your home (a dwelling unit you use as a residence during the year), not purely a rental or investment property.

2. **Your business operates through a separate legal entity.** The renter (your business) and the lessor (you, the homeowner) must be two distinct taxpayers in the eyes of the IRS. Qualifying entities include:
   - S corporations (most common setup for this strategy)
   - C corporations
   - Partnerships (multi-member LLCs taxed as a partnership)
   - Multi-member LLCs taxed as a partnership

3. **Your home can host legitimate, documented business meetings.** The rental days must coincide with real business activity: board meetings, strategy retreats, shareholder meetings, planning sessions, or other events with a genuine business purpose.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not use this strategy if any of the following apply:

- **You are a sole proprietor or single-member LLC without a corporate or partnership election.** A sole proprietor and their Schedule C business are the same taxpayer. There is no separate entity to pay rent, so there is no deductible expense and no arm's-length transaction. The strategy fails at the root.

- **You cannot document real business meetings.** The IRS and Tax Court require evidence that the meetings actually happened: agendas, minutes, attendee lists, and calendar records. The strategy is not a mechanism for routing distributions through inflated rent. The Tax Court's 2023 decision in *Sinopoli v. Commissioner*, TC Memo 2023-105, disallowed most of the claimed rent deductions when taxpayers could not produce contemporaneous documentation and had charged above-market rates.

- **You plan to rent for 15 or more days in the same tax year.** The moment the rental crosses 14 days, the entire Section 280A(g) exclusion disappears. The rental income becomes fully taxable for the year, and different -- more complex -- expense-allocation rules under Section 280A(c) and (e) apply instead.

- **Your state does not conform to IRC Section 280A(g).** The federal exclusion covers only federal income tax. Some states follow the federal treatment automatically; others do not. California in particular requires careful review because it has decoupled from certain federal provisions at various times. See the Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats section and confirm your state's treatment with your advisor before acting.

- **You cannot establish a fair market rental rate for your home as a meeting venue.** The rate you charge must be comparable to what a third party would pay for a similar venue in your market. Rates unsupported by market comparables will be reduced or disallowed by the IRS.

## Key Terms

**IRC Section 280A(g):** The provision of the Internal Revenue Code that creates the Augusta Rule. It states that if a dwelling unit is used by the taxpayer as a residence and is rented for fewer than 15 days during the tax year, the rental income is excluded from the taxpayer's gross income and no rental-use deductions are allowed.

**Gross income:** The total income a taxpayer must report to the IRS before any deductions. Income excluded from gross income does not appear on the tax return at all and is not subject to federal income tax.

**Dwelling unit used as a residence:** Under Section 280A(d), a property qualifies as your residence if you use it for personal purposes for more than 14 days during the year (or more than 10 percent of the days it is rented at fair rental, if greater). Most primary homes qualify easily.

**Separate taxpayer:** The IRS treats individual taxpayers and certain business entities as legally distinct. An S corporation, C corporation, partnership, or multi-member LLC is a separate taxpayer from its owners. A sole proprietorship and its owner are the same taxpayer.

**S corporation (S-corp):** A small business corporation that has elected to pass its income, deductions, and credits through to its shareholders for federal tax purposes. The business itself generally does not pay federal income tax; instead, income flows to the shareholders' personal returns via Schedule K-1. It files Form 1120-S annually.

**IRC Section 162:** The provision requiring that a business expense be "ordinary and necessary" to be deductible. Even when Section 280A(g) excludes the homeowner's income, the business entity's rent deduction must still satisfy Section 162. That means the amount must be reasonable (consistent with fair market value for comparable space) and the expense must genuinely serve the business.

**Fair market value (FMV):** The price that a willing buyer and a willing seller would agree upon in an arm's-length transaction, with neither party under compulsion and both having reasonable knowledge of the relevant facts. For the Augusta Rule, FMV means the rate a comparable commercial venue (hotel conference room, executive meeting space, event facility) would charge in your local market for a similar event.

**Form 1120-S:** The annual income tax return filed by an S corporation. Rent paid to a shareholder-homeowner is deducted on Line 11 (Rents) of Form 1120-S, reducing the corporation's ordinary business income.

**Schedule K-1 (Form 1120-S):** The form each S-corp shareholder receives showing their share of the corporation's income, deductions, and credits. After the rent deduction lowers business income, that reduction flows through to shareholders' K-1s.

**Schedule E (Form 1040):** The personal tax schedule used to report supplemental income and losses, including income from rental properties and S corporations. For Augusta Rule rentals, neither the rental income nor any rental-related deductions appear on Schedule E -- the income is simply excluded from the return.

**Form 1099-MISC:** An information return businesses use to report certain payments. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025), the reporting threshold for Form 1099-MISC increased from $600 to $2,000 for payments made in calendar year 2026 and later. If total rent payments to you exceed $2,000 in 2026, the business should issue you a Form 1099-MISC (Box 1, Rents). You still exclude the income on your personal return, but the 1099 creates an IRS matching record, so your exclusion should be properly noted.

**Contemporaneous documentation:** Records created at or very close to the time of the event, not reconstructed afterward. Courts and the IRS treat records created or dated retroactively as unreliable. All meeting records must be made in real time.

**Arm's-length transaction:** A transaction between unrelated, independent parties acting in their own self-interest. Because a shareholder renting to their own S-corp is a related-party transaction, the IRS scrutinizes it more closely than an arm's-length deal. Clean documentation and market-rate pricing are essential.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before starting the implementation process:

1. **Home address and property description:** Square footage of the space you will use for meetings, number of rooms, amenities (AV equipment, parking, catering capability, outdoor space).

2. **Business entity name, EIN, and entity type:** Confirm the business is a qualifying separate entity (S-corp, C-corp, partnership, or multi-member LLC). Confirm the entity's tax year (most use the calendar year).

3. **Planned meeting schedule for the year:** Dates, estimated attendees, and general business purpose for each planned rental day. You need this to track the 14-day ceiling and to draft the rental agreement.

4. **Local market comparable rates:** Three written quotes (email or printed confirmation) from comparable commercial venues in your area: hotel conference rooms, executive suites, private event facilities, or coworking event spaces. Collect quotes for the same capacity and date range as your planned meetings.

5. **Business bank account information:** The rent must be paid from the business account to your personal account. Verify both accounts are active and accessible.

6. **Prior year's return (if applicable):** To confirm the 14-day count has not already started if you used this strategy in a prior year for a different period (each tax year resets).

7. **State of residence and state(s) where the business files:** Needed to confirm whether your state(s) conform to the federal Section 280A(g) exclusion.

## Required Documents And Records

**Before the first rental day:**

- **Written Rental Agreement.** A signed document between you (the homeowner, as lessor) and the business entity (as lessee). The agreement must specify: the property address, the dates and hours of each rental period, the business purpose of each rental, the daily or per-event rental rate, and the payment terms. The agreement must be signed before the first rental day, not after.

- **Fair Market Value Analysis Memo.** A one-to-two-page memo documenting how you determined the rental rate. Attach the three comparable venue quotes, note the venues' names and dates of inquiry, and state the rate you selected and why it is at or below the median comparable. Save this with the rental agreement.

**During and immediately after each rental day:**

- **Meeting Agenda (distributed in advance).** A written agenda identifying the date, location, start/end times, attendees, and the business topics to be covered.

- **Meeting Minutes or Notes.** A record of what was discussed and decided. Does not need to be a formal legal document, but must be specific enough to show that real business decisions were made.

- **Attendee List.** Names (and ideally signatures or initials) of everyone present. If the meeting is a board or shareholder meeting, record titles.

- **Photographs (recommended).** Photos of the meeting in progress, including people and setup, provide strong corroborating evidence.

- **Invoice from Homeowner to Business.** A written invoice from you personally to the business entity, itemizing the date, property address, number of hours or days, and the agreed rate. Include a reference to the rental agreement.

- **Proof of Payment.** Bank statement or cleared check showing the business account debited and your personal account credited on or close to the meeting date. Payments must be traceable. Do not use cash.

**Day-count log:** A running tracker for the tax year showing each rental date used, the meeting purpose, and the cumulative total. Stop at 14 days. Keep this log with the meeting files.

**Retention period:** Keep all Augusta Rule records for at least 7 years from the date the related tax return is filed. The standard IRS audit window is 3 years, but it extends to 6 years if the IRS claims more than 25 percent of income was omitted. The 7-year rule gives you a conservative buffer. IRS Topic 305 confirms the general 3-year period; retain longer for related-party transactions given heightened scrutiny risk.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

**Step 1: Confirm entity eligibility.**
Verify that your business is a qualifying separate entity (S-corp, C-corp, partnership, or multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership). If you operate as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC without a corporate election, stop here -- the strategy requires a separate taxpayer. Confirm with your advisor before proceeding.

**Step 2: Determine fair market value for your home as a meeting venue.**
Contact at least three comparable commercial venues in your local market (hotel conference rooms, executive suites, coworking event spaces, or private event facilities). Request written email quotes for a private meeting space accommodating your typical attendee count. Document the venue names, dates of inquiry, quoted rates, and what amenities are included. Calculate the median of the three quotes. Set your rental rate at or below that median. Save all quotes in a single FMV Analysis Memo. This rate applies for the full year and should be reviewed annually.

**Step 3: Draft and sign the rental agreement.**
Create a written rental agreement between you as homeowner (lessor) and the business entity (lessee). Include: property address, all planned rental dates, business purpose for each date, rental rate per day or per event, total amount for the year, and payment terms. Both parties must sign the agreement before the first rental day. Use the Augusta Rule Payment Cadence Implementation Worksheet to build this agreement.

**Step 4: Schedule the meetings.**
Place each rental day on the company calendar. The meeting must serve a genuine business purpose. Acceptable examples include: S-corp annual shareholder meeting, board of directors meeting, annual or quarterly business planning session, budget review session, company retreat with documented business agenda. The purpose must be documented in the agenda; a social gathering does not qualify.

**Step 5: Create contemporaneous documentation for each meeting.**
At least 48 hours before each meeting, prepare and distribute an agenda listing the date, location, start and end time, attendees, and specific business topics. During or immediately after the meeting, complete the meeting minutes capturing what was discussed and decided. Collect the attendee list. Take photographs. File everything together as a meeting packet for that rental date.

**Step 6: Issue an invoice and make the payment.**
After each meeting (or on a schedule agreed in the rental agreement), the homeowner issues a written invoice to the business for the rental amount. The business pays the invoice by check or ACH transfer from the business bank account directly to the homeowner's personal bank account. Include a memo reference such as "Section 280A(g) rental -- [date] meeting." Payment should be made promptly and should clear before the end of the tax year.

**Step 7: Record the expense on the business books.**
The business records the rent payment as a deductible business expense. On Form 1120-S, this appears on Line 11 (Rents). The bookkeeper or accountant should code the transaction to a "meeting room rental" or "facility rental" expense category and attach the invoice to the transaction record in the accounting system.

**Step 8: Update the day-count log.**
After each rental day, update the running log. Total rental days must not exceed 14 for the tax year. If you approach 12 or 13 days, pause and confirm the count with your advisor before scheduling additional rental days.

**Step 9: Issue Form 1099-MISC if required.**
For the 2026 tax year, the Form 1099-MISC filing threshold for rent is $2,000 (raised from $600 by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, effective for payments made in 2026). If the total rent paid to you during 2026 exceeds $2,000, the business must issue you a Form 1099-MISC (Box 1, Rents) by January 31, 2027. Even if you receive a 1099, the income remains excludable under Section 280A(g) on your personal return.

**Step 10: File the tax returns.**
On the business return (Form 1120-S): the rent deduction flows through ordinary income to shareholders via Schedule K-1. No special election or form is required at the entity level beyond recording the expense on Line 11.

On your personal return (Form 1040): do not report the Augusta Rule rental income anywhere on Schedule E. The income is excluded from gross income entirely under Section 280A(g). If you received a Form 1099-MISC for this income, work with your advisor to ensure the exclusion is properly reflected so the IRS matching system does not flag it as unreported income. The common practice is to report the income received and then enter an offsetting Section 280A(g) exclusion so the net is zero.

**Step 11: Retain all records.**
Assemble each year's complete Augusta Rule file -- rental agreement, FMV memo, invoices, payment records, day-count log, and all meeting packets -- into a folder (physical or digital) labeled by tax year. Retain for at least 7 years.

## Timing And Deadlines

**Before any rental day:** The rental agreement must be signed. Documentation cannot be backdated or reconstructed after the fact.

**By the close of business each rental day:** Meeting minutes and attendee lists should be finalized while the event is fresh. Same-week completion is the minimum standard; same-day is best practice.

**Promptly after each rental day (same month):** Issue the invoice and execute the payment from the business account to your personal account. Do not let payments accumulate to December or the end of the year without a clear paper trail tying each payment to a specific meeting date.

**Before December 31 of the tax year:** All rent payments must be made and cleared within the same tax year. An S-corp on the cash method deducts expenses in the year paid. Payments made in January for December meetings do not qualify for the prior year's deduction.

**By January 31 of the following year:** If total rent payments exceed $2,000, the business issues Form 1099-MISC to the homeowner.

**Annually (at the start of each new tax year):** Reset the day-count log to zero. Review and update the FMV Analysis Memo with fresh comparable venue quotes, since market rates change. Update the rental agreement for the new year's planned meeting dates.

**14-day hard ceiling:** The day count resets each January 1. There is no carryover of unused days from one year to the next. If you did not use all 14 days in 2025, you still start fresh at zero for 2026.

**Recurring rhythm:** The strategy requires action each time a meeting is held at your home: prepare agenda, hold meeting, complete minutes, issue invoice, make payment, update log. This is not a set-and-forget annual election; it requires execution per meeting day.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure (required).** The strategy only works when the homeowner and the business are separate taxpayers. Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs without a corporate tax election cannot use this strategy.

**Payroll and reasonable compensation.** If you are an S-corp owner-employee, the Augusta Rule rent payment does not substitute for or reduce your required reasonable compensation (the salary the IRS expects you to pay yourself as a working owner-employee before taking profit distributions). Keep payroll and Augusta Rule rent as separate, independent transactions. Mixing them or using rent to avoid payroll taxes is a compliance risk.

**Related-party scrutiny.** The IRS and Tax Court apply heightened scrutiny to transactions between a shareholder and their own corporation (*Sinopoli v. Commissioner*, TC Memo 2023-105). The rent amount must be genuinely comparable to local market rates. Document the rate-setting methodology before you set the rate, not after the fact.

**State income tax.** The federal Section 280A(g) exclusion applies to federal gross income only. States make their own conformity decisions. States that automatically conform to the Internal Revenue Code generally follow the exclusion. States that have decoupled from certain IRC provisions (including California at various times) may require you to report and pay state income tax on the rental income even though you owe no federal tax. Check your state's current conformity status with your advisor before the first rental day.

**State-level 1099 requirements.** Some states have independent information-reporting requirements that may differ from the federal Form 1099-MISC thresholds. Confirm whether your state requires information return filing for rent payments to individuals.

**Multiple states.** If your business has nexus (a taxable presence, such as employees or property) in more than one state, each state's treatment of the exclusion must be reviewed separately.

**2026 current-year status.** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025) did not modify Section 280A(g). The 14-day exclusion carries into 2026 unchanged. The Act did raise the Form 1099-MISC reporting threshold to $2,000, which affects the information-reporting step but not the substantive tax treatment of the rental income.

**C-corp considerations.** A C corporation can pay rent to its owner-shareholder and deduct it under the same Section 162 rules. The owner excludes the income under Section 280A(g). However, C-corp situations involve different tax considerations (corporate tax on income, double taxation of dividends) and should be reviewed separately with your advisor.

**Home office deduction interaction.** If you already deduct a home office under Section 280A(c)(1), coordinate the Augusta Rule meetings with your advisor. The Section 280A(g) provision and the home office deduction operate under the same statute and must be applied consistently. Days counted as Augusta Rule rental days cannot also be treated as home office days.

## Common Mistakes

**Exceeding 14 days.** This is the most consequential error. Day 15 eliminates the exclusion for the entire year, not just the excess days. Track the count throughout the year, not only at year-end.

**Backdating the rental agreement.** The agreement must be signed before the first rental day. An agreement signed in November to cover meetings held in March is not contemporaneous and will not withstand scrutiny.

**Setting the rate too high.** The Sinopoli case showed that courts will re-price the deduction to actual market comparables. Rates above comparable venue prices will be reduced. Build and save the FMV Analysis Memo before setting the rate.

**No separate payment.** Some owners assume they can make a journal entry or a bookkeeping adjustment without an actual cash transfer. The payment must be a real, traceable transfer from the business bank account to the personal account. Journal entries alone do not satisfy the requirement.

**Paying in cash.** Cash payments cannot be traced and are unreliable in an audit. Use check or ACH.

**No meeting minutes.** Agendas without minutes are insufficient. The Sinopoli court specifically noted the absence of minutes as a basis for disallowance. Write minutes on the day of the meeting.

**Generic or vague meeting descriptions.** "Monthly business meeting" is not enough. Minutes should name the decisions made, topics discussed, and action items assigned.

**Forgetting state conformity.** Owners assume the federal exclusion covers their state return. Confirm state treatment each year.

**Forgetting Form 1099-MISC.** If total rent paid exceeds $2,000 in 2026, the business is required to issue a 1099-MISC by January 31, 2027. Failure to issue required information returns can result in penalties.

**Using the strategy in a disregarded entity.** Single-member LLCs that have not made an S-corp or C-corp election are disregarded for federal tax purposes and are treated as the same taxpayer as the owner. The strategy does not work in this structure.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and confirm with your advisor before taking action in any of these situations:

1. **Before the first year of implementation.** Have your advisor confirm your entity structure qualifies, your state conforms to the federal exclusion, and the strategy is appropriate for your overall tax situation.

2. **Before setting the rental rate.** Present your FMV Analysis Memo and three comparable quotes to your advisor for review before signing the rental agreement.

3. **Any time the day count reaches 12.** Get confirmation before scheduling additional rental days. A mistake at day 15 is irreversible for that tax year.

4. **If your state has changed its conformity to IRC Section 280A(g).** State tax law changes happen and are not always widely publicized. Confirm annually if you file in a state with a history of decoupling from federal provisions.

5. **If you receive a notice from the IRS referencing rental income or a Form 1099-MISC that was not filed or was filed with an amount that appears unreported.** Do not respond without your advisor.

6. **If you operate in multiple states.** Each state's conformity and information-reporting requirements must be confirmed independently.

7. **If you want to add a second property or rent a property other than your primary residence.** The Section 280A(g) rules apply per dwelling unit; a different property requires a separate analysis.

8. **If you want to increase the rental rate significantly from one year to the next.** Sudden rate increases attract scrutiny. Prepare fresh FMV documentation each year.

## Before You Act

Confirm the following with your advisor before executing any part of this strategy:

- Your business entity is a separate taxpayer for federal income tax purposes and qualifies to pay deductible rent to you as homeowner.
- Your state of residence conforms to the federal Section 280A(g) exclusion and will not tax the rental income at the state level.
- Your rental rate is grounded in documented, contemporaneous comparables for your local market.
- The rental agreement is drafted and signed before the first rental day.
- The meeting purpose and documentation plan are adequate to withstand IRS scrutiny.
- The Augusta Rule rental payments are completely separate from and do not substitute for your required reasonable compensation as an S-corp owner-employee (if applicable).

Do not file a tax return claiming the Augusta Rule exclusion, deduct rent on a business return, make rental payments, or execute the rental agreement until you have discussed your specific facts with your advisor and received their go-ahead.
