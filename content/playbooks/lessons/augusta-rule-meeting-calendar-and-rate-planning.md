# Augusta Rule Meeting Calendar and Rate Planning

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The Augusta Rule, named after the practice of Augusta, Georgia homeowners renting their homes during the Masters Golf Tournament, is a provision in the U.S. tax code (Internal Revenue Code Section 280A(g)) that allows you to rent your personal residence to your own business for up to 14 days per year. The rental income you receive is completely excluded from your federal taxable income. At the same time, your business can deduct the rent it pays as an ordinary and necessary business expense. The result is a legal tax benefit on both sides of the same transaction: tax-free income in your personal pocket and a business deduction that reduces your company's taxable profit.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is available to you if ALL of the following are true:

- You own your primary home or a vacation home that qualifies as a residence under the tax code (a house, condominium, apartment, mobile home, boat, or similar structure that provides basic living accommodations).
- You operate a business through a separate legal entity that is treated as a separate taxpayer for federal income tax purposes. Qualifying entities include: S corporations, C corporations, partnerships, and multi-member LLCs.
- Your business holds legitimate meetings, planning sessions, strategy retreats, trainings, or similar events that have a real business purpose.
- The total number of days per calendar year that your home is rented to your business does not exceed 14 days.
- You charge your business a rental rate that reflects what a comparable local venue would charge for the same type of space (called "fair market value").

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do NOT use this strategy if any of the following apply:

- Your business is a sole proprietorship or a single-member LLC that has NOT elected to be taxed as an S corporation or C corporation. Because a disregarded entity is treated as the same taxpayer as its owner for federal income tax purposes, the IRS sees you renting space to yourself, which does not create a valid landlord-tenant relationship and will not support a business deduction.
- Your business rents the home for 15 or more days in a calendar year. Section 280A(g) is an all-or-nothing provision. Once you cross 14 days, the income exclusion disappears for the entire year and all rental income becomes taxable.
- You cannot document a genuine business purpose for each rental day. Meetings that exist only on paper, with no agendas, no minutes, and no actual business decisions made, will be disallowed in an IRS audit.
- Your proposed rental rate is significantly above what local event venues, hotel conference rooms, or comparable homes would charge for the same space and purpose. Inflated rates invite IRS scrutiny and will be reduced to market rates in an audit (see the Sinopoli v. Commissioner case below).
- You do not intend to make an actual, traceable payment from your business account to your personal account. Verbal agreements, cash transfers, or informal arrangements do not create the paper trail the IRS expects.
- Your state does not conform to federal Section 280A(g) income exclusion rules. Check your state tax treatment before proceeding (see Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats below).

## Key Terms

**IRC Section 280A(g):** The specific paragraph of the Internal Revenue Code that creates the Augusta Rule. It states that if a dwelling unit is used as a residence and is actually rented for fewer than 15 days during the taxable year, the rental income shall not be included in the gross income of the taxpayer. The full statutory text is available at 26 U.S.C. 280A(g).

**Dwelling unit used as a residence:** A home qualifies as your "residence" for purposes of this rule when you personally use it during the year. This includes your primary home and vacation or second homes that you occupy personally.

**Gross income exclusion:** An amount of income that is legally removed from your taxable income. You receive the money, but you do not owe federal income tax on it, and you do not report it on your tax return. This is different from a deduction, which only reduces taxable income by a fraction of the amount.

**Ordinary and necessary business expense (IRC Section 162):** The standard that every business deduction must meet. "Ordinary" means the expense is common and accepted in your industry. "Necessary" means it is helpful and appropriate for your trade or business. Expenses do not need to be indispensable to qualify.

**Fair market value (FMV):** The price that a willing buyer would pay to a willing seller when neither is under pressure to complete the transaction. For rental purposes, this means the rate a third party would pay to rent a comparable space in your area for the same type of event.

**Related-party transaction:** A business deal between two parties who are connected, such as an S corporation paying rent to its own shareholder. The IRS applies extra scrutiny to related-party transactions to verify that pricing reflects arm's-length market rates and that the arrangement has economic substance.

**Separate taxpayer:** A business entity that the IRS treats as a taxpayer legally distinct from you as an individual. S corporations, C corporations, partnerships, and multi-member LLCs are separate taxpayers. Sole proprietorships and single-member LLCs taxed as disregarded entities are NOT.

**S corporation (S corp):** A corporation that has elected under Subchapter S of the Internal Revenue Code to pass its income and deductions through to shareholders' personal tax returns, avoiding corporate-level income tax. Owners of S corps must pay themselves a reasonable salary (W-2 wages) before taking profit distributions.

**C corporation (C corp):** A standard corporation taxed as a separate entity under Subchapter C of the Internal Revenue Code. C corps pay corporate income tax on their own profits, and shareholders pay tax again when they receive dividends.

**Pass-through entity:** A business structure (S corp, partnership, multi-member LLC) whose income and deductions flow through to owners' personal tax returns rather than being taxed at the entity level.

**Form 1099-MISC:** An IRS information return that businesses use to report certain payments they make, including rent. For tax year 2026, the reporting threshold for rent paid by a business is $2,000 or more, raised from the prior $600 threshold by Section 70433 of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (H.R. 1, signed July 4, 2025), effective for payments made after December 31, 2025. If your business pays you $2,000 or more in rent during 2026, it is generally required to issue you a Form 1099-MISC reporting that payment to the IRS. The threshold is indexed for inflation beginning in 2027.

**Schedule E (Form 1040):** The personal tax form used to report supplemental income and losses, including rental income. Under the Augusta Rule, if you qualify for the income exclusion, you do not report the rental income on Schedule E or anywhere else on your personal return.

**Form 1120-S:** The annual federal income tax return filed by S corporations. The rent deduction appears on this form.

**Form 1120:** The annual federal income tax return filed by C corporations. The rent deduction appears on this form.

**Form 1065:** The annual federal income tax return filed by partnerships. The rent deduction appears on this form.

**Sinopoli v. Commissioner, T.C. Memo. 2023-105:** A 2023 U.S. Tax Court memorandum opinion in which an S corporation paid shareholders approximately $291,000 in rent over three years for monthly home meetings. The Tax Court disallowed most of the deductions because the rent was far above comparable local meeting space rates (which were approximately $500 per day, as determined by the IRS through its own market research of local hotel and meeting spaces), and the meetings lacked adequate documentation. This case is the leading example of Augusta Rule overreach. The full opinion is available at T.C. Memo. 2023-105; an accessible summary appears at https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2023/8/15/tax-court-greatly-reduces-rent-deduction-for-s-corporation-where-taxpayer-attempted-to-take-advantage-of-the-masters-rule.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before starting implementation:

1. **Business entity type and tax status.** Confirm that your business is an S corp, C corp, partnership, or multi-member LLC, and confirm its tax classification with your advisor. Know your entity's Employer Identification Number (EIN).
2. **Home ownership details.** Confirm that you own (or have a qualifying ownership interest in) the home you plan to rent. Know the address and whether it is your primary home or a second home.
3. **Current year business meeting schedule.** A list of the actual business meetings, retreats, strategy sessions, or trainings you plan to hold at your home for the year, including proposed dates. You cannot exceed 14 total days.
4. **Local market rental rate data.** Research what comparable spaces charge for similar events in your area. You need at least three comparable rate data points before setting your rate (see Step 3 of the Implementation section).
5. **Business bank account access.** Your business must make the rent payment from its own business bank account to your personal account. Confirm you have both accounts available and that they have separate account numbers.
6. **Prior-year treatment (if applicable).** If you have used the Augusta Rule in prior years, gather those rental agreements and confirm that neither your personal nor business return reported the income or deduction inconsistently.

## Required Documents And Records

Create and retain all of the following. The IRS can audit returns up to three years after filing (six years if the IRS believes income was underreported by more than 25%), so retain all Augusta Rule records for at least six years after the return due date.

**Before each rental day:**
- Signed rental agreement (lease) between your business and you personally, executed before the rental period begins. The agreement should state: the address being rented, the specific date(s), the space being used, the daily rate, the total payment amount, and the business purpose of the rental.

**On or within 48 hours of each rental day:**
- Written meeting agenda showing the specific business topics discussed.
- Attendee list with names of all participants.
- Meeting minutes or notes documenting the topics covered and decisions made.
- Any supporting materials used in the meeting (budgets, reports, proposals, financial statements, marketing plans, vendor quotes, hiring plans, etc.).

**At time of payment:**
- Business check or bank transfer from the business account to your personal account. The memo line should reference "rent" or "facility rental" and cite the date(s) of the rental.
- Bank statements or wire/transfer confirmations showing the payment.

**For fair market value support:**
- Screenshots of at least three comparable venues (Airbnb listings for similar homes, hotel conference room rates, Peerspace or coworking space rentals, or event venue quotes) with the date the screenshots were captured. These should be from the same general time period as your meetings, not pulled retroactively.

**Annual tracking document:**
- A running day-count showing each rental date used during the calendar year and the cumulative total. This document is critical for ensuring you never cross 14 days.

**Tax return records:**
- Copy of the rental agreement and all meeting documentation, filed with your business tax return records for the year the deduction is claimed.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Step 1: Confirm your entity qualifies

Verify with your advisor that your business entity is an S corp, C corp, partnership, or multi-member LLC, and that it is treated as a separate taxpayer from you as an individual. If your LLC has not made an S corp or C corp election, it is likely a disregarded entity, and this strategy does not apply until that election is made. Do not proceed to Step 2 until this is confirmed.

### Step 2: Plan your meeting calendar for the year

Identify the specific business meetings, strategy sessions, trainings, board meetings, or planning retreats you will hold at your home this year. Write down the purpose of each meeting and a proposed date. Spread them throughout the year rather than clustering them together, as meetings that appear compressed into consecutive days on an annual basis may draw IRS scrutiny about whether separate meetings actually occurred. Total all proposed days. The combined total must stay at 14 days or fewer.

Common qualifying meeting types:
- Annual or quarterly strategic planning
- Budget review and approval
- Board of directors or officer meetings
- Key performance indicator (KPI) review
- Hiring decisions and compensation planning
- Marketing and sales strategy review
- Vendor or contract review
- Annual compliance and legal review

Open your attached Augusta Rule Meeting Calendar and Rate Planning Implementation Worksheet and enter your planned meeting dates and purposes in the Calendar Planning section before proceeding.

### Step 3: Determine your fair market rental rate

Research what it would cost to rent a comparable space in your local area for the same type of business event. Your goal is to identify the market rate that a third party would charge for similar square footage, amenities, and event type. Do not set your rate first and then search for data to support it; start with the data and let it guide your rate.

Sources to check:
- Airbnb and Vrbo: search for homes of comparable size and quality in your area, filtering for "entire home" rentals, and note the nightly or daily rate for the dates closest to your planned meetings.
- Hotel conference rooms: call two to three hotels near your home and ask for their conference room or meeting room rental rates by the day.
- Peerspace, Splacer, or similar venue platforms: search for event and meeting spaces in your zip code.
- Local event venues: wedding venues, banquet halls, and community centers often rent space for corporate events; call for day rates.

Document at least three data points, including the source, location, square footage or capacity, and rate. Save dated screenshots or printed quotes. Your rental rate should fall within the range these comparables support. If your home has unusual features (professional-grade kitchen, large outdoor space, distinctive architecture) that comparable venues also have, you may be able to justify a rate at the higher end of the range. If your rate would exceed what local venues charge, discuss the justification with your advisor before proceeding.

Enter your rate analysis in the Fair Market Rate Documentation section of the Implementation Worksheet.

### Step 4: Draft and sign a rental agreement before the first meeting

Prepare a written rental agreement between you (as the homeowner) and your business (as the tenant) before the first meeting date occurs. Do not backdate agreements. The agreement should include:

- Your name and home address (as landlord)
- Your business name and EIN (as tenant)
- Each rental date or date range covered
- The space being rented (the entire home or the specific area, such as the main living and dining area)
- The daily rental rate
- The total payment amount
- The business purpose of the rental
- Signature lines for both you as landlord and you as an authorized officer of your business

Both you personally and your business need to sign. If your S corp or C corp has other officers or directors, having a second signature from another officer can strengthen the arm's-length appearance of the transaction.

Use the rental agreement template in the Implementation Worksheet as your starting point.

### Step 5: Hold the meeting and document it in real time

On each rental day, do the following:
- Conduct a genuine business meeting with a real agenda. Do not hold a social gathering and call it a meeting.
- Take attendance (names of all people present).
- Prepare written minutes or notes during or immediately after the meeting. Record what was discussed, what decisions were made, and what action items resulted.
- Collect any supporting documents used in the meeting (financial reports, presentations, proposals).

File all documentation together in a folder labeled with the meeting date. Do not wait until year-end or until an audit notice arrives to reconstruct these records. Courts have rejected retroactively prepared documentation.

### Step 6: Issue the rent payment through proper business channels

After each rental date (or in a lump payment after all meetings for the period have concluded), have your business issue a check or bank transfer from the business account to your personal account. The payment should:
- Come from the business's bank account (not a shared account or personal account)
- Reference the rental agreement and dates in the memo field
- Be made within a reasonable time after the rental (within 30 days is a common best practice)
- Match the exact amount stated in the rental agreement

Retain bank statements or transfer confirmations showing the payment.

### Step 7: Track your rental day total and stop at 14

Maintain an annual day-count tracker. Each time you hold a meeting at your home under this arrangement, add that date to the tracker and update the cumulative total. The moment you reach 14 days for the calendar year, stop scheduling additional Augusta Rule meetings. Do not hold a 15th day under any circumstances. A single additional day eliminates the income exclusion for every dollar of rent already paid that year.

Use the Day Tracker section of the Implementation Worksheet for this purpose.

### Step 8: Record the deduction on your business tax return

When your business files its annual return, deduct the total rent paid as a business expense under Section 162:
- S corporations: deduct on Form 1120-S in the "other deductions" section (consult with your preparer on the specific line, as rent may appear under "rent" or "other deductions" depending on the form version and software).
- C corporations: deduct on Form 1120.
- Partnerships: deduct on Form 1065.

Label the deduction as "rent" or "facility rental" and retain the rental agreement and meeting documentation in your business records.

### Step 9: Handle the income on your personal return

Under IRC Section 280A(g), if the total rental days were 14 or fewer, you do NOT report the rental income on your personal Form 1040. You do not include it on Schedule E. You do not include it anywhere on your personal return. The income is legally excluded.

For tax year 2026, your business is required to issue you a Form 1099-MISC only if the total rent paid during the year is $2,000 or more (raised from the prior $600 threshold by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, effective for payments made after December 31, 2025). If rent paid is between $1 and $1,999, no 1099-MISC is required. If your business does issue you a Form 1099-MISC because rent reached or exceeded $2,000, the IRS matching system will expect to see the income somewhere on your return. In that case, discuss the correct handling with your advisor. A common approach is to show the income and an equal offsetting exclusion referenced to IRC Section 280A(g) so that the net taxable amount is zero, preventing an automated IRS mismatch notice while preserving your exclusion.

### Step 10: Conduct an annual review before the following year

At the end of each calendar year (or in the first quarter before filing), review the prior year's documentation with your advisor. Confirm that:
- Rental days did not exceed 14
- All rental agreements, meeting minutes, and payment records are complete and filed
- The rental rate remains supported by current market data
- Business and personal returns are consistent with each other
- Your entity structure and tax status have not changed in a way that affects eligibility

## Timing And Deadlines

**Calendar-year cap:** The 14-day limit resets every January 1. Days do not carry over from one year to the next. Plan your meeting calendar at the start of each year and track days in real time.

**No consecutive-day requirement:** The 14 days do not need to be used consecutively. You may spread them across the year (for example, 12 monthly meetings plus one 2-day annual retreat, as long as total days do not exceed 14).

**Rental agreement execution:** Sign the rental agreement before the first rental day it covers. An agreement signed after the rental period has already occurred looks manufactured and reduces its documentary value.

**Meeting documentation:** Prepare or finalize meeting minutes and agendas within 48 hours of each meeting. Do not prepare them weeks or months later.

**Payment timing:** Issue the rent payment from the business bank account within 30 days of the rental date(s) covered. Payments made many months later, or at year-end in a lump sum covering the entire year, raise questions about whether the arrangement was genuine.

**Business tax return deadlines (for year 2026 returns):**
- S corporations (Form 1120-S): March 16, 2026 (original) or September 15, 2026 (with extension). Note: 2026 returns cover the 2025 tax year for calendar-year filers; 2027 returns cover the 2026 tax year.
- C corporations (Form 1120): April 15, 2026 (original) for calendar-year corps, or October 15, 2026 (with extension).
- Partnerships (Form 1065): March 16, 2026 (original) or September 15, 2026 (with extension).

The Augusta Rule deduction is reported on the business return for the tax year in which the rent was paid. [Confirm 2026 return due dates with your advisor, as dates may shift if they fall on a weekend or federal holiday.]

**Record retention:** Retain all Augusta Rule documentation for at least six years from the due date of the tax return on which the deduction was claimed.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity requirement (critical):** The Augusta Rule requires a transaction between two separate taxpayers. If your business is a sole proprietorship or a single-member LLC that has not elected S corp or C corp status, it is a "disregarded entity" for federal tax purposes, meaning the IRS treats your business as the same taxpayer as you personally. There is no valid rental transaction between the same taxpayer, and no deduction will survive IRS scrutiny. If you are currently operating as a disregarded entity and want to use this strategy, discuss an S corp or C corp election with your advisor before implementing.

**S corporation payroll requirement:** S corp owners are required to pay themselves "reasonable compensation" (a W-2 salary that reflects what the IRS would expect a third party to be paid for the work the owner performs) before taking distributions. The Augusta Rule rental income is a separate payment and does not count toward or satisfy the reasonable compensation requirement. If you are an S corp owner who is not taking a W-2 salary or is taking a salary the IRS would view as unreasonably low, address the payroll issue before layering in the Augusta Rule, as the IRS tends to examine the full compensation picture when reviewing S corp owner transactions.

**State tax conformity:** Most states with an income tax conform to the federal Section 280A(g) exclusion, meaning the rental income is also excluded at the state level. However, not all states conform, and some states have their own rules or reporting requirements. Before implementing this strategy, confirm with your advisor how your specific state treats rental income excluded under Section 280A(g). States known to have non-conformity or independent filing requirements in some circumstances include California and Virginia, among others. [Confirm current state-specific treatment with your advisor, as state tax law changes frequently and cannot be verified by the author against all 50 states' official sources.]

**The business deduction must meet Section 162 standards independently:** Even if the homeowner's income is excluded under Section 280A(g), the business's deduction still must satisfy the ordinary and necessary business expense standard under Section 162. This means the meeting must have actually occurred, must have had a genuine business purpose, and the rate paid must be reasonable and consistent with local market rates. A business cannot use Section 280A(g) as a mechanism to shift money from the company to the owner without genuine business substance backing each meeting.

**No home-office deduction stacking:** If you are already claiming a home office deduction for the same space on a regular and exclusive basis, discuss with your advisor how the Augusta Rule rental days interact with that deduction. The two strategies apply different code sections and have different documentation requirements. Do not assume they are automatically compatible.

**Current-year status of the law:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (H.R. 1, signed July 4, 2025) made no changes to Section 280A(g) itself. The Augusta Rule carries into 2026 unchanged. However, the same Act did change the 1099-MISC reporting threshold from $600 to $2,000 for payments made after December 31, 2025 (see Section 70433). This means that while the income exclusion rule is unchanged, when a Form 1099-MISC is triggered for the rent payment has changed for tax year 2026. The guidance in this playbook reflects the new $2,000 threshold throughout. Tax law is subject to further change by Congress at any time; monitor legislative developments or ask your advisor to flag any changes to Section 280A.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Using a disregarded entity.** The most common disqualifying error is attempting to rent from a sole proprietorship or single-member LLC that has not elected S corp status. The rental payment is not arm's-length and the deduction will not hold.

**2. Crossing 14 days.** The exclusion is binary. Fourteen days preserves it; 15 days eliminates it for the entire year. There is no prorated benefit for the first 14 days if you go over. Maintain a running day tracker throughout the year and stop at 14.

**3. Backdating the rental agreement.** Creating or signing a rental agreement after the meeting has already occurred weakens the agreement's credibility. Prepare and sign agreements before each rental period.

**4. Retroactive documentation.** Preparing meeting agendas, minutes, or attendee lists weeks or months after the meeting took place rather than contemporaneously with the meeting. Retroactive records are a major red flag in audits and are often disallowed entirely.

**5. Setting an inflated rental rate.** Charging a daily rate far above what local comparable venues charge, without documented support, invites IRS scrutiny and rate reduction. The Sinopoli case (T.C. Memo. 2023-105) is the clearest warning: the Tax Court allowed only $500 per meeting when the taxpayer charged far more without adequate market support.

**6. Making untraceable payments.** Using cash, personal check, or informal transfers instead of a business bank account check or wire transfer makes it impossible to demonstrate that the business actually paid rent to you. The payment must flow from the business account to your personal account with a clear paper trail.

**7. Holding social events and calling them meetings.** If the primary purpose of an event is personal (a holiday party, a family gathering, a social dinner), calling it a business meeting does not make it one. The IRS looks at actual business substance: was a real agenda followed? Were real business decisions made? Would you have held the same meeting at a paid third-party venue if your home were not available?

**8. Misunderstanding the 2026 Form 1099-MISC threshold.** For tax year 2026, a Form 1099-MISC for rent is required only when rent paid is $2,000 or more, not the prior $600 threshold. If your total annual rent payment is between $1 and $1,999, your business is not required to issue a 1099-MISC for 2026. If rent does reach $2,000 or more, work with your advisor to address the 1099 correctly on your personal return while preserving the Section 280A(g) exclusion.

**9. Inconsistent treatment between personal and business returns.** If your business deducts the rent and your personal return shows the same income as taxable, or vice versa, the mismatch creates an audit trail. Both returns must be consistent: business deducts the rent, personal return excludes the income.

**10. Skipping the annual review.** Business structure, meeting cadence, local rental rates, and tax law all change. Reviewing the arrangement annually ensures it remains compliant and properly documented.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before taking action in any of the following situations:

- Before implementing for the first time. Your advisor must confirm your entity type, tax status, and eligibility before you execute any rental agreement or hold any meetings under this arrangement.
- If you are considering making an S corp election specifically to enable this strategy. The S corp election has payroll, salary, and other compliance consequences that must be evaluated in full before electing.
- Before setting your daily rental rate. Your advisor should review your comparable market data and confirm the rate is defensible before you sign any agreement.
- If you received a Form 1099-MISC from your business for the rental payment (issued when total rent paid is $2,000 or more for tax year 2026). The correct handling on your personal return requires professional guidance to avoid an IRS mismatch notice while preserving your exclusion.
- If your total planned meeting days are approaching 14 for the year. Your advisor should review the day count and confirm no additional rental days are appropriate before you schedule more.
- If any meeting was held primarily for personal rather than business reasons. Do not count that day as a rental day until your advisor confirms the meeting meets the ordinary and necessary business standard.
- If you operate in multiple states or your home is in a different state from your business. State conformity questions require state-specific review.
- If you received any IRS notice or inquiry related to rent paid by your business or rental income on your personal return.
- If your business structure or tax classification changed during the year.
- If you are planning to sell the home in the near future. Discuss whether the Augusta Rule usage affects your home sale exclusion under Section 121 with your advisor before completing any transactions.

This playbook teaches you how the Augusta Rule works and the steps required to implement it correctly. It does not replace the review of your specific facts by a qualified tax advisor.

Before you execute a rental agreement, hold a meeting under this arrangement, make or receive any rent payment, or report or exclude any income or deduction connected to this strategy, confirm all of the following with your advisor:
