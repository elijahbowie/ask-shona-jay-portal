# Vehicle Mileage and Actual Expense Kit

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

If you use a vehicle for business, the IRS allows you to deduct the business-use portion of your vehicle costs. You can calculate that deduction one of two ways: the standard mileage rate (multiply business miles driven by a flat cents-per-mile rate) or the actual expense method (add up what you actually spent on gas, insurance, repairs, depreciation, and similar costs, then multiply by your business-use percentage). For tax year 2026, the IRS business standard mileage rate is 72.5 cents per mile. This playbook walks you through choosing the right method, building an airtight mileage log using the attached Vehicle Mileage Log download, and correctly reporting the deduction on your return.

## Who This Applies To

This lesson applies to you if ALL of the following are true:

- You are self-employed (sole proprietor filing Schedule C), a partner in a partnership (Form 1065), or an S-corporation owner-employee whose business reimburses vehicle expenses through an accountable plan (see Key Terms).
- You use a personal or business-owned vehicle at least partly for qualifying business purposes (client visits, business errands, travel between job sites, business meetings, etc.).
- You have fewer than five vehicles operating in your business simultaneously (if you operate five or more, the standard mileage rate is not available to you -- you must use actual expenses).
- Your vehicle is a car, van, pickup, or panel truck. Certain specialized vehicles (semi-trucks, heavy construction equipment, vehicles over 14,000 lbs GVWR) follow different rules not covered here; confirm with your advisor.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do NOT use the strategy on this page if any of the following apply:

- **You are a W-2 employee.** Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025), the suspension of unreimbursed employee expense deductions originally enacted by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 is now permanent. W-2 employees (other than Armed Forces reservists, fee-basis state and local government officials, qualified performing artists, and eligible educators) cannot deduct business vehicle costs on their personal return, regardless of method. If your employer does not reimburse you, ask your advisor about the employer accountable plan approach instead.
- **You operate five or more vehicles simultaneously.** You must use actual expenses for all of them.
- **You use the vehicle exclusively for commuting.** Commuting miles (driving between your home and your regular, main place of work) are never deductible. Only trips beyond the regular commute -- to a client, a second work location, a supply run, or a temporary work site -- qualify.
- **You chose the actual expense method (including depreciation methods like Section 179 or bonus depreciation) in the first year you placed the vehicle in business service.** Once you use actual expenses in year one, you can never switch to the standard mileage rate for that vehicle. If this is your situation, continue with actual expenses and skip the standard mileage sections below.
- **You lease the vehicle and did not use the standard mileage rate starting from the first year of the lease.** For leased vehicles, if you start with standard mileage, you must continue using it for the entire lease term.
- **The vehicle is used for hire (taxi, limousine service, etc.)** -- standard mileage is not available.
- **You have no written records of business use.** Without a contemporaneous mileage log, the IRS will disallow the deduction. If your records are incomplete, start today; you cannot reconstruct a full year retroactively with confidence under audit.

## Key Terms

**Standard mileage rate:** A flat per-mile rate set by the IRS each year that combines all vehicle operating costs (fuel, oil, tires, maintenance, insurance, registration, and a built-in depreciation component) into one number. For 2026, the rate is 72.5 cents per mile. You multiply your qualified business miles by this rate and deduct the result. You do not separately deduct gas, repairs, or depreciation.

**Actual expense method:** You track every dollar you spend on the vehicle (gas, oil, repairs, tires, insurance, registration, license fees, garage rent, lease payments, and depreciation), add them up for the year, then multiply the total by your business-use percentage to get your deduction.

**Business-use percentage:** The share of total miles you drove that were for business purposes. Formula: business miles divided by total miles driven during the year. Example: if you drove 15,000 miles total and 9,000 were for business, your business-use percentage is 60%.

**Listed property:** IRS jargon for assets that can easily be used for personal purposes -- cars and other vehicles are listed property. Listed property is subject to stricter substantiation (proof) requirements than ordinary business assets, and the deduction is limited if business use falls at or below 50%.

**Depreciation:** The tax deduction for the gradual wearing out of a business asset over time. For vehicles, depreciation lets you deduct a portion of the vehicle's purchase price each year. The IRS caps annual depreciation on passenger automobiles (the "luxury auto limits," even though the caps apply to ordinary cars).

**Section 179 expensing:** An IRS election (under Internal Revenue Code Section 179) that lets you deduct the full cost of qualifying property in the year you buy it, instead of spreading deductions across multiple years. For vehicles, Section 179 is subject to the luxury auto annual caps described in The Numbers section, unless the vehicle exceeds 6,000 lbs GVWR.

**Bonus depreciation:** An additional first-year depreciation deduction under IRC Section 168(k). For property acquired after January 19, 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act restored bonus depreciation to 100%. Like Section 179, bonus depreciation on passenger automobiles is subject to the annual luxury auto caps.

**Luxury auto limits (Section 280F):** Annual dollar caps on total depreciation for passenger automobiles, regardless of how expensive the car actually is. See The Numbers section for 2026 figures. These limits apply even to inexpensive vehicles because of how the statute is written.

**GVWR (Gross Vehicle Weight Rating):** The maximum weight a vehicle is rated to handle, printed on the driver's door jamb sticker. GVWR determines whether special higher limits or the heavy-SUV Section 179 cap apply to your vehicle.

**Heavy SUV Section 179 cap:** SUVs, trucks, and vans primarily designed to carry passengers with a GVWR between 6,001 and 14,000 lbs have a separate, lower Section 179 cap ($32,000 for 2026) that is distinct from the overall Section 179 limit. Heavy trucks and vans NOT primarily designed to carry passengers are not subject to this cap.

**Commuting miles:** Miles driven between your home and your regular, principal place of work. These are personal expenses and are never deductible, even if you work during the commute or the trip is long.

**Contemporaneous records:** Records created at or near the time of each business trip, not reconstructed weeks or months later. The IRS requires that mileage logs be kept in a timely manner; a weekly log is explicitly acceptable under IRS guidance.

**Accountable plan:** An employer reimbursement arrangement that meets three IRS requirements: (1) expenses must have a business connection, (2) employees must substantiate expenses with adequate records within a reasonable time (60 days is the IRS safe harbor), and (3) employees must return any excess reimbursement within a reasonable time (120 days is the safe harbor). Reimbursements made under a valid accountable plan are not included in the employee's W-2 wages and are not subject to payroll taxes.

**Form 4562:** IRS form used to report depreciation and amortization, including vehicle depreciation under the actual expense method, and to disclose annual vehicle information for listed property.

**Schedule C:** IRS form (Profit or Loss From Business) filed by sole proprietors and single-member LLCs taxed as sole proprietors. Vehicle expenses appear on Line 9 (car and truck expenses).

**Business-use percentage election threshold:** Vehicle depreciation deductions are subject to the luxury auto limits only when business use exceeds 50%. If business use is 50% or below, you are limited to straight-line depreciation over a longer recovery period and cannot use Section 179 or bonus depreciation for that vehicle.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before you begin. Your mileage log will capture most of them throughout the year, but you also need these at tax time:

1. **January 1 odometer reading** for each vehicle used in the business (or the reading on the first day you used the vehicle for business during 2026 if it was not in business service on January 1).
2. **December 31 odometer reading** for each vehicle.
3. **Total business miles driven** for each vehicle during 2026 (from your mileage log).
4. **Total commuting miles** for each vehicle (not deductible -- needed to calculate business-use percentage).
5. **Total personal miles** for each vehicle (total minus business minus commuting).
6. **Total miles driven** for each vehicle (business + commuting + personal).
7. **Date each vehicle was first placed in business service** (the first day you drove it for business purposes).
8. **Purchase price (cost basis)** of each vehicle if using actual expenses with depreciation.
9. **Vehicle identification information:** year, make, model, and VIN.
10. **Whether you have a written mileage log** (Form 4562, Line 24b, requires you to certify this).
11. **Entity type:** sole proprietor (Schedule C), partnership (Form 1065), S-corp (Form 1120-S), or C-corp (Form 1120). This affects where the deduction appears on your return.

If using the actual expense method, also gather:

12. Receipts or records for: gasoline and oil, repairs and maintenance, tires, insurance premiums, registration and license fees, garage or parking fees paid regularly, lease payments (if leasing).

## Required Documents And Records

### Mileage Log (for all taxpayers)

Every business vehicle trip requires a record containing at least these four elements (per IRS Publication 463):

- **Date** of the trip
- **Destination** (city or address)
- **Business purpose** (what business activity was conducted)
- **Miles driven** for that trip

In addition, record your odometer reading at the start and end of the year (or each time you start using a new vehicle). Per-trip odometer readings are not legally required but are strongly recommended because they make your log harder to challenge.

**Timing rule:** Records must be kept contemporaneously -- meaning at or near the time of each trip. A weekly log is acceptable. A year-end reconstruction is not. Use the attached Vehicle Mileage Log to stay current throughout the year.

### Actual Expense Receipts and Records

If you use the actual expense method, retain:

- Receipts or credit card statements for fuel, oil changes, repairs, tires, insurance, registration fees, and any other vehicle operating costs.
- Lease agreements if the vehicle is leased.
- Purchase documents (sales contract, loan paperwork) showing cost basis if you own the vehicle.
- Any trade-in or disposition records if you sold or stopped using the vehicle during 2026.

### Record Retention Period

Keep vehicle records for at least **three years** from the date you file the return on which the deduction is claimed (the standard IRS audit window). Because vehicles often span multiple tax years and depreciation deductions extend across several years, most tax professionals recommend keeping records for the full depreciation period of the vehicle plus three years. A conservative approach is seven years.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Part A: Decide Which Method to Use

**Step 1. Confirm eligibility for standard mileage rate.**
Review the "Who This Applies To" and "When This Is Not A Fit" sections above. If you used actual expenses (including any depreciation method) in the first year you placed this vehicle in business service, you must continue using actual expenses. If this is the vehicle's first year in business service, you may choose either method this year -- but choose carefully, because the choice in year one locks you in for the vehicle's business life (with the exception noted in Step 2).

**Step 2. Understand the switching rule.**
- If you choose **standard mileage in year one**, you may switch to actual expenses in a later year. However, if you switch, you must use straight-line depreciation (a flat annual deduction spread evenly over the vehicle's remaining useful life) rather than accelerated methods.
- If you choose **actual expenses in year one**, you can never switch to standard mileage for that vehicle.

**Step 3. Estimate your deduction under each method (optional but recommended).**
Once you have your total business miles and actual costs for the year, run a quick comparison:
- Standard mileage deduction = total business miles x 72.5 cents
- Actual expense deduction = (total actual costs x business-use percentage) + applicable depreciation

Generally, standard mileage is simpler and favors taxpayers with fuel-efficient vehicles or low actual costs. Actual expenses tend to produce larger deductions for high-cost vehicles, heavily used vehicles, or new vehicles eligible for accelerated depreciation. Ask your advisor to run both calculations in the first year.

### Part B: Maintain Your Mileage Log All Year

**Step 4. Record every business trip in the Vehicle Mileage Log.**
For each business trip, log immediately (at minimum, same week):
- Date
- Starting point and destination (address or city)
- Business purpose (brief but specific: "client meeting with [client type]," "bank deposit for business account," "supply run for job site," etc.)
- Miles driven for that trip

Do not log personal trips or commuting miles in the business section of the log. If you use the log for your own reference, mark them clearly as non-business.

**Step 5. Record odometer readings.**
At the start of January 2026 (or the first business-use day), note your odometer. At the end of December 2026, note it again. This gives you your total miles driven for the year, which you need to calculate your business-use percentage.

**Step 6. Total your columns at month-end or year-end.**
The Vehicle Mileage Log has columns for monthly subtotals. Tally business miles by month. At year-end, sum all monthly totals for your annual business miles figure.

### Part C: Collect Actual Expense Records (Actual Expense Method Only)

**Step 7. Gather all vehicle cost receipts throughout the year.**
Keep a folder (physical or digital) for: fuel receipts, repair and maintenance invoices, tire purchases, insurance premium statements, registration renewal notices, and any parking or garage fees that are regular business expenses (not trip-by-trip parking, which may be deducted separately).

**Step 8. Tally total annual vehicle costs.**
At year-end, add up all vehicle expenses for the year. This is your total vehicle cost before the business-use adjustment.

**Step 9. Calculate your business-use percentage.**
Divide your total business miles (from Step 6) by your total miles driven for the year (from your odometer readings). Round to two decimal places. Example: 9,000 business miles / 15,000 total miles = 60.00% business use.

**Step 10. Calculate your deductible actual expenses.**
Multiply total vehicle costs by your business-use percentage. Example: $8,400 total costs x 60% = $5,040 deductible before depreciation.

### Part D: Calculate Depreciation (Actual Expense Method Only)

**Step 11. Determine whether your vehicle is subject to the luxury auto limits (Section 280F).**
Passenger automobiles (cars, SUVs, trucks, and vans with GVWR under 6,000 lbs) are subject to annual depreciation caps. Check your vehicle's GVWR on the driver's door jamb sticker.

- GVWR under 6,000 lbs: Subject to luxury auto caps (see The Numbers section). Maximum first-year depreciation is $20,300 with bonus depreciation, or $12,300 without, before the business-use percentage reduction.
- GVWR 6,001 to 14,000 lbs (heavy SUVs): Not subject to the standard luxury auto caps, but Section 179 is capped at $32,000 for SUVs primarily designed to carry passengers. Heavy trucks and vans in this range that are NOT primarily for passengers are not subject to the $32,000 SUV cap and may be fully expensed up to the overall Section 179 limit.
- GVWR over 14,000 lbs: No luxury auto caps or heavy-SUV cap; not addressed further in this playbook.

**Step 12. Confirm business-use percentage exceeds 50%.**
If your business-use percentage is 50% or below, you may not use Section 179 or bonus depreciation, and you are limited to straight-line depreciation (equal annual deductions) over 5 years (or longer per IRS tables). Confirm with your advisor.

**Step 13. Apply the appropriate depreciation method and cap.**
Work with your advisor and the Form 4562 instructions to calculate your allowable depreciation. The key inputs are: cost basis, business-use percentage, whether you elect Section 179, whether bonus depreciation applies, and the annual luxury auto cap. Your advisor's tax software will handle the computation; your job is to supply the inputs accurately.

### Part E: Report the Deduction

**Step 14. Complete Form 4562 if required.**
You must file Form 4562 if you are depreciating a vehicle (actual expense method) or if you are required to answer the vehicle questions in Section B of Form 4562. For any vehicle used by a sole proprietor, partner, or owner of more than 5% of the business, complete Section B of Form 4562 even if you use the standard mileage rate.

**Step 15. Report on the correct business return.**
- **Sole proprietor / single-member LLC (Schedule C):** Vehicle expenses appear on Line 9 (car and truck expenses). Depreciation appears on Line 13.
- **Partnership (Form 1065):** Vehicle expenses are deducted on the partnership return (or allocated through the partner's Schedule K-1 if the vehicle is partner-owned). Confirm with your advisor which approach your partnership uses.
- **S-corporation (Form 1120-S):** If the business owns the vehicle, expenses appear on the corporate return. If you own the vehicle personally and the S-corp reimburses you under an accountable plan, the reimbursement is deducted by the corporation and is not included in your W-2.
- **C-corporation (Form 1120):** Business-owned vehicle costs are deducted on the corporate return. Personal use of a company vehicle must be included in the employee's W-2 as taxable compensation.

**Step 16. Answer the Form 4562 written records question honestly.**
Line 24b of Form 4562 asks whether you have written evidence supporting your business-use claim. Answer truthfully. If you do not have a contemporaneous written log, do not claim the deduction until you have one for the current year going forward.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity type matters for the deduction location:**
- Sole proprietors deduct vehicle costs directly on Schedule C. No payroll involved.
- S-corp owners must either have the corporation own the vehicle (and deduct costs on the S-corp return) or use an accountable plan reimbursement. If you personally own the vehicle and the S-corp does not reimburse you, the deduction is lost because W-2 employees cannot claim unreimbursed vehicle expenses.
- C-corp employees who use a company vehicle for personal purposes must have the fair market value of that personal use added to their W-2 as taxable compensation. This applies even to owner-employees.
- Partnership vehicles may be owned by the partnership (deducted on Form 1065) or by individual partners (potentially deducted on Schedule E as an unreimbursed partner expense -- confirm this treatment with your advisor as it can be fact-specific).

**W-2 employee permanent disallowance:**
The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025) permanently repeals the miscellaneous itemized deduction for unreimbursed employee expenses, including business vehicle costs, for W-2 employees. This is not a temporary measure. The only path to a vehicle deduction for an employee is an employer accountable plan reimbursement.

**State law may differ:**
Several states do not conform to federal vehicle deduction rules or mileage rates. California, for example, historically follows different conformity rules for depreciation and bonus depreciation. If you operate in a state with a personal income tax, confirm the state treatment of your vehicle deduction with your advisor.

**Bonus depreciation and the One Big Beautiful Bill Act:**
Bonus depreciation was restored to 100% for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. This applies to vehicles subject to the luxury auto limits -- meaning you can elect 100% bonus depreciation in year one on a qualifying passenger automobile, but your total first-year deduction is still capped at $20,300 (the Year 1 luxury auto limit with bonus depreciation). For vehicles with a GVWR above 6,000 lbs that are not subject to the luxury auto limits, bonus depreciation may allow a full first-year write-off up to the vehicle's entire cost basis, subject to the heavy-SUV Section 179 cap of $32,000 for SUVs.

**Commuting disallowance is absolute:**
No business structure or deduction method eliminates the commuting disallowance. Miles between home and a fixed regular place of business are always personal. However, if your home is your principal place of business (and you qualify for the home office deduction under IRC Section 280A), trips from your home office to a client or job site are fully deductible business miles.

**Lease vs. ownership:**
If you lease a vehicle, you use the actual expense method or the standard mileage rate (subject to the first-year election rule for the lease term). If you use actual expenses on a leased vehicle and the vehicle's fair market value at lease inception exceeded certain IRS thresholds, you may be required to include an annual income inclusion amount (a lease inclusion amount per IRS tables in Rev. Proc. 2026-15). This prevents a double benefit from leasing a high-value vehicle. Confirm lease-specific treatment with your advisor.

**Vehicle placed in service mid-year:**
If you purchased or began using a vehicle for business during 2026 (not January 1), your depreciation deduction is not prorated by months -- instead, IRS depreciation conventions (typically the "half-year convention") apply. Your advisor's software handles this, but be sure to provide the exact date of first business use.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Logging commuting miles as business miles.** The commute from home to your regular office is never deductible. Many people accidentally include it. Only trips from a qualified home office, or trips between two business locations, or trips to clients or temporary work sites are deductible.

2. **Reconstructing the mileage log at year-end.** The IRS does not consider a year-end reconstruction to be contemporaneous. Under audit, a reconstructed log is likely to be partially or fully disallowed. Maintain the log weekly.

3. **Not logging total miles.** You need total miles (business + personal + commuting) to calculate your business-use percentage. Forgetting to record the January 1 and December 31 odometer readings is the most common way to lose this number.

4. **Choosing actual expenses in year one and then trying to switch to standard mileage.** This is not permitted. If actual expenses were used in the first year the vehicle was placed in business service, you are locked into actual expenses for that vehicle forever.

5. **Claiming the full vehicle cost as a business deduction on a vehicle with mixed personal and business use.** You can only deduct the business-use percentage of your costs. A vehicle used 60% for business supports a 60% deduction, not 100%.

6. **Exceeding the luxury auto annual caps.** Even with Section 179 or bonus depreciation elections, passenger automobiles (under 6,000 lbs GVWR) are capped at $20,300 in year one (with bonus depreciation). Claiming more than the cap is an error that will trigger adjustment on audit.

7. **Forgetting to track the standard mileage rate's depreciation component.** Each year you use standard mileage, 35 cents per mile reduces your vehicle's tax basis. If you later sell the vehicle, you may owe tax on the gain created by this basis reduction. Keep a running record.

8. **Deducting vehicle costs as a W-2 employee without an accountable plan.** Post-OBBBA, this deduction is permanently disallowed. Work with your employer to establish an accountable plan reimbursement instead.

9. **Ignoring state conformity.** Assuming your state follows federal vehicle depreciation rules without checking. Some states, including California, decouple from bonus depreciation or Section 179 for personal property.

10. **Failing to file Form 4562 when required.** Any owner of more than 5% of a business who uses a vehicle for business must complete the Section B vehicle questions on Form 4562, even when using the standard mileage rate. Omitting this form can trigger correspondence from the IRS.

## Advisor Review Gates

Contact your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

- **Before your vehicle's first year in business service:** Have your advisor help you choose between standard mileage and actual expenses. The year-one choice locks you in; running both calculations before filing is worth the time.
- **Before claiming Section 179 or bonus depreciation on a vehicle:** Confirm the vehicle's GVWR qualifies for the higher deduction, that business use exceeds 50%, and that your entity type does not create complications.
- **If you operate or plan to operate five or more vehicles:** Standard mileage is unavailable. Your advisor should set up an actual-expense tracking system for the fleet.
- **If you use your vehicle for both business and personal purposes and the split is close to 50/50:** Business use at exactly 50% disqualifies Section 179 and bonus depreciation. Have your advisor confirm the percentage calculation before filing.
- **Before switching from standard mileage to actual expenses:** Confirm you are eligible to switch and understand that you will be limited to straight-line depreciation going forward.
- **If you are an S-corp owner whose vehicle is personally owned:** Your advisor must structure the vehicle reimbursement as an accountable plan or the deduction may be lost entirely.
- **If your state imposes an income tax:** Confirm whether your state conforms to the federal standard mileage rate, Section 179 limits, and bonus depreciation rules before computing your state deduction.
- **If you sold a vehicle previously used for business:** The sale may trigger depreciation recapture (ordinary income) and capital gain. Do not report this transaction without advisor guidance.
- **If you use a vehicle for a rental property business:** Vehicle costs related to rental activity are subject to different passive activity rules. Confirm the correct treatment with your advisor.

## Using The Download(s)

**Vehicle Mileage Log**

This is your primary substantiation document. Without a completed, contemporaneous log, the vehicle deduction does not survive an audit.

- **Where in the workflow:** Begin using the log on the first day of 2026 (or the first day you use a vehicle for business during 2026). Do not wait until tax season.
- **How to fill it out:**
  - At the very top: enter the vehicle's year, make, model, VIN, and your odometer reading on January 1 (or first business use date).
  - For every business trip: fill in the date, starting location, destination, business purpose, and miles driven. Be specific about business purpose -- "met with client" is weaker than "reviewed contract with [industry: retail client]."
  - At month-end: total the business miles for the month and carry the running year-to-date total forward.
  - At December 31: enter the final odometer reading.
- **Who fills it out:** The driver of the vehicle. If multiple employees drive a business vehicle, each driver should maintain their own portion of the log or a shared log with their name on each entry.
- **Digital alternative:** The log may be kept on paper or in a spreadsheet or mileage-tracking app, as long as the required elements are captured. A weekly mileage tracking app that exports a report meets IRS requirements if it captures date, destination, purpose, and miles.
- **Bring this to your advisor:** At tax time, provide the completed log alongside your actual expense receipts (if using actual expenses). Your advisor needs the year-end totals: business miles, total miles driven, and odometer start and end readings.

   Verifies: 72.5 cents/mile business rate for 2026; 20.5 cents/mile medical/moving rate; 14 cents/mile charitable rate; first-year election requirement for owned vehicles; fleet restriction (5+ vehicles); lease commitment rule.

2. **IRS Notice 2026-10**
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-10.pdf
   Verifies: All 2026 standard mileage rates; 35 cents/mile depreciation component; $61,700 FAVR maximum automobile cost.

3. **Rev. Proc. 2026-15 (via Journal of Accountancy and Eide Bailly analysis)**
   URL: https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/mar/irs-issues-higher-2026-depreciation-limits-for-passenger-automobiles/
   URL: https://www.eidebailly.com/insights/blogs/2026/3/autodepreciationlimits2026
   Verifies: 2026 luxury auto depreciation caps under Section 280F: Year 1 with bonus depreciation $20,300; Year 1 without $12,300; Year 2 $19,800; Year 3 $11,900; Year 4+ $7,160.

4. **IRS Topic No. 510 -- Business Use of Car**
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc510
   Verifies: Actual expense deductible items (gas, oil, repairs, tires, insurance, registration, depreciation/lease payments); first-year election requirement; commuting disallowance.

5. **IRS Publication 463 (2025) -- Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses**
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463
   Verifies: Record-keeping requirements (date, destination, business purpose, miles); contemporaneous records standard; switching rules between standard mileage and actual expense methods; commuting definition.

6. **Section179.org -- 2026 Vehicle Deduction Limits**
   URL: https://www.section179.org/section_179_vehicle_deductions/
   Verifies: Heavy SUV Section 179 cap of $32,000 for 2026; overall Section 179 limit of $2,560,000; phase-out at $4,090,000.

7. **IRS.gov -- One Big Beautiful Bill provisions (individuals and workers)**
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/one-big-beautiful-bill-provisions-individuals-and-workers
   Verifies: New car loan interest deduction (not related to business vehicle deduction, but confirms the bill's scope); permanent employee expense disallowance context.

8. **Current Federal Tax Developments -- IRS Notice 2026-10 Analysis**
   URL: https://www.currentfederaltaxdevelopments.com/blog/2025/12/29/irs-notice-2026-10-and-the-2026-standard-mileage-rates
   Verifies: 35 cents/mile depreciation component; OBBBA impact on employee mileage deductions.

9. **National Tax Tools -- Mileage Deduction Guide 2026**
   URL: https://nationaltaxtools.com/guides/mileage-deduction/
   Verifies: Fleet restriction (5+ vehicles simultaneously); vehicles-for-hire restriction; OBBBA Section 70110 permanent employee disallowance; switching lock-in if actual expenses used in year one.

10. **Treasury Regulation Section 1.62-2(g)**
    Verifies: Accountable plan safe harbor periods: substantiate within 60 days, return excess within 120 days.
