# Augusta Rule Meeting Packet

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The Augusta Rule is a nickname for a specific provision in the U.S. tax code -- IRC Section 280A(g) -- that lets a homeowner rent their personal residence to their own business for up to 14 days per calendar year and pay zero federal income tax on that rental income. At the same time, the business deducts the rent payment as an ordinary business expense, reducing the income that flows through to the owner's personal return. The result is a legal shift of dollars from taxable business profit to tax-free personal cash, using a meeting or planning event at your home as the business purpose. This playbook focuses on building and maintaining a complete meeting packet -- the paper trail that makes every Augusta Rule event defensible in an IRS examination.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is designed for business owners who:

- Own (do not rent) their primary residence or a second home they use personally
- Operate their business through a legally separate entity: an S corporation, a C corporation, a partnership, or a multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership or corporation
- Hold actual business meetings, planning sessions, training events, strategy retreats, or similar gatherings that could legitimately take place at a comparable commercial venue
- Have reviewed the Augusta Rule basics and are now ready to implement meeting-by-meeting documentation

You do not need a large or expensive home. The strategy works for any home that could plausibly substitute for a hotel conference room or executive meeting suite in your area.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not use this strategy if any of the following apply:

- **You operate as a sole proprietor or a single-member LLC that has not elected S-corp or C-corp taxation.** A sole proprietor is the same taxpayer as the business, so there is no separate entity to pay you rent. The strategy requires two distinct taxpayers: you personally and your business entity. If you file business income on Schedule C (Form 1040) without a separate entity, this does not work.
- **You have already rented your home to this business for 14 or more days during the calendar year.** The 14-day ceiling is all-or-nothing. Crossing into day 15 eliminates the income exclusion for the entire year, not just the extra days.
- **The meetings you plan to hold are personal events with a thin business label.** Family gatherings, holiday parties, or social events dressed up as strategy sessions will not survive IRS scrutiny.
- **You claim a home office deduction for the same space on the same days.** There is meaningful overlap risk between the home office deduction (which requires exclusive and regular business use of a dedicated space) and Augusta Rule rentals. Use them for different spaces within the home or seek guidance before combining them.
- **Your home is already your principal place of business.** Renting a space you already claim as your primary business location creates an inconsistency the IRS can use to challenge both deductions.
- **Your state does not conform to the federal IRC Section 280A(g) exclusion.** Most states follow federal treatment, but you must verify for your specific state before relying on the income exclusion at the state level (see the Caveats section).

## Key Terms

**IRC Section 280A(g):** The subsection of the Internal Revenue Code that creates the Augusta Rule. The exact statutory text reads: "Notwithstanding any other provision of this section or section 183, if a dwelling unit is used during the taxable year by the taxpayer as a residence and such dwelling unit is actually rented for less than 15 days during the taxable year, then -- (1) no deduction otherwise allowable under this chapter because of the rental use of such dwelling unit shall be allowed, and (2) the income derived from such use for the taxable year shall not be included in the gross income of such taxpayer under section 61."

**Gross income exclusion:** The rental income is not included in gross income (total income before deductions) on your personal return. You do not report it anywhere on Form 1040 unless your business issues you a 1099-MISC for the payment -- in that rare case, you would show the income on Schedule E and then show an offsetting Section 280A(g) adjustment to bring the net taxable amount to zero.

**IRC Section 162:** The provision that requires a business expense to be "ordinary and necessary" to be deductible. Even though the homeowner's income exclusion is automatic under Section 280A(g), the business's deduction must independently qualify under Section 162 as a legitimate, reasonable business expense. The IRS and Tax Court apply heightened skepticism to transactions between related parties (you and your own company), so documentation and pricing must be defensible.

**Fair market value (FMV) rental rate:** The price a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither under compulsion, would agree to for comparable meeting space in your area. For Augusta Rule purposes, this means the rate a comparable hotel conference room, executive meeting suite, or private event venue would charge for the same capacity, duration, and amenities. Residential short-term rental rates (Airbnb, VRBO) are generally not appropriate comparables for a business meeting context; commercial venue quotes are.

**Rental agreement:** A written contract executed before the first rental day that specifies the parties (you and your business), the property address, the specific dates, the space being rented, the rental rate per day, and the payment terms.

**Corporate resolution:** A formal written decision recorded by the governing body of your business (the board of directors of a corporation, or the members/managers of an LLC) authorizing the rental arrangement before it begins.

**Meeting minutes:** A contemporaneous written record of what was discussed and decided at each business meeting, including attendees, agenda items covered, and any action items or decisions.

**Day count log:** A running record of how many Augusta Rule rental days you have used in the current calendar year, updated after each event. This is the control that prevents you from accidentally crossing the 14-day limit.

**Sinopoli v. Commissioner (TC Memo 2023-105):** A 2023 U.S. Tax Court decision in which the court disallowed most of an S corporation's Augusta Rule rent deductions because (1) the rates charged far exceeded what comparable local meeting space actually cost and (2) the business meeting records were inconsistent and not credible. The court allowed only $500 per substantiated meeting day. This case is the most important cautionary example in the Augusta Rule space.

**Schedule E (Form 1040):** The personal income tax form used to report rental income and expenses. Under Section 280A(g), Augusta Rule income is excluded and does not appear on Schedule E in the standard case.

**Form 1120-S:** The annual income tax return filed by an S corporation. The rent paid to you under the Augusta Rule is deducted on this form as an ordinary business expense.

**Pass-through taxation:** The way S corporations and partnerships work: business income and deductions flow through to the owner's personal return. The Augusta Rule rent deduction reduces business income before it passes through to your Form 1040, which is why it produces a real tax savings on your personal return.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before starting:

1. **Your entity type.** Confirm whether your business files as an S corporation (Form 1120-S), C corporation (Form 1120), partnership (Form 1065), or multi-member LLC taxed as one of those. If you are unsure, check your most recent business tax return or the EIN confirmation letter from the IRS.
2. **Your remaining Augusta Rule days for the year.** If you have already used the strategy earlier in the year, confirm how many days remain. If this is your first event, you have 14 days available.
3. **The business events you plan to hold.** List each proposed meeting or event: date, duration (full day or partial), topic, planned attendees, and a brief description of what business will be conducted.
4. **Comparable venue quotes for your area.** Before setting your rental rate, get written quotes or screenshot documented rates from at least three comparable commercial venues near your home: hotel conference rooms, executive suites, coworking event spaces, or private event homes marketed for corporate use. The goal is to find what the market actually charges for equivalent space and capacity.
5. **Property details.** The square footage of the space you will be renting, the number of people it can accommodate, any amenities relevant to pricing (AV equipment, catering kitchen, outdoor space used for the event, private parking).
6. **Your business bank account information.** The rent payment must flow from the business bank account to your personal account. Cash payments are not acceptable documentation.

## Required Documents And Records

Build a dedicated folder (physical or digital) for each calendar year you use the Augusta Rule. Keep every document in the list below. The IRS generally has three years from the filing date to audit a return, but the statute of limitations extends to six years if you omit gross income exceeding 25% of the gross income stated on your return (IRC Section 6501(e)(1)(A)); retain Augusta Rule records for at least seven years.

**Before the first rental event:**

- [ ] Written rental agreement (signed by both you personally and an authorized representative of the business, executed before the first rental day)
- [ ] Corporate resolution or LLC member consent authorizing the rental arrangement
- [ ] Fair market value analysis memo: documents the three comparable venue quotes you gathered, the methodology you used, and the rate you selected
- [ ] Copies or screenshots of each comparable venue quote, dated

**For each rental event:**

- [ ] Written meeting agenda prepared in advance
- [ ] Attendee list with each person's name and business role
- [ ] Meeting minutes or notes documenting what was actually discussed and decided
- [ ] Photographs of the meeting setup (optional but strongly recommended)
- [ ] Calendar invitation or scheduling confirmation showing the event date and participants

**After each payment:**

- [ ] Bank statement or ACH confirmation showing the business account transfer to your personal account
- [ ] Business bookkeeping entry categorizing the expense as rent
- [ ] Updated day count log showing total days used for the year

**At year end:**

- [ ] Day count summary for the year (total days used, dates, and meeting topics)
- [ ] Reconciliation confirming the total rent paid equals daily rate times number of days

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Before Any Meetings Occur

**Step 1: Confirm your entity qualifies.**
Check your most recent business tax return. If it is Form 1120-S (S corporation), Form 1120 (C corporation), or Form 1065 (partnership), you are eligible. If your business income flows through Schedule C on your personal return without a separate entity, stop and consult your advisor about entity options before proceeding.

**Step 2: Verify your remaining Augusta Rule days for the year.**
Review any prior Augusta Rule rental agreements or records from earlier in the calendar year. Confirm the total days already used. You may proceed only if the sum of days already used plus the days you plan to rent remains at 13 or fewer (keeping you within the 14-day ceiling with a safety buffer). If you need to use all 14 days, count carefully; cross into day 15 and the entire year's income exclusion is lost.

**Step 3: Research comparable commercial venue rates in your area.**
Search online or call at least three commercial venues near your home: hotel conference rooms, executive suites, and coworking or private event spaces designed for business gatherings. Ask for or record the rate for a day or half-day rental for comparable capacity. Take dated screenshots of any online listings. Do not use Airbnb or VRBO rates as primary comparables -- those reflect residential hospitality pricing, not commercial meeting pricing, and using them as a benchmark can result in a disallowed deduction if the IRS compares your rate to what the comparable commercial venues actually charge.

**Step 4: Document your fair market value analysis.**
Write a brief memo (one to two pages is sufficient) explaining the space you are renting, its capacity, relevant amenities, and the three comparable rates you gathered. State the rate you selected and why it falls within the market range. Date and sign the memo before the first rental event. Keep this in your Augusta Rule folder.

**Step 5: Draft and execute the rental agreement.**
The rental agreement must be in writing and signed before the first rental day. It must include:
- Full legal name of the homeowner (you) as landlord
- Full legal name of the business entity as tenant
- Property address and a description of the specific space being rented (for example: "the 400-square-foot dining room and adjacent living area")
- Each rental date (list dates individually or as a schedule attached to the agreement)
- Rate per day
- Total rent due
- Payment method and timing
- A brief statement of the business purpose (for example: "corporate strategy sessions and board meetings")
- Signatures of both parties

**Step 6: Pass a corporate resolution (S-corps and C-corps).**
The board of directors or managing officers of your corporation should record a brief written resolution authorizing the rental. It should state that the corporation has approved renting your home for business meetings, the rate, and the dates. For LLCs, a member consent in writing serves the same purpose. Date this document and keep it with the rental agreement.

### For Each Meeting Event

**Step 7: Prepare a written agenda before the meeting.**
The agenda should identify the date, location, start and end time, expected attendees, and the specific business topics to be covered. Generic descriptions ("business discussion") are weaker than specific ones ("Q3 marketing budget review, new vendor contract approvals, 2026 health insurance options"). Prepare the agenda in advance -- do not backfill it after the fact.

**Step 8: Hold the actual business meeting.**
The meeting must be a real business event. Conduct the agenda items listed, involve the listed attendees, and make actual business decisions or produce actionable next steps. Mixing personal social time with business time on the same day is not automatically fatal, but the business purpose must be genuine and documentable.

**Step 9: Record meeting minutes or notes during or immediately after the meeting.**
Minutes do not need to be long or formal. They must capture: who attended, what was discussed for each agenda item, and what was decided or assigned as a follow-up action. Date and sign them on the day of the meeting or within 24 hours. Reconstructed records created weeks or months later are far weaker in an audit.

**Step 10: Photograph the meeting setup (recommended).**
A few photographs of the space set up for the meeting -- chairs arranged, materials on the table, attendees present -- create contemporaneous visual evidence that the event actually occurred as described.

**Step 11: Update your day count log.**
Add the number of days used for this event to your running total. If this event plus prior events brings you to 14 days, do not schedule any further Augusta Rule rentals for the remainder of the calendar year.

### After the Meeting

**Step 12: Issue the rent payment from the business account.**
The business must pay you by check or bank transfer from the business bank account to your personal account. The memo line of the check or the ACH transfer description should reference "rent -- [date of meeting] -- Section 280A(g)." Pay within a reasonable time after the meeting (within the same month is appropriate practice). Do not use cash.

**Step 13: Record the expense in the business books.**
In your bookkeeping software, categorize the payment as "Rent expense" or a similar category. Attach the rental agreement or the relevant meeting documents to the transaction record for easy retrieval. Your accountant will report this deduction on your business tax return (Form 1120-S Line 11 for rents, or confirmed with your advisor for the applicable line on your entity's return) as an ordinary and necessary business expense under IRC Section 162.

**Step 14: File the Augusta Rule Meeting Packet documents.**
Place the agenda, attendee list, minutes, photographs, and payment confirmation in your Augusta Rule folder under a subfolder for this event date. This is the audit file. Never discard these during the retention period.

### At Year End

**Step 15: Reconcile and confirm total days used.**
Before filing your business tax return, verify that the total rental days for the year are 14 or fewer. Confirm that the total rent paid matches the rental agreement rate times the number of days.

**Step 16: Confirm income exclusion on your personal return.**
If your business did not issue you a Form 1099-MISC or 1099-NEC for the rental payments, you do not report this income anywhere on your personal return. The exclusion under Section 280A(g) means the income simply does not appear. If a 1099 was issued (uncommon, but possible if your bookkeeping system auto-generates them), report the gross amount on Schedule E and show an equal offsetting deduction labeled "IRC Section 280A(g) exclusion" so the net taxable amount is zero. Consult your advisor on the proper mechanics for your software.

**Step 17: Store the full year's Augusta Rule folder.**
Archive the complete year's folder and retain it for seven years from the return due date for that tax year.

## Timing And Deadlines

**Calendar year only.** The 14-day limit is measured per calendar year (January 1 through December 31). Days do not carry over. You start fresh each January 1.

**Execute the rental agreement before the first rental day.** This is not optional. A rental agreement signed after the meeting has already happened is a reconstruction, which is substantially weaker in an IRS examination. The agreement must precede the event.

**Meeting minutes within 24 hours.** Minutes should be dated and completed on the day of the meeting or the following day. The longer you wait, the more a court or examiner will question whether the meeting actually occurred as described.

**Payments within the same month.** Issue the rent payment from the business account within the calendar month in which the meeting occurred. Very long delays between the meeting and the payment create questions about whether the transaction was real.

**Year-end cutoff.** Any meeting you want to count for the current tax year must occur and be paid before December 31 of that year.

**Retain records for seven years.** The standard IRS audit window is three years from the return filing date. The IRS has six years if you omit gross income exceeding 25% of the gross income stated on your return, per IRC Section 6501(e)(1)(A). Retain all Augusta Rule documentation for seven years to cover the longer window.

**State-level timing.** If your state has its own income reporting rules that do not conform to the federal exclusion, state-level reporting may have separate deadlines. Confirm with your advisor for your specific state.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity requirement is absolute.** A sole proprietorship and a single-member LLC that has not elected corporate taxation are the same taxpayer as the owner. There is no separate entity to rent from you, so the strategy does not produce any tax benefit. You must have a distinct legal entity (S-corp, C-corp, partnership, or multi-member LLC with a filing entity return) to use this strategy.

**S-corp payroll does not affect this strategy directly.** Reasonable compensation requirements for S-corp owners apply to wages, which are separate from Augusta Rule rent. The rent payment is not wages and does not go through payroll. Do not run the Augusta Rule payment through payroll processing.

**California:** California generally conforms to federal tax treatment but has a history of diverging from specific federal provisions in certain circumstances. Confirm with your advisor whether California will respect the Section 280A(g) exclusion for your situation and whether any California-specific rental income reporting applies.

**Other states:** Most states that impose individual income tax follow the federal definition of gross income and therefore honor the Section 280A(g) exclusion. However, some states have their own conformity dates or specific non-conforming provisions. Verify state-level treatment before relying on the income exclusion at the state level.

**Local occupancy or lodging taxes:** Some counties and municipalities impose short-term rental or occupancy taxes on residential property rentals, even for business-purpose events. Check your local jurisdiction's rules.

**The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025):** This legislation did not alter IRC Section 280A(g). The Augusta Rule is unchanged for 2026 filings. However, monitor for any future legislative changes that could modify this provision.

**QBI interaction:** The Augusta Rule rent deduction reduces the business's net income, which also reduces the income base for the Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction if your business qualifies for one. This is a normal consequence of any business deduction and is not a reason to avoid the strategy, but it is a number your advisor should factor into your overall tax projection.

**Home office interaction:** If you already claim a home office deduction for a dedicated portion of your home, using the same space on the same days for Augusta Rule rental creates an inconsistency: the home office deduction requires exclusive business use for the owner's own business, while the Augusta Rule rental is a transaction with a separate entity. Using a different room, or using the home office space on days when it is not being used as a home office, may resolve the conflict, but this is a fact-specific question for your advisor.

**Related-party scrutiny.** The IRS and Tax Court apply heightened scrutiny to transactions between a business owner and their own company. The Sinopoli case (TC Memo 2023-105) is the clearest warning: if your rate is not supportable by arm's-length market comparables, or if your meeting records are thin, the deduction can be reduced to whatever the court determines is a fair market rate for the meetings that are actually documented. This makes the meeting packet documents in this lesson the single most important element of compliance.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Not having a written rental agreement before the first meeting.**
Verbal agreements are not sufficient. Agreements signed after the event raise questions about whether the arrangement was agreed to in advance or invented to explain a payment already made.

**2. Setting the rental rate without comparable commercial venue research.**
Using a round number, or backing into the rate from a desired tax savings amount, is exactly what the IRS looks for. Your rate must be grounded in documented market data.

**3. Using residential short-term rental rates (Airbnb, VRBO) as the benchmark.**
A business meeting venue is priced differently from an overnight vacation stay. Hotel conference rooms and executive suites are the correct comparables.

**4. Letting the day count reach 15 or more.**
Crossing day 15 eliminates the entire year's income exclusion, not just the income from the extra days. Keep a running log and stop at 14.

**5. Holding the meeting but skipping the minutes.**
Meeting minutes are the primary evidence that a real business event occurred. Attending families, no written agenda, no notes, and no decisions recorded are the hallmarks of a personal event with a business label.

**6. Paying from the wrong account.**
The rent payment must come from the business's bank account. A transfer from your personal account to another personal account does not demonstrate a genuine business transaction.

**7. Paying in cash.**
Cash payments leave no traceable paper trail. Always pay by check or bank transfer.

**8. Reconstructing records at tax time.**
Creating agendas, minutes, and agreements after the fact -- especially right before filing -- is far weaker than contemporaneous documentation and can be treated as fabrication in an examination.

**9. Forgetting to exclude the income from the personal return correctly.**
The income does not go on Schedule E in the standard case. Reporting it and not claiming the exclusion means you pay unnecessary tax. If a 1099 was issued, handle it with an offsetting Section 280A(g) adjustment on Schedule E.

**10. Assuming all states automatically follow federal treatment.**
State conformity is not universal. Check your state before filing.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before proceeding if any of the following are true:

- **You are not certain your business entity qualifies.** Entity type determines eligibility. If you are unsure whether your LLC is taxed as a sole proprietor, partnership, or corporation, get confirmation before signing any rental agreement.
- **You have already used the Augusta Rule this calendar year and are unsure of the day count.** A miscounting error that pushes you to 15 days destroys the entire year's exclusion.
- **Your planned rental rate is above the median of your comparable venue research.** Rates above market are the most common reason for disallowed deductions. Your advisor should review the comparables and rate before you finalize the rental agreement.
- **You claim a home office deduction and want to use the same property for Augusta Rule rentals.** The interaction between these two provisions is not settled for all fact patterns. Get a specific opinion before combining them.
- **You operate in California or a state with known non-conformity to federal income exclusions.** State-level income tax consequences need to be confirmed.
- **The meetings you plan to hold involve only you and immediate family members.** Family-only meetings without arm's-length business purposes draw additional scrutiny.
- **Your S-corp has not yet set up reasonable compensation payroll.** Reasonable compensation requirements are separate from this strategy but matter for overall S-corp compliance. If payroll is not in order, address it before adding Augusta Rule transactions.
- **You receive a Form 1099-MISC or 1099-NEC from your business for the rental payment.** The tax filing treatment for this scenario requires specific handling on Schedule E. Confirm with your advisor before filing.

## Using The Download(s)

**Augusta Rule Meeting Packet** -- This is the core document for this lesson.

The Meeting Packet download is a pre-formatted, fillable set of forms that covers the complete documentation cycle for each Augusta Rule rental event. Use it as follows:

- **Before each meeting:** Complete the Rental Agreement section with the specific date, space description, rate, and business purpose. Have both parties sign it before the meeting date.
- **Before each meeting:** Complete the Meeting Agenda section with the planned attendees, start and end times, and specific agenda items.
- **On the day of the meeting:** Use the Attendee Sign-In sheet to capture every participant's name and their role in the business.
- **During or immediately after the meeting:** Complete the Meeting Minutes section with what was discussed for each agenda item and what decisions or action items resulted.
- **After payment:** Attach the payment confirmation (bank screenshot or check copy) to the packet and note the payment date, amount, and method.
- **Ongoing:** Use the Day Count Log at the front of the packet to track all rental days for the calendar year. Update it after every event.

Keep the completed packet for each meeting event in your year's Augusta Rule folder. Do not modify or backfill any section after the meeting date. If your business issues a 1099 for the payments, keep a copy of that form attached to the annual summary page of the packet.

One completed packet per meeting event is the goal. Twelve meetings require twelve completed packets. Each packet must be independently substantiated.

Confirm the following with your advisor before signing any rental agreement, holding any meeting, or issuing any payment under this strategy:
