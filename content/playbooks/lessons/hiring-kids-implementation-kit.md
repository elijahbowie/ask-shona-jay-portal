# Hiring Kids Implementation Kit

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

When a business owner employs their own children in a legitimate capacity, the wages paid are deductible business expenses that reduce the owner's taxable income -- and in the right business structure, those wages avoid Social Security, Medicare, and federal unemployment taxes entirely. The child pays little or no federal income tax on the earnings because their standard deduction offsets most or all of the income, and the wages create an earned income foundation that allows the child to contribute to a Roth IRA. Done correctly, this strategy shifts income from the parent's high tax bracket into the child's zero or low bracket, saving real dollars while building the child's financial future. This kit walks you through every setup step, payroll obligation, record-keeping requirement, and compliance rule for tax year 2026.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is most valuable for:

- **Sole proprietors (Schedule C filers):** You receive the maximum tax benefit. Wages paid to your child under age 18 are exempt from Social Security and Medicare taxes (FICA), and wages paid to a child under age 21 are also exempt from federal unemployment tax (FUTA). Consult your advisor about your state's unemployment tax rules.
- **Husband-and-wife partnerships where both partners are the child's parents:** The same FICA and FUTA exemptions apply as for sole proprietors, because each partner is a parent of the child. Confirm your partnership agreement lists both parents as partners.
- **Single-member LLCs taxed as sole proprietorships:** Treated the same as sole proprietors for this purpose. The FICA and FUTA exemptions apply.
- **Business owners who have genuine, age-appropriate work their children can perform:** The work must be real and documented. Examples: filing, photography, social media content, stuffing envelopes, data entry, modeling products, cleaning the office, creating graphics, answering emails under supervision.
- **Parents with children who have earned income for Roth IRA planning purposes:** Even modest wages create IRA contribution eligibility for the child.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **S-corporations and C-corporations:** If your business is incorporated as an S-corp or C-corp (or a single-member LLC that has elected S-corp tax treatment), the FICA and FUTA exemptions do NOT apply. Wages paid to your child through a corporation are fully subject to Social Security, Medicare, and FUTA taxes at the same rates as any other employee, regardless of the child's age. The income-shifting benefit still exists in theory, but the payroll tax savings disappear. Discuss the cost-benefit with your advisor before implementing through a corporation.
- **Partnerships where not all partners are the child's parents:** If one partner is not a parent of the child, the FICA and FUTA exemptions do not apply. Confirm the partnership structure with your advisor.
- **Situations where the child has no real work to perform:** Paying your child for doing nothing, or for tasks they did not actually do, is not a tax strategy -- it is tax fraud. The wages must reflect genuine services at reasonable market rates.
- **Very young children (generally under age 7):** While federal tax law does not set a minimum age for employing your child, the IRS scrutinizes wages paid to very young children because the reasonableness of the work and compensation is harder to document. Work with your advisor if the child is under 7.
- **Owners seeking to deduct wages paid for household chores:** Ordinary household chores (cleaning their own room, washing family dishes, mowing the family lawn) are not business services and are not deductible. The work must serve the business, not the household.
- **Businesses without a payroll system:** You cannot pay your child informally in cash and skip W-2 reporting. A proper payroll setup is required. If you are unwilling to establish payroll and file required forms, do not start.
- **Owners in community property states with complex ownership structures:** Community property state rules can affect how wages to a child are characterized. Confirm the application with your advisor.

## Key Terms

**FICA:** Federal Insurance Contributions Act. The combined Social Security tax (6.2% employee + 6.2% employer = 12.4% total) and Medicare tax (1.45% employee + 1.45% employer = 2.9% total) that is normally withheld from employee wages and matched by the employer. For tax year 2026, the Social Security wage base is $184,500 -- meaning the 6.2% rate applies only to the first $184,500 of wages; Medicare has no cap.

**FUTA:** Federal Unemployment Tax Act. The federal unemployment tax the employer pays (not the employee) on the first $7,000 of wages paid to each employee per year. The standard FUTA rate is 6.0%, but employers who pay into their state unemployment fund on time receive a credit of up to 5.4%, reducing the effective federal rate to 0.6%. Wages paid to a child under age 21 employed by a parent in a sole proprietorship or qualifying parent partnership are exempt from FUTA.

**SUTA:** State Unemployment Tax Act. Your state's unemployment tax, which varies by state and employer experience rating. SUTA may still apply to wages paid to your child even if FUTA does not -- check your state's rules.

**Schedule C:** The IRS tax form (part of Form 1040) on which sole proprietors report business income and deductions. Wages you pay your child are deducted on Schedule C, Line 26 (Wages), reducing your net self-employment income.

**Schedule SE:** The IRS form on which sole proprietors calculate self-employment tax (the 15.3% rate -- 12.4% for Social Security plus 2.9% for Medicare -- applied to 92.35% of net self-employment income). When you shift income to your child's wages, you reduce the net income on Schedule C, which also reduces your Schedule SE self-employment tax liability.

**Self-employment tax:** The tax a sole proprietor pays in lieu of the employee and employer halves of FICA. The effective rate is 15.3% on net self-employment income up to the Social Security wage base, and 2.9% above that. Every dollar of wages paid to your child reduces your net self-employment income and therefore reduces your self-employment tax, in addition to reducing your income tax.

**Standard deduction (dependent child):** The amount of income a child can earn before owing any federal income tax. For 2026, a dependent child's standard deduction is the greater of (a) $1,350 or (b) the child's earned income plus $450 -- but not more than the regular standard deduction for a single filer ($16,100 for 2026). In practical terms: if your child earns $15,650 or less, their standard deduction equals their wages plus $450, offsetting all earned income. If your child earns between $15,650 and $16,100, their deduction is capped at $16,100.

**Earned income:** Wages, salaries, and other pay received for actual work performed. This is distinct from unearned income (interest, dividends, capital gains). The FICA and FUTA exemptions apply to wages (earned income). A child's earned income from the family business also creates IRA contribution eligibility.

**Kiddie tax (Form 8615):** A rule that taxes a child's unearned income (interest, dividends, capital gains) above $2,700 (for 2026) at the parent's marginal tax rate instead of the child's lower rate. The kiddie tax applies only to unearned income -- it does NOT apply to wages. Your child's wages from the family business are taxed at the child's own (lower) rate.

**Reasonable compensation:** The wage amount that would be paid in an arm's-length transaction for the same services -- in other words, what you would pay a non-family employee to do the same work. Under IRC Section 162(a) and Treasury Regulation 1.162-7, compensation is deductible only if it is both for actual services rendered and reasonable in amount. Paying your 10-year-old $150 per hour to "consult" is not reasonable; paying $12 to $15 per hour for age-appropriate work is.

**Form W-2:** The annual Wage and Tax Statement an employer gives each employee. You must furnish a W-2 to your child by February 1, 2027 (for wages paid in 2026), even if no income tax was withheld because earnings were below the filing threshold. January 31, 2027 falls on a Sunday, so the next business day -- Monday, February 1, 2027 -- is the deadline for both furnishing W-2s to employees and filing Copy A with the SSA.

**Form W-3:** The transmittal form sent to the Social Security Administration (SSA) along with copies of all Forms W-2. Due February 1, 2027 (for 2026 wages). Because January 31, 2027 falls on a Sunday, the deadline shifts to the next business day, Monday, February 1, 2027 -- the same date as the employee-copy deadline.

**Form W-4:** Employee's Withholding Certificate. Your child completes this form when hired. It tells you how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. Because a child's annual wages will likely be below the standard deduction, the child may claim "Exempt" on Line 4c (no withholding needed) if they expect to owe no federal income tax. Review eligibility annually.

**Form 941:** Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return. Most employers use this form to report wages paid and taxes withheld each quarter. For wages paid to your child in a sole proprietorship where FICA is exempt, you will report the wages but will not owe Social Security or Medicare on those wages. Quarterly due dates for 2026: Q1 April 30, Q2 July 31, Q3 November 2 (October 31 falls on a Saturday, so the due date shifts to the next business day, Monday, November 2, 2026), Q4 January 31, 2027.

**Form 944:** Employer's Annual Federal Tax Return. A simplified alternative to Form 941 for very small employers whose annual employment tax liability is $1,000 or less. You must receive IRS written notification to use Form 944 instead of 941. If you qualify, Form 944 is due January 31 of the following year.

**Form 940:** Employer's Annual Federal Unemployment (FUTA) Tax Return. Due January 31 of the following year. If your child's wages are FUTA-exempt (under age 21 in a sole proprietorship or qualifying partnership), you generally do not owe FUTA on those wages and may not need to file Form 940. Confirm with your advisor.

**EIN (Employer Identification Number):** A nine-digit number the IRS assigns to businesses for payroll and tax reporting. If you do not already have one, apply free at IRS.gov/ein. Required before you can run payroll or file employment tax returns.

**EFTPS:** Electronic Federal Tax Payment System. The free IRS online system (eftps.gov) for making all federal tax deposits electronically. Employers are required to deposit employment taxes electronically; paper checks are not accepted for deposits.

**Roth IRA:** A type of individual retirement account to which contributions are made with after-tax dollars, and qualified distributions in retirement are tax-free. A child with earned income can contribute to a Roth IRA. For 2026, the annual IRA contribution limit is $7,500, but contributions cannot exceed the child's earned income for the year (if the child earned $3,000, the maximum contribution is $3,000).

**I-9 (Employment Eligibility Verification):** A federal form (USCIS Form I-9) that every employer must complete for every new employee to verify identity and authorization to work in the United States. Required for your child just as for any other employee.

**IRC Section 3121(b)(3)(A):** The Internal Revenue Code provision that exempts from FICA wages paid by a parent to a child under age 18 employed in the parent's trade or business conducted as a sole proprietorship or as a qualifying partnership.

**IRC Section 3306(c)(5):** The Internal Revenue Code provision that exempts from FUTA wages paid by a parent to a child under age 21 in a sole proprietorship or qualifying parent partnership.

**IRC Section 162:** The Code provision authorizing the deduction for ordinary and necessary business expenses, including wages paid to employees. Your child's wages must satisfy Section 162 to be deductible: they must be for actual services, and the amount must be reasonable.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before setting up payroll for your child:

1. **Your business entity type and tax classification:** Sole proprietorship (Schedule C), single-member LLC taxed as sole proprietor, partnership (with both parents as partners), S-corp, or C-corp. The FICA and FUTA exemptions depend on this. Confirm with your advisor.
2. **Your EIN:** If you do not have one, you will need to apply before running payroll. Apply free at IRS.gov/ein.
3. **Each child's full legal name, Social Security Number (SSN), date of birth, and home address:** Required for W-2 and payroll records.
4. **State(s) in which you operate:** State income tax withholding, SUTA, and state child labor laws vary. Your state may have its own exemptions or requirements.
5. **The specific tasks the child will perform:** Write these down in a job description before the first paycheck is issued. Use the "Hiring Kids Job Description Template" download to draft this.
6. **Market wage for those tasks:** Research what a non-family employee would be paid for the same work (for example, check local job boards for comparable positions). Document this research.
7. **Planned hours and pay frequency:** Weekly, biweekly, or monthly. How many hours per week will the child work?
8. **Whether you already have a payroll system in place:** If not, factor in time to set one up (payroll software or a payroll service) before the first pay date.
9. **Whether the child has other earned income in 2026:** Affects W-4 withholding and IRA contribution planning.
10. **Whether you intend to open a Roth IRA for the child:** Affects how much you target in annual wages.

## Required Documents And Records

**Before the first paycheck -- set up documents:**

- Written job description for each child (use the Hiring Kids Job Description Template download)
- Signed Form W-4 from the child (even minors; a parent signs alongside for very young children)
- Completed USCIS Form I-9 (Employment Eligibility Verification) for each child
- Written offer letter or simple employment agreement describing title, duties, start date, and hourly rate (use the Simple Management Agreement Template download)
- Proof of wage research: printout or screenshot showing comparable pay rates for similar work

**During employment -- ongoing records:**

- Timesheets signed by the child and reviewed/approved by you (use the Sample Employee Time Sheet download). Record: date, start time, end time, total hours, task performed.
- Work product records where possible: saved photos, files, printed flyers, screenshots of completed tasks. These prove the work happened.
- Payroll records for each pay period: gross wages, any income tax withheld, net pay, payment date, and payment method (check number or bank transfer reference).
- Bank records showing actual payment: pay your child by check or direct deposit -- never informally in cash without documentation.

**Annually -- tax and reporting documents:**

- Form W-2 for each child, provided to them by February 1, 2027
- Copy of Form W-2 filed with the SSA (with Form W-3 transmittal) by February 1, 2027
- Copies of all quarterly Forms 941 (or annual Form 944) filed during the year
- Copy of Form 940 if applicable (FUTA return)

**Retention period:** Keep all employment tax records for at least 4 years after the filing due date of the return on which you reported the taxes, or the date the taxes were paid, whichever is later.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Pre-Hire Setup

**Step 1: Confirm your entity type qualifies for the FICA exemption.**
Verify with your advisor that your business is organized as a sole proprietorship, a single-member LLC taxed as a sole proprietorship, or a partnership where every partner is a parent of the child. If you operate through an S-corp or C-corp, the FICA/FUTA exemptions do not apply -- ask your advisor whether the income-shifting benefit alone justifies the extra payroll tax cost.

**Step 2: Obtain an EIN if you do not have one.**
Go to IRS.gov/ein and apply online for free. The application takes about 15 minutes. You receive the EIN immediately online. Do not pay any third-party website for this -- the IRS issues EINs at no charge. Write down the EIN; you will need it on all payroll tax forms.

**Step 3: Determine age-appropriate, legitimate job duties for each child.**
Open the "Hiring Kids Job Description Template" download. For each child, draft a written job description that lists: job title, duties (specific tasks, not vague descriptions), required skills or qualities, scheduled hours, and hourly rate. Use the "Kids Payroll Job Description Prompts" download for ideas organized by child age and business type. Use the "Sample Kids Job Descriptions" download to see completed examples.

**Step 4: Set a reasonable hourly wage.**
Research what a non-family employee would earn for the same work in your area. Document this research (save the job postings or salary surveys you looked at). The wage must be what you would actually pay a stranger to do the same job. If you would pay an outside vendor $15/hour to photograph products, pay your child the same. Do not pay $50/hour for tasks worth $12/hour -- the excess will be disallowed by the IRS.

**Step 5: Set up payroll.**
You need a system that can calculate withholding, process payments, generate pay stubs, and produce year-end W-2s. Options include: (a) a payroll software subscription such as Gusto, ADP, or QuickBooks Payroll; (b) a manual payroll ledger if you are comfortable calculating withholding tables from IRS Publication 15-T; or (c) a bookkeeper or payroll service that handles this for you. Confirm your chosen method with your advisor before your first pay date.

**Step 6: Register with EFTPS to make electronic tax deposits.**
Go to eftps.gov and enroll your business. Allow 5 to 7 business days for your PIN to arrive by mail. You will use EFTPS to deposit any income tax withheld from your child's paycheck. (If your child claims Exempt on Form W-4 and owes no federal income tax, and no FICA is owed because of the age and entity exemptions, deposits may not be required -- confirm with your advisor and your payroll system.)

**Step 7: Register for state payroll taxes if required.**
Check your state's Department of Revenue and Department of Labor websites for: state income tax withholding registration, state unemployment insurance (SUTA) registration, and any state-specific new hire reporting requirements. Most states have new hire reporting due within 20 days of the hire date.

### Phase 2: Onboarding the Child

**Step 8: Complete Form I-9 (Employment Eligibility Verification).**
Download the current Form I-9 from USCIS.gov (search "Form I-9"). Complete Section 1 (employee section) with the child; you as the employer complete Section 2 after examining identity documents (typically a U.S. passport, or a state ID plus a Social Security card). For minor children without a driver's license, acceptable documents include a school ID with photo plus a birth certificate. Keep the completed I-9 on file. Do not send it to USCIS.

**Step 9: Have the child (and you) complete Form W-4.**
Download the current Form W-4 from IRS.gov. The child is the employee completing the form. If the child expects to owe zero federal income tax for the year (because annual wages will be less than the standard deduction -- $16,100 for a single filer in 2026), the child may write "Exempt" on Step 4(c) of the W-4, meaning no federal income tax is withheld from each paycheck. Review eligibility each January -- a new W-4 must be filed if claiming Exempt, and Exempt status expires each February 15. For a young child, you as the parent may complete the form alongside the child.

**Step 10: Sign a simple employment agreement or offer letter.**
Use the "Simple Management Agreement Template" download. The document should state: the child's name, job title, job duties (referencing the job description), start date, hourly rate (or flat rate per project), pay schedule (weekly, biweekly, monthly), and supervisor (you). Both you and the child should sign and date it.

### Phase 3: Running Payroll

**Step 11: Track hours worked on a timesheet for every pay period.**
Use the "Sample Employee Time Sheet" download for each pay period. Record: date, start time, end time, break time if any, total hours, and tasks performed that day. The child should sign the timesheet; you should countersign as the employer approving it. File the original signed timesheets.

**Step 12: Calculate and issue payroll.**
For each pay period: multiply hours worked by the hourly rate to get gross wages. Determine whether federal income tax withholding is required (zero if the child claimed Exempt on W-4). No FICA (Social Security or Medicare) withholding applies in a qualifying sole proprietorship for a child under 18. Pay the child by check (note "payroll" and the pay period dates on the memo line) or by direct deposit to a bank account in the child's name. Issue a pay stub showing gross pay, any withholding, and net pay.

**Step 13: Deposit withheld taxes (if any) using EFTPS.**
If you withheld federal income tax (because the child's wages are above the Exempt threshold or the child chose not to claim Exempt), deposit those amounts through EFTPS. New employers are monthly schedule depositors: deposit by the 15th of the month following the month in which the wages were paid. If total employment taxes for a quarter are less than $2,500, you may pay with your Form 941 filing instead of making mid-quarter deposits -- confirm with your advisor.

**Step 14: Report wages quarterly on Form 941 (or annually on Form 944 if you qualify).**
File Form 941 by the quarterly due dates: Q1 by April 30, Q2 by July 31, Q3 by November 2, 2026 (October 31 falls on a Saturday, so the due date shifts to Monday, November 2), Q4 by January 31, 2027. On the form, report total wages paid (including your child's wages) and any income tax withheld. In the FICA section: since your child's wages are exempt from FICA in a qualifying sole proprietorship, you will not include those wages in the Social Security or Medicare wage lines. Your payroll software should handle this calculation if you flag the employee correctly. If you expect annual employment tax liability of $1,000 or less, you can request to file Form 944 instead -- call the IRS at 1-800-829-4933 by April 1, 2026, or submit a written request postmarked by March 15, 2026 (the IRS must confirm in writing before you switch to Form 944).

### Phase 4: Year-End

**Step 15: Prepare and distribute Form W-2 by February 1, 2027.**
Your payroll software or provider will generate Form W-2 for your child. Box 1 (Wages) shows total wages paid. Box 3 and Box 5 (Social Security and Medicare wages) should be zero or blank if FICA was exempt. Box 4 and Box 6 (employee FICA withheld) should be zero. Give the child Copies B, C, and 2 of the W-2 by February 1, 2027 (January 31, 2027 falls on a Sunday; the next business day is Monday, February 1, 2027). Keep Copy D for your records.

**Step 16: File Form W-3 and Copy A of Form W-2 with the Social Security Administration by February 1, 2027.**
The SSA uses W-2 data to track individuals' Social Security earnings records. The deadline to file with SSA is the same as the employee-copy deadline: February 1, 2027 (January 31, 2027 is a Sunday; the next business day is Monday, February 1, 2027). File electronically through the SSA's Business Services Online (BSO) portal (ssa.gov/employer) or through your payroll software. Paper filers who submit fewer than 10 W-2s may file by mail, but electronic filing is recommended.

**Step 17: File Form 940 (FUTA) by January 31, 2027, if required.**
If you paid wages to any employee other than your FUTA-exempt child, you may need to file Form 940. If your child is your only employee and their wages are FUTA-exempt (under age 21 in a qualifying sole proprietorship), consult your advisor about whether Form 940 is required. If you do owe FUTA on any wages, deposit the liability through EFTPS and file the return.

**Step 18: Consider a Roth IRA contribution for the child.**
Once you know the child's total earned income for 2026, calculate how much can be contributed to a Roth IRA. The contribution limit is the lesser of (a) $7,500 or (b) the child's total earned income for the year. A parent or grandparent can make the contribution on the child's behalf as long as the contribution does not exceed the child's actual earned income. Open a custodial Roth IRA at a brokerage in the child's name (as the minor) with you as custodian. Contributions for 2026 can be made until the tax filing deadline (typically April 15, 2027). Confirm the specifics with your advisor.

**Step 19: Confirm the child's tax filing obligation.**
Your child must file a federal income tax return if their gross income exceeds the standard deduction applicable to them. For 2026: if wages are the only income and total wages are $16,100 or less, the child likely owes no federal income tax and may not be required to file. However, if you withheld any income tax from the child's paychecks, the child should file to receive a refund. Confirm with your advisor, especially if the child has any unearned income (interest, dividends) that could trigger the kiddie tax.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity type is the most important variable.** The FICA and FUTA exemptions only apply in sole proprietorships and qualifying parent partnerships. If your business is an S-corp or C-corp, those exemptions do not exist. The income-shifting benefit (moving income from your bracket to the child's zero bracket) still works through a corporation, but you will owe full payroll taxes on the child's wages just as you would for any other employee. Run the numbers with your advisor before implementing through a corporation.

**Single-member LLCs:** A single-member LLC that has NOT elected to be taxed as an S-corp is treated as a sole proprietorship ("disregarded entity") for federal tax purposes. The FICA and FUTA exemptions apply. If your SMLLC has made an S-corp election, the corporation rules apply and the exemptions do not.

**State income tax withholding:** Most states with an income tax require you to withhold state income tax from employee wages and remit it to the state, even for family members. Register with your state's tax agency before the first paycheck.

**State unemployment tax (SUTA):** Many states do not follow the federal FUTA exemption for children employed by a parent. Your state may require SUTA contributions on wages paid to your child even if FUTA does not apply federally. Check your state Department of Labor website.

**State child labor laws:** Federal law (FLSA) allows children of any age to work in a business solely owned by their parent in non-manufacturing, non-mining, and non-hazardous occupations. However, many states impose stricter age minimums, work-hour limits, and permit requirements. Some states require a work permit (sometimes called a work permit or "working papers") for minors under 16 or 18. Check your state's Department of Labor for the specific rules.

**Federal child labor (FLSA) minimum rules:** Under federal law (the Fair Labor Standards Act), youth may work for their parents in the parent's solely owned non-agricultural business except in manufacturing, mining, and occupations declared hazardous by the Secretary of Labor. Eighteen is the minimum age for employment in any occupation declared hazardous. Children under 18 may not be employed in hazardous occupations regardless of the parent exemption. Confirm with the Department of Labor (dol.gov/whd) for the current list of hazardous occupations.

**Wages must be for actual services at reasonable rates.** The IRS can reclassify wages as disguised gifts if the child did not perform genuine services, if the work is not documented, or if the compensation exceeds what is reasonable for the tasks and the child's age and capability. Disallowed wages become non-deductible gifts.

**Do not pay by cash without documentation.** If you cannot show bank records or cancelled checks demonstrating actual payment, the IRS may disallow the deduction. Always pay by check or ACH transfer to a bank account in the child's name.

**One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) 2025 -- no direct change to family employment rules.** The OBBBA (signed July 4, 2025) did not change IRC Sections 3121(b)(3) or 3306(c)(5), which govern the child employment FICA and FUTA exemptions. The 2026 standard deduction ($16,100 single) reflects both ordinary inflation adjustments and OBBBA provisions. Confirm with your advisor whether any other OBBBA provisions affect your specific situation.

**Roth IRA contribution eligibility:** Contributions to a Roth IRA are also subject to income phase-out rules for the contributor. Because a child's income from the family business will typically be well below the Roth IRA income limits for single filers (phase-out begins at $153,000 for 2026), phase-out is not a concern in most cases. Confirm with your advisor.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Paying for household chores.** Cleaning their own room, doing family laundry, or mowing the family lawn are not business services. The child must perform tasks that serve the business, not the household.

**2. Paying an unreasonable wage.** Paying $75/hour to a 9-year-old for "marketing consulting" will not survive IRS scrutiny. The rate must match what you would actually pay an outside worker for the same task.

**3. No timesheets or work documentation.** If you cannot show when the child worked, what they did, and how many hours, the IRS can disallow the deduction entirely. Timesheets are not optional.

**4. Paying in cash with no paper trail.** Informal cash payments are extremely difficult to defend in an audit. Always use a check or electronic transfer that appears in both your business bank account and the child's bank account.

**5. Skipping the W-2.** Even if no tax is owed, you must issue a W-2 and file it with the SSA. Failure to file W-2s carries penalties: $60 per form for returns filed up to 30 days late; $130 per form for returns filed 31 days late through August 1; $340 per form for returns filed after August 1 or not filed at all; and $680 per form for intentional disregard -- for returns required to be filed in 2026. Source: https://www.irs.gov/payments/information-return-penalties [Confirm the exact 2026 penalty amounts and caps with your advisor -- the penalty schedule is adjusted for inflation.]

**6. Using the wrong entity type and assuming FICA is exempt.** Owners of S-corps and C-corps frequently assume the child wage exemption applies to them. It does not. This mistake results in under-deposited FICA taxes, penalties, and interest.

**7. Forgetting state requirements.** Focusing only on federal rules and ignoring state income tax withholding, SUTA, work permits, or new hire reporting. Each state is different.

**8. Starting payroll mid-year without back-dating job documentation.** You cannot create timesheets after the fact. Documentation must be created contemporaneously -- at the time the work is performed.

**9. Hiring children too young for the tasks.** A 4-year-old cannot reasonably be the company's social media manager. The work must match the child's actual developmental capacity.

**10. Failing to have the child complete a W-4 before the first paycheck.** Without a W-4, you must withhold at the default rate (single, no adjustments), which may result in overwithholding and require the child to file a return for a refund.

## Advisor Review Gates

Before acting, confirm the following with your advisor:

- **Entity structure confirmation:** Has your advisor confirmed that your entity type (sole proprietorship, qualifying partnership) makes your child's wages eligible for the FICA and FUTA exemptions? If you operate through any corporate structure, stop here and get specific guidance on cost vs. benefit.
- **State-specific rules:** Has your advisor or you researched your state's income tax withholding requirement, SUTA requirements, and work permit rules for minors? These vary significantly.
- **Wage reasonableness sign-off:** Does your advisor agree that the hourly rate you have set is reasonable for the tasks and the child's age? Document this conversation.
- **Payroll system confirmation:** Has your payroll system been configured to correctly flag the child as FICA-exempt (if applicable) before the first paycheck runs? An incorrectly configured system will withhold FICA it should not -- or fail to withhold income tax it should.
- **IRA contribution amount:** Before contributing to the child's Roth IRA, confirm the exact earned income amount for the year and the contribution limit that results.
- **Child's filing requirement:** Confirm whether the child is required to file a federal (and state) income tax return for 2026 based on total income and any withholding.
- **If the child is under age 7:** Get explicit advisor sign-off on the reasonableness of employment at a very young age, given heightened IRS scrutiny.

## Using The Download(s)
