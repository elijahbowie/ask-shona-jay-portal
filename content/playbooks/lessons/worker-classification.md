# Worker Classification

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Worker classification is the process of correctly identifying whether a person who performs services for your business is an employee or an independent contractor. The IRS and state agencies treat these two categories very differently: employees trigger payroll tax withholding obligations, unemployment insurance, and benefits requirements, while independent contractors handle their own taxes. Getting the classification wrong, in either direction, can expose your business to back taxes, penalties, interest, and state-level fines. This playbook walks you through the IRS common-law test, the documentation you need, the forms involved, and how to correct a past mistake safely.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is for business owners who:

- Pay individuals for services and need to determine whether those individuals are employees or independent contractors.
- Have received an IRS or state audit notice related to worker classification.
- Want to proactively document their classification decisions to defend against future challenge.
- Are considering reclassifying workers they currently treat as contractors.
- Have been told by a worker that the worker believes they should be classified as an employee.

This applies regardless of entity type (sole proprietor, LLC, S corporation, C corporation, or partnership).

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not use this playbook as your only resource if any of the following apply:

- You have already received a formal IRS employment tax audit notice (IRS Letter 950 or similar). Stop and contact your advisor immediately before taking any action.
- You are in California, New Jersey, Massachusetts, or another state with an "ABC test" (a stricter three-prong rule described in the Key Terms section). Federal rules alone are not enough; you must also satisfy your state's standard.
- You are classifying workers in industries with specific federal labor rules, such as trucking, home care, or certain construction trades. Additional Department of Labor rules may apply.
- You want to reclassify a worker from independent contractor to employee for reasons other than genuine compliance. The IRS looks at economic reality, not labels.
- The working relationship involves a staffing agency, professional employer organization (PEO), or leased employees. Those arrangements have their own rules.

## Key Terms

**Employee (common-law):** A worker is a common-law employee if the business has the right to control not only what work is done but also how the work is done. The business does not actually have to exercise that control; merely having the right to do so is enough.

**Independent contractor:** A worker is an independent contractor if the business controls only the result of the work, not the methods or means used to accomplish it. Independent contractors typically set their own hours, use their own tools, and may work for multiple clients simultaneously.

**Behavioral control:** One of the three IRS categories for classification. It examines whether the business directs how the worker performs tasks, including the type of instructions given, training provided, and the degree to which the worker can set their own schedule and methods.

**Financial control:** One of the three IRS categories. It examines whether the business controls the business aspects of the worker's job, including how the worker is paid, whether expenses are reimbursed, whether the worker has a significant investment in their own tools or facilities, and whether the worker can realize a profit or loss.

**Type of relationship:** One of the three IRS categories. It looks at the facts that show how both parties perceive the arrangement, including written contracts, employee-type benefits (health insurance, paid leave, pension), whether the relationship is expected to continue indefinitely, and whether the work is central to the business's core operations.

**Form 1099-NEC:** The IRS form used to report nonemployee compensation (payments to independent contractors) of $2,000 or more in a calendar year (for payments made on or after January 1, 2026; the prior threshold was $600). "NEC" stands for nonemployee compensation.

**Form W-9:** A one-page IRS form that an independent contractor fills out to give you their legal name, business name (if applicable), taxpayer identification number (TIN), and certification that they are not subject to backup withholding. You keep this form on file; you do not send it to the IRS.

**Form SS-8:** The form either a business or a worker files with the IRS to ask for an official determination of whether a specific worker is an employee or an independent contractor. The IRS review can take several months and is binding only for the specific arrangement described.

**Form W-2:** The annual wage and tax statement that employers must provide to employees and file with the IRS and Social Security Administration. If a worker is an employee, you use this form, not Form 1099-NEC.

**Form 8919:** A form that a worker who was misclassified as an independent contractor but believes they were actually an employee files with their personal tax return. It allows the worker to pay only the employee share of Social Security and Medicare taxes rather than the full self-employment tax.

**Self-employment (SE) tax:** The tax independent contractors pay to cover their Social Security and Medicare obligations. Because contractors have no employer withholding, they pay the combined employer-plus-employee rate of 15.3% (12.4% Social Security and 2.9% Medicare) on net self-employment earnings of $400 or more. SE tax is computed on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings (this factor approximates the employer-equivalent reduction). Contractors may then deduct one-half of the SE tax actually computed -- the employer-equivalent share -- as an adjustment to income when calculating their adjusted gross income (AGI). The deductible amount is one-half of the dollar amount of SE tax you actually owe, not a flat percentage of gross income.

**FICA taxes:** Federal Insurance Contributions Act taxes. For employees in 2026, this is 6.2% Social Security and 1.45% Medicare withheld from the employee's wages, plus a matching 6.2% and 1.45% paid by the employer. Independent contractors pay the entire 15.3% themselves as SE tax.

**FUTA:** Federal Unemployment Tax Act. Employers pay FUTA on employee wages at 6.0% on the first $7,000 paid to each employee per year. If the employer paid into a qualifying state unemployment fund, a credit of up to 5.4% typically applies, bringing the effective federal rate to as low as 0.6%. FUTA does not apply to payments to independent contractors.

**Section 530 relief:** A provision in the Tax Reform Act of 1976 (extended indefinitely in 1982) that protects businesses from employment tax liability for misclassified workers if the business meets three requirements: (1) consistent reporting on Forms 1099 rather than W-2, (2) never having treated a similarly situated worker as an employee after December 31, 1977, and (3) a "reasonable basis" for the classification, such as relying on prior IRS audit results, court decisions, or long-standing industry practice.

**Voluntary Classification Settlement Program (VCSP):** An IRS program that allows businesses to proactively reclassify workers from independent contractors to employees and pay a reduced amount of past employment taxes, with no penalties or interest, in exchange for agreeing to treat those workers as employees going forward.

**Section 3509 reduced rates:** When a business is found to have misclassified workers and does not qualify for Section 530 relief, but did not intentionally disregard the withholding obligation, the IRS may assess taxes at reduced rates under Internal Revenue Code Section 3509(a): 10.68% of compensation up to the Social Security wage base and 3.24% of compensation above it, rather than full employment tax rates.

**ABC test:** A stricter worker classification standard used in some states (most notably California under AB5, and New Jersey). To classify a worker as an independent contractor under the ABC test, the hiring business must prove all three of: (A) the worker is free from the control and direction of the hiring entity in connection with performing the work; (B) the worker performs work outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business; and (C) the worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade or occupation of the same nature as the work performed. Failing any single prong means the worker is an employee under that state's law.

**Backup withholding:** When a contractor does not provide a valid taxpayer identification number or the IRS notifies you that their TIN is incorrect, you must withhold 24% of payments and remit it to the IRS. You report backup withholding on Form 945.

**Form 940:** The annual IRS form for reporting FUTA taxes paid (or owed) on employee wages. Filed once per year; due January 31 of the following year.

**Form 941:** The quarterly IRS form for reporting federal income tax withheld and FICA taxes (Social Security and Medicare) for employees. Filed four times per year.

**Payroll tax:** The collective term for FICA taxes and federal income tax withholding that employers must calculate, withhold, and remit to the IRS on behalf of employees.

## Required Client Inputs

Before beginning the classification analysis for any worker, gather the following:

1. A description of the work the person performs, including what tasks, how they are assigned, and how completion is measured.
2. Whether you or the worker decides the schedule, location, and methods used to complete the work.
3. Whether you provide tools, equipment, software licenses, or a workspace, or whether the worker supplies their own.
4. How the worker is paid: hourly, daily, per-project, on a flat-fee basis, or a regular salary.
5. Whether the worker may simultaneously perform the same type of work for your competitors or other clients.
6. Whether you provide the worker with benefits such as health insurance, paid time off, retirement contributions, or expense reimbursements.
7. Whether there is a written agreement describing the relationship (a contract, statement of work, or engagement letter).
8. How long the working relationship has continued and whether it is expected to be ongoing or project-based.
9. Whether the worker has made a significant personal investment in equipment or a business presence (for example, their own website, business registration, or professional license).
10. Whether the worker can realize a financial profit or take a financial loss depending on how they manage the engagement.
11. The state or states in which the worker performs services (because state rules vary significantly).
12. Whether you have previously filed Forms 1099-NEC or W-2 for this worker (or for other workers in the same role).

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect before the engagement begins:**

- A completed and signed Form W-9 from every independent contractor before you make any payment. Keep the original; do not send it to the IRS.
- A written independent contractor agreement signed by both parties describing the scope of work, payment terms, and nature of the relationship. The contract should specify that the contractor controls the methods and means of their work.

**Maintain throughout the engagement:**

- Invoices from the contractor for each payment made. Contractors should invoice you; you should not issue them paychecks formatted like payroll stubs.
- Bank records, wire confirmations, or check images documenting each payment.
- Any written communications (email, text, project briefs) that show you directed only the outcome, not the day-to-day methods.
- Time records showing the contractor set their own schedule rather than following your required business hours.
- Any evidence the contractor provides their own tools, uses their own software subscriptions, or maintains their own office.

**File with the IRS by deadline:**

- Form 1099-NEC for each contractor paid $2,000 or more during calendar year 2026.
- Form 1096 as the transmittal cover sheet if filing paper copies with the IRS (not required for electronic filers).

**Retain after the engagement ends:**

- Keep all classification documentation for at least four years from the later of the date the tax was due or the date you paid it. The IRS generally has three years from the filing date to audit, but the statute of limitations extends to six years if the IRS believes income was substantially understated. Four years is a safe minimum; six years is safer.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Follow these steps in order for each new worker or for a review of existing workers.

**Step 1: Collect the worker's Form W-9 before making any payment.**

Ask the contractor to complete Form W-9 (Request for Taxpayer Identification Number and Certification). This form gives you their legal name, business name if applicable, address, Social Security number or Employer Identification Number, and their certification that they are not subject to backup withholding. File the original in your records. If a contractor refuses or fails to provide a valid W-9, you must withhold 24% of payments (backup withholding) and report it on Form 945. Do not make any payments until you have a completed W-9 on file.

**Step 2: Apply the three-factor IRS common-law test to document the classification.**

Use the Worker Classification Implementation Worksheet (the attached download) to record your analysis. Work through all three categories:

*Behavioral control:* Write down the specific ways the worker controls how they do their work. Does the worker set their own hours? Do they choose the order in which tasks are completed? Do they use their own methods without instruction from you? The more you direct these details, the more the relationship looks like employment. Note any training you provide: if the worker receives training on your methods, that points toward employee status.

*Financial control:* Record how the worker is paid (per project or on a flat fee is contractor-like; a regular hourly or weekly wage is employee-like). List whether the worker has unreimbursed expenses, provides their own tools, and whether they can profit or lose money based on how efficiently they work. If the worker works only for you and you are their only client, that is a flag worth noting.

*Type of relationship:* Note whether you have a written contract, whether you provide benefits, and whether the relationship is expected to be ongoing and indefinite (employee-like) or project-specific (contractor-like). Ask whether the work is integral to your core business. A graphic designer hired to build one website for a law firm looks different from a contractor who handles daily accounting for an accounting firm.

At the end of this analysis, document your conclusion and the primary factors supporting it. Keep this worksheet with your other business records.

**Step 3: Execute a written independent contractor agreement.**

If your analysis supports independent contractor status, put the relationship in writing before work begins. The agreement should state that the contractor controls the methods and means of their work, that they are responsible for their own taxes, that they are not entitled to employee benefits, and that the engagement is project-based or terminable by either party without the formalities of employment separation. A written contract does not override the actual working relationship in the IRS's eyes, but it is strong evidence of the parties' intent and is one of the factors considered under "type of relationship."

**Step 4: Pay the contractor and track cumulative payments across the calendar year.**

Keep a running total of what you pay each contractor during the calendar year. For 2026, once cumulative payments to a single contractor reach $2,000, you will owe a Form 1099-NEC. Consider using accounting software to track this automatically. Pay contractors from your business bank account in response to invoices they submit; do not run their compensation through payroll software as if they were employees.

**Step 5: Issue Form 1099-NEC by January 31, 2027 for calendar year 2026 payments.**

For each independent contractor paid $2,000 or more during 2026, complete the current version of Form 1099-NEC (check irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1099-nec for the current revision before filing). You must:

- Furnish Copy B to the contractor (the recipient) by January 31, 2027.
- File Copy A with the IRS by January 31, 2027 (both paper and electronic filers share this deadline for Form 1099-NEC under Section 6071(c)).
- File Form 1096 as a paper transmittal cover if you file paper returns. Electronic filers do not use Form 1096.
- Use the IRS's free IRIS (Information Returns Intake System) Taxpayer Portal for free e-filing, or file through an approved software provider.

Note: If you file 10 or more information returns of any type in a calendar year, electronic filing is mandatory.

**Step 6: Retain documentation for at least four years.**

After filing, store the following together: the contractor's completed W-9, the written agreement, all invoices and payment records, your completed classification worksheet, and copies of the filed Form 1099-NEC. Store these in a dedicated contractor file by year.

**Step 7 (if reclassification is needed): Evaluate VCSP before taking any other action.**

If you have been treating workers as independent contractors and now believe they should be employees, do not simply switch them to payroll without evaluating your options. Contact your advisor. If you qualify for the Voluntary Classification Settlement Program (see "The Numbers" section), you can reclassify prospectively, pay a small fraction of the back taxes you would otherwise owe, and avoid penalties and interest. File Form 8952 at least 120 days before the date you want to begin treating the workers as employees.

**Recurring rhythm:**

- Each month: verify that payment totals to each contractor are being tracked and that W-9s are on file for anyone newly engaged.
- Each quarter: confirm that your payroll forms (Form 941) do not inadvertently include contractor payments, and that your contractor payments are not running through payroll software.
- Each January (by the 31st): issue Form 1099-NEC to all qualifying contractors for the prior year and file with the IRS.
- Each year: review the roles of workers you classified in prior years to confirm the facts of the relationship have not changed enough to alter the classification.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity type:** The IRS classification analysis applies the same three-factor test regardless of whether your business is a sole proprietorship, LLC, S corporation, C corporation, or partnership. An S corporation with one owner-employee is still subject to these rules for any workers it engages beyond the owner.

**Payroll setup:** If you reclassify workers to employees, you must set up payroll withholding immediately. You will need an Employer Identification Number (EIN) if you do not already have one, a payroll system to calculate and withhold federal income tax and FICA, and registration with your state for state income tax withholding and unemployment insurance. Your advisor or a payroll provider can set this up, but plan for several weeks of lead time.

**State laws with stricter tests:** If you do business in or hire workers in California, New Jersey, Massachusetts, or other states that have adopted the ABC test, the federal common-law analysis is a necessary but not sufficient condition for independent contractor status. California's AB5 (effective January 1, 2020, last amended by AB1514 effective January 2026) presumes every worker is an employee unless all three prongs of the ABC test are satisfied. Failing Prong B (the worker performs work outside the usual course of your business) is a common stumbling block in California. Consult your advisor before engaging California-based workers as contractors.

**Department of Labor standards:** The IRS test governs federal employment taxes. The U.S. Department of Labor applies a separate "economic reality" test under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) to determine minimum wage and overtime obligations. A worker can be an independent contractor for IRS purposes but still have protections under the FLSA. These are separate analyses.

**Current-year change (2026): One Big Beautiful Bill Act:** The threshold for Form 1099-NEC increased from $600 to $2,000 for payments made on or after January 1, 2026. The $600 threshold had been in place since 1954. Beginning in 2027, the threshold will be adjusted annually for inflation, with the adjusted amount rounded to the nearest $100 (per Section 70433 of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act). Make sure your accounting software and payroll system reflect the new threshold.

**VCSP ineligibility triggers:** You cannot apply for the VCSP if you are currently under an IRS employment tax audit, a Department of Labor audit, or a state agency audit related to worker classification. If you failed to file required Forms 1099 for any of the three years before your application, you may also be ineligible. Act before an audit begins, not after.

**TCJA and QBI:** If you reclassify contractors as employees, the workers are no longer self-employed and may lose their own Section 199A qualified business income (QBI) deduction. That is a consideration for the worker, not you, but it may affect negotiations about compensation adjustments.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Labeling a worker a contractor without testing the relationship.** A contract that says "independent contractor" does not make it so. The IRS looks at economic reality, not labels. Businesses that simply use contractor agreements without examining behavioral, financial, and type-of-relationship factors are taking on significant risk.

**2. Providing substantial behavioral direction to contractors.** Telling a contractor when to show up, requiring them to attend daily team meetings, assigning them a desk, and directing their day-to-day activities are all signs of an employment relationship even if the worker is paid on an invoice basis.

**3. Using a contractor exclusively.** If a worker performs services only for your business and has no other clients, that exclusivity is a strong indicator of employee status. Independent contractors typically market their services to multiple clients.

**4. Forgetting to collect Form W-9 before payment.** If you pay a contractor without a W-9 and later cannot collect one, you may owe backup withholding going back to the first payment. The IRS can assess backup withholding liability against the payer.

**5. Missing the Form 1099-NEC deadline.** For returns required to be filed in 2027 (covering calendar year 2026 payments), penalties for late or missing 1099s are $60 per form if corrected within 30 days, $130 per form if corrected after 30 days through August 1, and $340 per form if filed after August 1 or not filed at all. The intentional-disregard penalty is at least $680 per form. The small-business maximum (gross receipts under $5 million) is $1,397,000 per calendar year. These figures are set by Rev. Proc. 2025-32 and apply to information returns required to be filed in 2027. See also IRS Information Return Penalties at irs.gov/payments/information-return-penalties. File by January 31, 2027 to avoid penalties.

**6. Not tracking cumulative payments across the year.** Payments that individually look small can cross the $2,000 reporting threshold when totaled. Track payments per contractor per calendar year from day one.

**7. Reclassifying workers abruptly without using VCSP.** If you realize you have been misclassifying workers and simply switch them to payroll without seeking relief, you may be exposed to full back employment taxes, plus penalties and interest, for up to three prior years. VCSP eliminates penalties and interest and caps the back-tax payment at 10% of Section 3509(a) rates.

**8. Applying only federal rules in states with ABC tests.** Passing the IRS three-factor test is not enough in California, New Jersey, and other ABC-test states. Treating a California worker as a contractor while meeting only the federal standard exposes you to state-level back taxes, fines of $5,000 to $25,000 per violation, and private civil lawsuits.

**9. Treating all workers in a role the same without individual analysis.** Two workers with the same job title may have different actual arrangements. The IRS tests each individual working relationship on its own facts.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before taking action if any of the following apply:

- You have received an IRS audit notice, an IRS inquiry letter, or a Department of Labor contact related to worker classification.
- A worker has filed or indicated they may file Form SS-8 requesting an official IRS determination of their status.
- A worker has filed Form 8919 on their personal tax return, which notifies the IRS that they believe they were misclassified.
- You are considering engaging workers in California, New Jersey, Massachusetts, or any other state you have not previously hired in, because state ABC tests may apply.
- Your analysis under the three-factor test is not clearly pointing in one direction. Mixed facts are normal; the final call should involve your advisor, especially for workers who generate significant revenue for your business.
- You want to apply for the VCSP. The application (Form 8952) must be filed correctly and far enough in advance, and the timing of reclassification must be coordinated with your payroll setup.
- You are in a franchise arrangement, a staffing agency arrangement, or a platform-economy business model. These have unique classification overlays.
- You are considering whether to pay a worker through your S corporation as an employee or as a subcontractor. Mixing classification decisions with S-corp payroll strategy requires coordinated planning.
- A contractor's cumulative payments for the year will be large enough that a reclassification to employee would materially affect your FICA liability and your profit/loss for the year.

**Worker Classification Implementation Worksheet**

Use this worksheet when you onboard a new contractor or when you are reviewing the status of an existing contractor. Complete one worksheet per worker or per substantially similar group of workers.

Where in the workflow to use it:

- Pull up the worksheet at Step 2 of the implementation steps above, after you have collected the worker's Form W-9 but before you finalize the working arrangement.

## Before You Act

Before you classify any worker, file any Form 1099-NEC, switch a contractor to employee payroll, apply for the VCSP, or sign any independent contractor agreement, confirm the following with your advisor:

1. The facts of the working relationship satisfy both the federal common-law test and any applicable state test (ABC test or other state standard) for the states in which the worker performs services.
2. You are not currently under an IRS, DOL, or state agency audit or inquiry that would affect your options.
3. The Form 1099-NEC threshold ($2,000 for calendar year 2026 payments, indexed for inflation beginning 2027) and filing deadline (January 31, 2027 for 2026 payments) have not been updated by IRS guidance or legislation issued after this playbook was last reviewed.
4. Your entity structure, S-corporation payroll obligations, and any profit-sharing arrangements with the worker have been accounted for in your plan.

Worker classification has some of the largest penalty exposure in the tax code. A proactive, documented approach costs far less than defending a reclassification audit.
