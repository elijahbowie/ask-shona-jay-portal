# Business Losses, At-Risk Limits, Passive Losses, and NOLs

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

When your business or investment generates a loss, four separate sets of federal rules determine whether you can actually use that loss to reduce your tax bill right now, or whether it gets suspended and carried forward to a future year. Those four filters, applied in order, are: (1) the basis limitation, (2) the at-risk limitation, (3) the passive activity loss rules, and (4) the excess business loss cap. If a loss survives all four filters, any remaining amount that exceeds your income can often be preserved as a net operating loss (NOL) that offsets income in future years. Understanding where a loss gets stuck, and why, lets you plan around the bottleneck rather than discovering the problem at tax time.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is most relevant to:

- **Business owners who operate through a pass-through entity** (S-corporation, partnership, or multi-member LLC) and receive a Schedule K-1 showing a loss.
- **Real estate investors** who own rental property and generate rental losses, whether or not they qualify as real estate professionals.
- **Sole proprietors and single-member LLC owners** (Schedule C filers) who expect a net business loss in the current year or have losses carried over from prior years.
- **Investors in passive activities** such as limited partnerships, syndications, or any deal where you do not materially participate in day-to-day operations.
- **Taxpayers with NOL carryforwards** from prior loss years who want to understand how and when those carryforwards can offset income.
- **High-income earners** (roughly above $100,000 of modified adjusted gross income for individuals, or $150,000 and above where certain exceptions fully phase out) who need to know in advance whether a loss will actually reduce their current-year taxes.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **C-corporations:** These rules apply differently at the entity level. C-corporations face their own NOL rules under Section 172 and are generally not subject to the passive activity loss rules the same way individuals are. C-corp owners should work through these issues with their advisor separately.
- **Taxpayers with only W-2 income and no business or investment activities:** If you do not own a business, rental property, or pass-through interest, none of these loss limitation rules affects your return.
- **Taxpayers whose losses are already fully absorbed by passive income:** If you have enough passive income to absorb your passive losses in the same year, the passive loss limitation does not restrict you further.
- **Situations where the loss was already disallowed in a prior year for a different reason:** If a loss was suspended under the basis limitation or the at-risk rules, it stays suspended until you restore basis or increase your at-risk amount, regardless of passive income. Trying to treat that loss as currently deductible before restoring basis is an audit risk.

## Key Terms

**Basis (tax basis):** Your starting investment in an asset for tax purposes. For a partnership interest, it equals the cash you contributed plus your share of partnership liabilities plus your share of income, minus distributions and losses already deducted. For an S-corporation, it equals the cost of your stock plus loans you personally made to the corporation (not loans the corporation took from a bank), plus your share of income, minus distributions and losses. You cannot deduct losses that exceed your basis; the excess is suspended until basis is restored.

**At-risk amount:** The dollars you could actually lose from an activity. It is similar to basis but stricter because it excludes borrowed money for which you are not personally liable (called nonrecourse debt) and excludes amounts protected by guarantees or stop-loss arrangements. Governed by Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 465. Your loss deduction cannot exceed your at-risk amount.

**Nonrecourse debt:** A loan for which the lender's only recourse if you do not repay is to seize the collateral. You are not personally on the hook. Nonrecourse debt generally does not increase your at-risk amount (with one important exception for real estate described below).

**Qualified nonrecourse financing:** A special category of nonrecourse debt used in real estate activities, defined in IRC Section 465(b)(6). Even though it is nonrecourse, it does count toward your at-risk amount if the financing is secured by real property used in the activity, borrowed from a qualified lender (a bank, government entity, or other lender in the business of lending and not related to you), and is not convertible debt. This exception exists because Congress recognized that real estate is routinely financed with nonrecourse mortgages.

**Passive activity:** A trade or business in which you do not materially participate, or any rental activity (regardless of participation level, with limited exceptions). Governed by IRC Section 469. Losses from passive activities can only offset income from other passive activities. They cannot offset wages, S-corp income in which you materially participate, interest, dividends, or other non-passive income.

**Material participation:** Meeting one of seven IRS tests that demonstrate you are involved in the activity on a regular, continuous, and substantial basis. The most commonly used test is participating more than 500 hours in the activity during the tax year. Others include: participating substantially all of the time anyone participates in the activity (even if less than 500 hours); participating more than 100 hours and at least as much as any other person; meeting the material participation standard in at least 5 of the 10 prior tax years (for activities you have run for a while); or meeting it in any 3 prior years for personal service activities. If you meet any one of the seven tests, the activity is non-passive for that year.

**Active participation:** A lower, easier-to-meet standard that applies only to the $25,000 rental real estate exception described below. You actively participate if you make management decisions in a genuine and meaningful sense, such as approving tenants, setting rents, or deciding on repairs, even if you use a property manager. You must own at least 10% of the rental activity (including your spouse's interest) to qualify. You cannot be a limited partner and claim active participation.

**Real estate professional:** A taxpayer who satisfies two tests in the same tax year under IRC Section 469(c)(7): (1) more than half of the personal services you perform in all trades or businesses during the year are performed in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate; and (2) you perform more than 750 hours of services in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate. Meeting both tests converts your rental activities from passive to non-passive, allowing rental losses to offset any income.

**Suspended loss:** A loss that was disallowed in the current year because of one of the four limitation rules, but that is preserved (not lost forever) and carries forward to be used in a future year when the limiting condition is removed.

**Net operating loss (NOL):** The amount by which your business deductions exceed your business income for the year, after applying the other loss limitation rules. Governed by IRC Section 172. Post-2017 NOLs can be carried forward indefinitely but can only offset up to 80% of taxable income in any single future year.

**NOL carryforward:** The preserved loss amount from a prior year that you carry forward and deduct against income in a future year, subject to the 80% annual cap.

**NOL carryback:** Using a current-year loss to amend a prior year's return and claim a refund. Under current law (for losses arising after 2020), carrybacks are generally not allowed, except for certain farming losses, which may be carried back 2 years.

**Excess business loss:** The amount by which your total net business losses (from all trades or businesses combined) exceed your total business income plus a threshold amount set by law. Governed by IRC Section 461(l). Any excess becomes an NOL carryforward for the next year. This rule applies to individuals, estates, and trusts, not to C-corporations.

**Modified adjusted gross income (MAGI):** For purposes of the passive loss rules, your adjusted gross income (AGI) recalculated by adding back certain deductions such as IRA contributions, student loan interest, and rental real estate losses. MAGI is used to determine whether the $25,000 rental exception phases out.

**Schedule K-1:** The IRS form issued by a partnership (Form 1065), S-corporation (Form 1120-S), or estate/trust to each owner or beneficiary, showing the owner's share of income, loss, deductions, and credits to report on their personal return.

**Form 6198:** The IRS form used to calculate the at-risk limitation and determine how much of a loss from an at-risk activity is deductible. You file this alongside your personal return when your losses from an activity exceed your at-risk amount.

**Form 8582:** The IRS form used to calculate passive activity loss limitations and track suspended passive losses. Filed with your personal return when you have passive activities with losses.

**Form 461:** The IRS form used to calculate the excess business loss limitation under IRC Section 461(l). Filed by noncorporate taxpayers when their net business losses exceed the threshold.

**Form 172:** The IRS form used by individuals, estates, and trusts to compute and track net operating loss carryforwards.

**Section 704(d):** The IRC provision that limits a partner's deductible share of partnership losses to the partner's outside basis (their investment in the partnership) at the end of the partnership's tax year.

**Section 1366(d):** The IRC provision that limits an S-corporation shareholder's deductible share of S-corp losses to the sum of the shareholder's stock basis plus the basis in any debt the shareholder personally loaned to the S-corp (not bank debt the S-corp owes to others).

**Outside basis (partnership):** A partner's basis in their partnership interest, tracked from the outside perspective of the partner. Distinct from the partnership's own internal tracking of assets.

**TCJA:** The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, which introduced the 80% NOL limitation, eliminated NOL carrybacks for most taxpayers, and created the excess business loss limitation.

**OBBBA:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21), signed in 2025, which made the excess business loss limitation permanent (it had been scheduled to expire after 2028) and reset the threshold amounts back toward the original TCJA figures starting in 2026.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather all of the following before starting the implementation workflow:

1. **Entity type for each activity:** Is the loss coming from a sole proprietorship (Schedule C), S-corporation, partnership, rental property (Schedule E), or a combination?
2. **Current tax basis:** For each pass-through interest, what is your current outside basis (partnership) or stock-plus-loan basis (S-corp)? Your prior-year tax return and any Schedule K-1 worksheets should show this.
3. **Amount of any nonrecourse debt:** Does the entity or activity carry nonrecourse loans? For real estate, is that debt qualified nonrecourse financing under Section 465(b)(6)?
4. **At-risk amount as of the start of the year:** From your prior-year Form 6198 (line 19 carryover amount), or calculated fresh for a new investment.
5. **Hours spent in each activity this year:** Keep a running log. This is required to establish whether any activity meets the material participation tests or, for real estate professionals, the 750-hour test.
6. **Modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) estimate:** For the rental real estate $25,000 exception, you need to know whether your MAGI is below $100,000, between $100,000 and $150,000, or above $150,000.
7. **Suspended losses from prior years:** Pull Form 8582 and Form 6198 carryover worksheets from your prior-year return to know what losses are already waiting to be freed up.
8. **NOL carryforward amounts:** From your prior-year Form 172 or the NOL carryforward worksheet on your prior return. Note whether each NOL arose before or after January 1, 2018, because pre-2018 and post-2017 NOLs are subject to different rules.
9. **Ownership percentage in each pass-through entity.**
10. **State(s) of operation:** Many states do not conform to federal passive loss or NOL rules. Know your state's rules (consult your advisor).

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect before filing:**

- Schedule K-1s from every partnership, S-corporation, and trust in which you have an interest.
- Prior-year tax return, especially Schedules C, E, Form 6198, Form 8582, Form 461, and any NOL carryforward schedules.
- Loan agreements for any money you personally loaned to an S-corporation (needed to establish loan basis under Section 1366(d)).
- Mortgage or loan documents for real estate activities (to confirm whether financing qualifies as qualified nonrecourse financing under Section 465(b)(6)).
- Contemporaneous time log documenting hours you worked in each activity during the year, with dates, descriptions, and hour counts.
- Property records and depreciation schedules for rental property.
- Documentation of your ownership percentage in each entity.

**Records to create and keep:**

- A running activity log updated throughout the year (not reconstructed at year-end) showing hours and tasks for each business and rental activity. Courts and the IRS have rejected reconstructed logs that were assembled from memory after the fact.
- Year-end basis calculation worksheets for each pass-through interest, updated after you receive K-1s.
- Copies of Form 6198, Form 8582, and Form 461 filed with each year's return, plus the carryover worksheets showing suspended loss amounts.
- Form 172 or NOL carryforward schedule showing each year's NOL origin date and the amount remaining.

**Retention guidance:** Keep all of the above for at least 3 years after the year you finally use the carried-over loss or the carryforward expires, whichever is later. The IRS instructs taxpayers to keep NOL records for 3 years after you have used the carryback or carryforward, per Form 172 instructions. Because some carryforwards can stretch many years, the practical standard is to never discard basis worksheets, loss carryover schedules, or NOL records until the loss is fully absorbed and 3 years have passed.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Apply the Basis Limitation First

**Step 1. Calculate your current basis in the pass-through interest.**

For an S-corp shareholder (Section 1366(d)): Start with your prior-year stock basis. Add your share of this year's income and separately stated income items. Add any additional capital contributions you made this year. Add the basis of any new loans you personally made directly to the S-corp (not bank loans the S-corp took out). Subtract distributions received. The result is your current stock and loan basis.

For a partner (Section 704(d)): Start with your prior-year outside basis. Add your share of this year's income, your share of partnership liabilities that increased, and any contributions. Subtract your share of liabilities that decreased and any distributions. The result is your outside basis.

Sole proprietors have no basis limitation that functions separately from the at-risk rules; proceed to Step 2.

**Step 2. Compare your allocated loss to your basis.**

If your share of the loss is less than or equal to your basis, the full loss passes the basis test. Proceed to Step 3.

If your share of the loss exceeds your basis, only the amount up to your basis is deductible this year. The excess is suspended under Section 704(d) or 1366(d) and carries forward (it does not expire). You cannot proceed further with the suspended portion until you restore basis in a future year.

**Step 3. Restore basis through additional contributions or income.**

If losses were suspended due to insufficient basis, you can restore basis by contributing additional capital to the entity, by the entity generating income (which increases your basis), or for S-corp shareholders, by personally lending money directly to the S-corp. Discuss the timing and mechanics with your advisor before making contributions solely for this purpose, since the entity structure and state law can affect whether and when the contribution increases basis.

### Phase 2: Apply the At-Risk Limitation

**Step 4. Complete Form 6198 for each applicable activity.**

Form 6198 applies to individuals, estates, trusts, and closely held C-corporations with losses from activities covered by Section 465. This includes most trades or businesses, farming, oil and gas, equipment leasing, and other activities. Note: for real estate activities, the at-risk rules apply to property placed in service after 1986.

Part I of Form 6198 calculates your profit or loss from the activity. Part II (simplified method) or Part III (full method) calculates your current at-risk amount. Part IV determines how much of the loss is deductible.

**Step 5. Calculate your at-risk amount.**

Your at-risk amount generally equals: cash and property you contributed to the activity, plus amounts you borrowed for the activity for which you are personally liable (recourse debt), plus any property (not used in the activity) you pledged as security for nonrecourse loans, plus, for real estate activities only, your share of qualified nonrecourse financing (bank or government nonrecourse mortgages on the real property, under Section 465(b)(6)).

Not at risk: any amount protected by a guarantee, stop-loss agreement, or similar arrangement; amounts borrowed from a person who has an interest in the activity or from a related party; and general nonrecourse debt (other than qualified nonrecourse financing for real estate).

**Step 6. Compare loss (after basis test) to at-risk amount.**

If the loss is less than or equal to your at-risk amount, the loss passes the at-risk test. Proceed to Step 8.

If the loss exceeds your at-risk amount, only the amount up to your at-risk amount is deductible this year. The excess suspends on Form 6198 and carries forward.

**Step 7. Track suspended at-risk losses.**

The carryover from Form 6198 is the amount to enter on next year's Form 6198. These suspended amounts are freed up in future years when your at-risk amount increases (through additional contributions, income, or repayment of nonrecourse debt that becomes recourse).

### Phase 3: Apply the Passive Activity Loss Rules

**Step 8. Determine whether each activity is passive or non-passive.**

An activity is passive if either: (a) it is a rental activity (generally passive by default, regardless of participation), or (b) it is a trade or business in which you do not materially participate.

Test each trade or business against the material participation tests (see Key Terms above). The most commonly used test is participation exceeding 500 hours during the year. Document your hours.

**Step 9. Complete Form 8582 to calculate allowable passive losses.**

List income and losses from all passive activities. Net passive income offsets passive losses. If you have net passive losses, they are generally suspended.

**Exception 1: The $25,000 rental real estate allowance (IRC Section 469(i)).**

If you actively participate in a rental real estate activity (see definition above), you may deduct up to $25,000 of net rental losses against non-passive income. This allowance phases out:

- Full $25,000 allowance: MAGI of $100,000 or less.
- Partial allowance: MAGI between $100,001 and $149,999. For every $2 of MAGI above $100,000, the $25,000 limit decreases by $1. Formula: $25,000 minus [50% x (MAGI - $100,000)].
- No allowance: MAGI of $150,000 or more.
- Married filing separately (living apart all year): Maximum is $12,500; phase-out begins at $50,000 MAGI and is fully eliminated at $75,000.

**Exception 2: Real estate professional (IRC Section 469(c)(7)).**

If you qualify as a real estate professional (more than 750 hours in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate, AND more than half your total working hours are in those activities), your rental activities are reclassified as non-passive. You may then deduct rental losses against any income without the $25,000 cap. You must still materially participate in each rental property (or elect to group all rental properties as a single activity). Document hours meticulously.

**Step 10. Carry forward suspended passive losses.**

Passive losses that are not allowed in the current year are suspended on Form 8582 and carried forward indefinitely. They become deductible when: (a) you generate passive income in a future year; (b) you dispose of your entire interest in the passive activity in a fully taxable transaction; or (c) the activity becomes non-passive.

### Phase 4: Apply the Excess Business Loss Cap

**Step 11. Calculate your aggregate net business loss (Form 461).**

After the first three filters, add up all business income and all business losses from all trades or businesses (sole proprietorships, S-corps, partnerships). If your total business losses exceed your total business income by more than the threshold amount, you have an excess business loss.

For tax year 2026, the threshold is $256,000 for single filers and $512,000 for married filing jointly (per Rev. Proc. 2025-32, Section 4.31, reflecting the OBBBA's reset of these amounts). Amounts above the threshold cannot be deducted in 2026. The excess automatically becomes an NOL carryforward to 2027.

This rule applies to individuals, estates, and trusts. It does not apply to C-corporations.

**Step 12. File Form 461 if your net business loss exceeds the threshold.**

The excess converts to an NOL carryforward, subject to the 80% rule described below.

### Phase 5: Net Operating Loss (NOL) Rules

**Step 13. Determine your NOL for the current year, if any.**

An NOL arises when your allowable business deductions exceed your income for the year, after all four prior filters. Calculate it using Form 172.

**Step 14. Apply the carryforward rules for post-2017 NOLs.**

NOLs arising from tax years beginning after December 31, 2017, may only be carried forward (not back, with the farming exception below). They can be carried forward indefinitely. However, in any year you use an NOL carryforward, your deduction is capped at 80% of your taxable income for that year (computed before the NOL deduction, the qualified business income deduction under Section 199A, and the Section 250 deduction). The remaining 20% of taxable income is taxable even if you have a large NOL carryforward.

Example: If your taxable income in a future year is $200,000 (before using the NOL), you can deduct at most $160,000 of your NOL carryforward (80% of $200,000), leaving $40,000 still taxable.

**Step 15. Apply the farming loss carryback exception if applicable.**

If the loss came from a farming business (a trade or business involving cultivation of land or raising agricultural or horticultural commodities), the farming loss portion may be carried back 2 years, allowing you to file an amended return and claim a refund for a prior year. Use Form 1045 (Application for Tentative Refund) within 1 year after the end of the NOL year, or Form 1040-X within 3 years of the due date of the NOL-year return.

**Step 16. Track NOL carryforwards by year of origin on Form 172.**

Keep a schedule showing: the tax year each NOL arose, whether it is pre-2018 or post-2017 (because the rules differ), the original amount, and how much has been used in each subsequent year. Pre-2018 NOLs are not subject to the 80% cap and may have carryback rights; post-2017 NOLs are subject to the 80% cap and have no carryback (except farming). Mixing them without tracking creates errors.

**Step 17. Deduct NOL carryforward on your return.**

Report the NOL deduction on Schedule 1 (Form 1040). Do not exceed the 80% cap for post-2017 losses. Attach Form 172 or the NOL carryover worksheet showing your calculation.

## Timing And Deadlines

**During the tax year (ongoing):**
- Maintain and update your activity hour log for each business and rental activity at least monthly. Courts have rejected logs reconstructed at year-end.
- Track basis in each pass-through interest after each K-1 is received or estimated.
- Review suspended loss carryovers quarterly so you can make informed decisions (additional contributions, disposition of activity) before year-end.

**Year-end planning (October through December):**
- Estimate whether any activity is likely to generate excess losses before year-end. If you are near the excess business loss threshold ($256,000 single/$512,000 joint for 2026), deferring income or accelerating deductions into or out of the year can affect how much of the loss is usable in the current year.
- Confirm real estate professional hour count before year-end. If you are approaching the 750-hour minimum, verify you will meet it and that your hours in real property trades exceed all other working hours.
- For the $25,000 rental exception, project your MAGI. If you will exceed $150,000, no rental loss exception is available for the current year.

**Filing season:**
- S-corp and partnership K-1s are due when the entity files its return, generally March 15 for calendar-year entities, with an extension to September 15. You cannot finalize your personal basis calculations or your Form 8582 or 6198 until K-1s are received.
- Your personal return (Form 1040) is due April 15, extendable to October 15. Filing an extension does not extend payment of tax owed.
- Form 1045 (NOL carryback for farming losses): within 1 year after the end of the NOL year.
- Form 1040-X (amended return for NOL carryback): within 3 years of the original due date of the NOL-year return.

**Annual cadence:**
- Every year you have pass-through losses: update Form 6198 (at-risk carryover), Form 8582 (passive loss carryover), and the NOL schedule.
- Every year you have a carryforward: confirm the carryforward amount on last year's return before filing the current year.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity type determines which forms you use:**

- S-corp shareholders: loss limited by stock and loan basis (Section 1366(d)) first, then at-risk (Section 465), then passive rules (Section 469), then excess business loss (Section 461(l)).
- Partners: loss limited by outside basis (Section 704(d)) first, then the same sequence.
- Sole proprietors: no separate basis limitation beyond the at-risk rules; proceed directly to at-risk, then passive (unusual since Schedule C activities are generally non-passive if you materially participate), then excess business loss.
- C-corporations: Section 469 passive activity rules apply differently (only closely held C-corps and personal service corporations face modified passive rules). The excess business loss limitation does not apply to C-corps. C-corps have their own NOL rules under Section 172 with an 80% cap on carryforward use.

**Self-employed individuals and payroll:** These rules affect income tax, not payroll tax. Suspended losses do not reduce self-employment tax (which applies to net earnings from self-employment). A loss that is suspended does not reduce your SE tax base for that year.

**State conformity (important):** Many states have their own passive activity and NOL rules that do not conform to federal law. Common state non-conformity issues include: (1) some states still allow NOL carrybacks that federal law no longer permits; (2) some states cap NOL carryforward periods shorter than the federal indefinite period; (3) some states do not recognize the real estate professional exception; (4) some states impose their own excess business loss rules or income limitations different from the federal $256,000/$512,000 thresholds. Verify your specific state's rules with your advisor. Do not assume that a loss allowed on your federal return is also allowed on your state return.

**OBBBA changes effective 2026:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21) made the excess business loss limitation permanent (previously set to expire after 2028) and also reset the thresholds to lower amounts starting in 2026. The Section 174A change allowing immediate deduction of domestic research and experimental expenditures may affect which activities carry at-risk or passive implications. Confirm with your advisor whether any of your activities involve research and development.

**When an activity generates both passive income and passive losses:** Net passive income from one passive activity can absorb passive losses from another passive activity in the same year. This netting happens on Form 8582 before any loss is suspended.

**Disposition of a passive activity:** When you sell or otherwise dispose of your entire interest in a passive activity in a fully taxable transaction, all suspended passive losses from that activity become immediately deductible against any income (not just passive income). This is often a powerful timing strategy for taxpayers with large suspended passive loss carryovers.

**At-risk amount can go below zero (but not be deducted below zero):** If you received a distribution that reduced your at-risk amount below zero, you may have to recapture previously deducted at-risk losses as income. This is tracked on Form 6198, Part II.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Not tracking hours contemporaneously.** Reconstructing an activity log at year-end from memory or a calendar is one of the most frequently challenged items in audits. The log must be created as you go, showing dates, descriptions of tasks, and hours.

2. **Confusing basis with at-risk amount.** Many taxpayers assume these are the same. They are not. Basis can include items (like your share of entity-level nonrecourse debt in a partnership) that are not included in your at-risk amount. Always apply both filters.

3. **Claiming the S-corp shareholder loan basis without a bona fide loan.** An S-corp shareholder gets loan basis only for money they personally loaned directly to the S-corp, documented as an actual loan (with a note and repayment terms). Guaranteeing a bank loan the S-corp took out does not create loan basis, even if you end up paying it.

4. **Forgetting that passive losses only free up on full disposition.** If you sell part of your interest in a passive activity (not 100%), suspended passive losses are not freed up. They remain suspended until you dispose of the entire interest or generate sufficient passive income.

5. **Treating the real estate professional election as automatic.** You must affirmatively establish both the 750-hour test and the more-than-half test every year. The election does not carry forward automatically. A year where you work fewer hours or shift to more non-real-estate work may knock you out of real estate professional status.

6. **Using a large NOL carryforward to zero out all taxable income.** Post-2017 NOLs can only offset 80% of taxable income per year. Using an amount that would reduce taxable income below 20% of the pre-NOL amount is an error and will result in a notice from the IRS.

7. **Ignoring the 2026 threshold drop for excess business losses.** The threshold dropped from $313,000 (single)/$626,000 (joint) in 2025 to $256,000 (single)/$512,000 (joint) in 2026 due to the OBBBA. Taxpayers who were under the 2025 threshold may now be over it.

8. **Filing the personal return before all K-1s arrive.** S-corp and partnership K-1s may arrive late (entities can extend to September 15). Filing your personal return based on estimated K-1 figures and then needing to amend is avoidable by extending your personal return.

9. **Omitting state-level analysis.** A federal passive loss or NOL deduction does not automatically translate to the same deduction on your state return. Failing to run the state analysis separately results in unexpected state tax bills.

10. **Not grouping rental properties when qualifying as a real estate professional.** Without a grouping election (made under Treas. Reg. 1.469-9(g)), each rental property is tested for material participation separately. If you have multiple rentals, you may not materially participate in each one individually, and the exception fails property by property. An election to group all rental real estate interests as one activity (made on an original timely-filed return) can solve this problem. This election is generally irrevocable once made; discuss with your advisor before electing.

## Advisor Review Gates

Do not proceed without first reviewing the following decisions with your advisor:

1. **Before claiming real estate professional status:** Confirm your hour count, that more than half your working hours are in real property trades, that you are materially participating in the rental activities, and whether a grouping election is needed. A mistake here converts significant deductions into suspended losses.

2. **Before making an additional capital contribution to restore basis:** Confirm the contribution will actually increase your basis for loss deduction purposes in the entity structure you are using, and that you are not inadvertently triggering other tax consequences (like a distribution).

3. **Before personally lending money to your S-corporation to create loan basis:** The loan must be genuine (documented promissory note, arms-length interest rate, realistic repayment terms). Confirm the structure qualifies as bona fide debt with your advisor.

4. **Before disposing of a passive activity to release suspended losses:** Confirm the disposition is a fully taxable transaction (installment sales do not always release all losses immediately), that the entire interest is being disposed of, and that you understand the state tax consequences.

5. **Before using an NOL carryforward:** Calculate the 80% limit for the year. Using more than 80% of taxable income is an error. Confirm which year each NOL arose to apply the correct rules (pre-2018 NOLs have no 80% cap).

6. **Before filing for an NOL carryback (farming losses):** Confirm the activity qualifies as a farming business, calculate the refund, and confirm state conformity before filing Form 1045.

7. **Before grouping rental properties as one activity:** This election is generally irrevocable. Review the long-term implications before making it on your return.

8. **Whenever your MAGI is near $100,000 or $150,000:** The rental real estate exception phases out between those levels. A small increase in income can eliminate thousands of dollars of deductions. Year-end income management may be available.

9. **Whenever your net business losses are near $256,000 (single) or $512,000 (joint) for 2026:** Excess losses above those thresholds become an NOL carryforward, not a current deduction, and are subject to the 80% annual cap when used. Plan year-end income and deductions accordingly.

10. **Whenever state law differs from federal:** Your advisor should confirm state-specific passive loss, NOL, and excess business loss treatment for every state where you file.

## Before You Act

This playbook teaches the mechanics of four loss limitation rules and the NOL system. It is not a substitute for a review of your specific facts. Before you claim a deduction based on any of these rules, file any of the forms described here, make an election (such as the grouping election), make a capital contribution to restore basis, or take any other action described above, confirm your specific facts with your advisor. The dollar thresholds, phase-out ranges, and form requirements described here reflect the law as of 2026 but may change in future years. State law may differ materially from federal law.
