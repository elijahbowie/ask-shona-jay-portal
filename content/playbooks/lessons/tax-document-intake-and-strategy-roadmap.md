# Tax Document Intake and Strategy Roadmap

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Every effective tax strategy depends on one thing that often gets skipped: a complete, organized set of documents gathered at the right time. This playbook walks you through exactly which documents to collect, where to find them, how to organize them, and how to use them to build or refine your personal tax strategy roadmap. The process has two phases: intake (gathering every document your advisor needs to work from) and roadmap (mapping what you find in those documents to the strategies available at your income level and entity type). The attached download, the Tax Document Intake and Strategy Roadmap Implementation Worksheet, is the fill-in tool you will complete as you move through each step.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook applies to you if:

- You own a business or have self-employment income and want to make sure your advisor has everything they need to prepare your return and build your strategy plan.
- You are a new Beyond Freedom Financial client completing your initial intake or a returning client starting a new tax year.
- You have received income from multiple sources (a business, a W-2 job, rental property, investments, or a side hustle) and want to make sure nothing is missed before filing.
- You want a clear roadmap showing which tax-saving strategies are available to you based on your specific income, entity type, and filing situation (not a generic list, but one matched to your actual numbers).
- You are preparing for a planning meeting with your advisor and want to arrive with organized, complete information so the meeting can focus on strategy rather than document hunting.

This playbook does not replace the individual strategy playbooks. It is the gateway: after completing it, you will know which strategy playbooks to use next.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **You have not yet started earning income from a business or self-employment.** If your only income is a W-2 and you have no business, side hustle, or rental property, most of the strategy-mapping sections will not apply. Return to this playbook when business or self-employment income begins.
- **You are in active IRS collection or audit proceedings.** Intake in that situation requires a different document list and a different sequence. Your advisor will guide you through that process separately before returning to proactive strategy planning.
- **Your return is already prepared and filed.** This playbook is most valuable as a before-filing or between-filings process. If the current year's return is already filed, use it as the starting point for the next year's intake.
- **You have a C-corporation as your primary business entity.** C-corporation returns involve entity-level tax, dividends, and retained earnings rules that are beyond the scope of this intake playbook. Your advisor will direct you to C-corp specific guidance.

## Key Terms

**Adjusted Gross Income (AGI):** Your total gross income minus specific above-the-line deductions such as contributions to a traditional IRA, self-employed health insurance premiums, half of self-employment tax, and HSA contributions. AGI appears on line 11 of Form 1040 and is the starting point for calculating many deductions and credits. Several strategy thresholds (QBID, Roth IRA, HSA) are measured against AGI or a close variant called MAGI.

**Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI):** AGI with certain deductions added back. The IRS uses MAGI (not AGI) to test eligibility for Roth IRA contributions, the QBI deduction phase-out, the SALT deduction phase-out, the Net Investment Income Tax, and several other provisions. Your MAGI and AGI are often close but not identical; your tax software or advisor will calculate it.

**Taxable income:** AGI minus the standard deduction (or itemized deductions) and any qualified business income deduction (QBID). This is the number the tax brackets are actually applied to. It appears on line 15 of Form 1040.

**Effective tax rate:** Total federal income tax divided by total taxable income. This is the average rate you paid on all taxable income. It is always lower than your marginal rate and gives you a realistic picture of your overall federal tax burden.

**Marginal tax rate:** The rate applied to your next dollar of income. If your taxable income puts you in the 24% bracket, you pay 24 cents of federal income tax on each additional dollar of income you earn and save 24 cents for each dollar of legitimate deduction you take. Strategies that reduce income in a higher bracket produce greater savings per dollar.

**Pass-through entity:** A business structure (sole proprietorship, single-member LLC, multi-member LLC, S-corporation, or partnership) where business income is not taxed at the entity level. Instead, it passes through to the owner's personal Form 1040 and is taxed at the owner's individual rates.

**S-corporation (S-corp):** A corporation (or LLC that has elected to be treated as a corporation and then elected S-corp status) that has made an election under IRC Section 1362. The S-corp itself does not pay federal income tax (with limited exceptions). Instead, income and losses pass to the owner-employees through Schedule K-1 and appear on their Form 1040. An S-corp owner who works in the business must pay themselves reasonable compensation (a W-2 salary) and may then take additional profit as distributions not subject to self-employment or FICA taxes.

**Sole proprietorship:** A business owned and operated by one individual with no separate legal entity. Income and expenses are reported on Schedule C (Form 1040). Net profit is subject to both income tax and self-employment tax. No separate business tax return is required.

**Self-employment (SE) tax:** The 15.3% tax that self-employed individuals pay on net earnings in place of the employee-and-employer shares of Social Security and Medicare (FICA) taxes. It consists of 12.4% Social Security on net earnings up to the 2026 Social Security wage base of $184,500, and 2.9% Medicare on all net earnings. You may deduct half of SE tax paid as an above-the-line deduction on Form 1040. S-corp profit distributions are not subject to SE tax; only the W-2 salary portion is subject to FICA.

**Reasonable compensation:** The W-2 salary that the IRS expects an S-corp owner-employee to pay themselves for the services they perform in the business. It must be comparable to what an unrelated employer would pay someone to do the same work. Paying compensation that is too low is the most frequently audited S-corp issue.

**Qualified Business Income (QBI):** The net amount of income, gain, deduction, and loss from a qualified trade or business conducted within the United States. W-2 wages you pay yourself, capital gains, and investment income are excluded from QBI. Calculated on IRS Form 8995 or Form 8995-A.

**Qualified Business Income Deduction (QBID):** A deduction under IRC Section 199A that allows eligible owners of pass-through businesses to deduct up to 20% of their QBI from taxable income, reducing the income tax rate on business profits without affecting self-employment tax. Made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025). A minimum deduction of $400 applies if QBI is at least $1,000 and you materially participate.

**Specified Service Trade or Business (SSTB):** A category of professional service businesses (including health, law, consulting, financial services, athletics, brokerage, and performing arts) whose QBID is phased out and then eliminated at higher income levels. Architecture and engineering are excluded from the SSTB category. Your advisor will confirm whether your business qualifies as an SSTB.

**Standard deduction:** A flat dollar amount that reduces your taxable income, available to taxpayers who do not itemize. For tax year 2026: $32,200 for married filing jointly, $16,100 for single filers, and $24,150 for head of household. You compare the standard deduction to your total itemized deductions and use whichever is larger.

**Itemized deductions:** Specific deductions listed on Schedule A (Form 1040) such as mortgage interest, state and local taxes (capped at $40,400 for most filers in 2026), charitable contributions, and qualifying medical expenses. You itemize only when your total Schedule A deductions exceed the standard deduction for your filing status.

**Above-the-line deduction:** A deduction taken before AGI is calculated. Examples include the deduction for half of self-employment tax, self-employed health insurance premiums, HSA contributions, alimony (pre-2019 agreements), and contributions to a traditional IRA. These reduce AGI and are more broadly available than itemized deductions.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The form used by sole proprietors and single-member LLCs to report business income and expenses. Net profit from Schedule C flows to Form 1040 and is subject to both income tax and self-employment tax.

**Schedule E (Form 1040):** Used to report income or loss from rental real estate, partnerships, S-corporations, trusts, and royalties. Rental income and losses and your share of S-corp or partnership income from Schedule K-1 appear here.

**Schedule K-1:** The form that flows each partner's or S-corp shareholder's share of income, loss, deductions, and credits to their personal Form 1040. It is the bridge between the entity-level return and the owner's personal return. You receive one K-1 for each pass-through entity in which you hold an interest.

**Form 1120-S:** The U.S. Income Tax Return for an S-corporation. Filed annually by March 15 (calendar year entities); generates Schedule K-1 forms for each shareholder.

**Form 1065:** The U.S. Return of Partnership Income. Filed annually by March 15 (calendar year entities); generates Schedule K-1 forms for each partner.

**Form W-2:** Wage and Tax Statement. Issued by an employer to each employee by January 31 (for wages paid in the prior calendar year). Reports wages paid, federal and state income tax withheld, Social Security and Medicare taxes withheld, and the value of certain employer-provided benefits.

**Form 1099-NEC:** Nonemployee Compensation. Issued to independent contractors who were paid $600 or more during the calendar year. Due to the recipient by January 31.

**Form 1099-MISC:** Used to report miscellaneous payments such as rents, prizes, and royalties paid of $600 or more, and direct sales of consumer goods of $5,000 or more. Due to the recipient by January 31 for most boxes.

**Form 1099-K:** Payment Card and Third Party Network Transactions. Issued by payment processors (PayPal, Stripe, Venmo for Business, Square, etc.) when you receive payments through their platforms. Reports gross payment receipts; does not subtract your business expenses. For 2025 and later years, a payment processor is required to issue Form 1099-K only when gross payments to you exceed $20,000 AND the number of transactions exceeds 200 (the One Big Beautiful Bill Act restored this pre-2021 threshold, reversing the $600 phase-in; see IRS IR-2025-107). A processor may still issue a 1099-K below the threshold voluntarily.

**Form 1099-INT / 1099-DIV:** Report interest income (from bank accounts, savings bonds, CDs) and dividend income (from stocks and mutual funds), respectively. Issued by the paying financial institution.

**Form 1099-R:** Reports distributions from retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s, pensions). Issued by the plan custodian.

**Form 1098:** Mortgage Interest Statement. Issued by your lender showing the mortgage interest and mortgage insurance premiums you paid during the year, which may be deductible on Schedule A.

**Form W-9:** Request for Taxpayer Identification Number and Certification. Not filed with the IRS. Completed by contractors and service providers and given to the hiring business so the business can issue a correct Form 1099. If you hire contractors, you collect W-9s from them.

**Health Savings Account (HSA):** A tax-advantaged savings account available only to individuals enrolled in a qualifying High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP). Contributions are deductible (or pre-tax if made through payroll), investment growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. Governed by IRC Section 223.

**High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP):** A health insurance plan that meets IRS-defined minimums for annual deductibles and maximums for out-of-pocket costs. Required for HSA eligibility. For 2026: minimum annual deductible is $1,700 (self-only coverage) or $3,400 (family coverage); maximum out-of-pocket is $8,500 (self-only) or $17,000 (family).

**Accountable plan:** A written employer reimbursement policy that meets IRS requirements under Treasury Regulation 1.62-2. Allows a business to reimburse an employee (including an owner-employee) for qualified business expenses without including those reimbursements in taxable wages. Requires a written plan document, business-purpose documentation, adequate accounting, and return of excess amounts.

**Estimated tax:** Quarterly prepayments of income and self-employment tax on income not subject to employer withholding. Required when you expect to owe $1,000 or more after withholding. Paid four times per year via IRS Direct Pay (directpay.irs.gov) using Form 1040-ES.

**Strategy roadmap:** For purposes of this playbook, a strategy roadmap is a one-page written summary (completed in the worksheet) showing which tax-saving strategies apply to your facts, what the estimated savings from each strategy are, and what the implementation deadline is for each. It is the output of the intake process.

**Statute of limitations (for tax purposes):** The window during which the IRS can assess additional tax on a return or during which you can amend a return for a refund. The general period is 3 years from the return due date or filing date, whichever is later. It extends to 6 years if you omit more than 25% of gross income. There is no limit if fraud is involved or if no return was filed.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following facts before starting the intake. Write each answer in the corresponding section of the Tax Document Intake and Strategy Roadmap Implementation Worksheet.

1. **Your filing status for 2026:** Single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, head of household, or qualifying surviving spouse. Filing status determines your standard deduction, tax bracket thresholds, and several phase-out ranges.

2. **Your business entity structure:** Sole proprietorship (files Schedule C), single-member LLC (typically files Schedule C unless an election was made), multi-member LLC or partnership (files Form 1065), S-corporation (files Form 1120-S), C-corporation (files Form 1120). If you are unsure, ask your advisor: the entity type determines which strategies are available and which forms you need.

3. **Estimated 2026 gross revenue and net profit:** Your best current estimate. If you are midyear, use year-to-date actuals plus a projection for the remainder of the year. You need this to assess whether your income puts you above or below the thresholds for key strategies such as the QBID phase-out, Roth IRA eligibility, and the need for S-corp election.

4. **Whether you currently run payroll:** If you have an S-corp, you must be on payroll. If you hire employees or pay contractors, note that separately. If you pay family members (spouse, children), note their ages and roles.

5. **Whether you have a High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP) and whether you have an HSA:** Both must be true to contribute to an HSA for 2026. If you have HDHP coverage but no HSA account, opening one is a potential immediate action.

6. **Your state(s) of residence and states where the business earns income:** State conformity to federal tax rules varies significantly. Your advisor will confirm state-specific implications after reviewing the intake documents.

7. **Whether you own rental property:** Rental income is reported on Schedule E. Rental property may also be subject to depreciation, cost segregation, and passive activity rules that affect your overall strategy.

8. **Any major anticipated transactions in 2026:** Sale of a business, sale of real estate or investments, receipt of an inheritance, large equipment purchase, hiring of employees, change of entity structure. These create strategy opportunities and deadlines.

9. **Whether you received any IRS notices in the past 12 months or have any unfiled prior-year returns:** These issues take priority before strategy planning. Note them on the worksheet so your advisor can address them first.

## Required Documents And Records

Organize the following documents into the categories shown. For each document, note on the worksheet whether you have it (Present), need to request it (Pending), or believe it does not apply to you (N/A). Bring all Present documents (or digital copies) to your advisor meeting.

### Category 1: Income Documents (All Sources)

| Document | Who Issues It | When Available |
|---|---|---|
| Form W-2 (wages from employment) | Each employer | By January 31 |
| Form 1099-NEC (nonemployee compensation $600+) | Each business that paid you | By January 31 |
| Form 1099-MISC (rents, royalties, prizes $600+) | Each business that paid you | By January 31 |
| Form 1099-K (payment processor receipts) | PayPal, Stripe, Square, etc. | By January 31 |
| Form 1099-INT (bank interest) | Banks, credit unions | By January 31 |
| Form 1099-DIV (dividends) | Brokerages, mutual funds | By January 31 |
| Form 1099-R (retirement distributions) | IRA/401(k) custodian | By January 31 |
| Form 1099-S (real estate proceeds) | Settlement agent | By February 15 |
| Form 1099-B (brokerage sales proceeds) | Brokerage | By February 15 |
| Schedule K-1 (partnership or S-corp income) | Each entity you own or invest in | By March 15 (can be later if entity files extension) |
| Business bank statements (all accounts, all months) | Your bank | Available on request |
| Invoices or billing records showing gross revenue | Your records | Ongoing |

**Important note on Form 1099-K:** Payment processors report gross receipts (the full amount paid to you before any refunds, fees, or business expenses are subtracted). The 1099-K total is not your taxable profit. Your actual taxable profit is gross receipts minus allowable business expenses.

### Category 3: Entity and Payroll Records

| Document | What It Proves |
|---|---|
| IRS S-corp election acceptance letter (Form 2553) | That your S-corp election is in effect and was accepted by the IRS |
| Articles of organization or incorporation | Entity formation date and structure |
| Operating agreement or shareholder agreement | Ownership percentages and governance |
| Prior-year Form 1120-S or Form 1065 with all schedules | Last year's entity-level income, deductions, officer compensation |
| Schedule K-1 from each entity (current and prior year) | Your share of pass-through income |

| W-2s you received as an S-corp employee-owner | Your own wages from the S-corp |

### Category 4: Prior-Year Returns and IRS Account

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Prior-year Form 1040 with all schedules (at minimum the last 3 years; 7 is ideal) | Establishes your income history, carryovers, and prior positions |
| Prior-year state income tax return(s) | Shows state taxes paid (relevant to SALT deduction) and any state carryovers |
| IRS tax transcripts (Account Transcript, Wage and Income Transcript) | Confirms what the IRS has on file for you; catches missing 1099s; shows estimated tax payments recorded |
| Estimated tax payment confirmations from IRS Direct Pay or EFTPS | Supports credit for prepayments on your return |
| Any IRS notices received in the past 3 years | Must be addressed before or alongside filing |
| Prior-year extension requests (Form 4868 or Form 7004) | Confirms prior filing deadlines were properly extended |

**How to access IRS transcripts:** Go to irs.gov/account and create or log into your IRS Online Account. Under the "Records and Transcripts" section, request your Account Transcript (shows payments, penalties, and assessments) and your Wage and Income Transcript (shows every information return, including W-2s and 1099s, the IRS received for you in that tax year). The Wage and Income Transcript is especially useful for catching 1099s you may have forgotten.

### Category 5: Property and Investment Records

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Rental property income and expense records (by property) | Schedule E reporting; supports depreciation deduction |

| Mortgage interest statement (Form 1098) | Supports Schedule A mortgage interest deduction |

### Category 6: Retirement and Health Benefit Records

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Retirement plan contribution statements (401k, SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA) | Supports deduction; confirms you did not exceed annual limits |
| IRA contribution records (traditional and Roth) | Confirms contribution amounts and years |
| Form 5498 (IRA contributions reported by the custodian) | May arrive after April 15 but confirms contribution amounts |
| HSA contribution records (Form 5498-SA from HSA trustee) | Confirms total contributions; supports Form 8889 |
| HSA Form 1099-SA (HSA distributions) | Reports withdrawals from HSA; must be matched to qualified medical expenses |
| Proof of HDHP enrollment (insurance card, plan summary, or employer benefits statement) | Required to claim HSA contributions; confirms eligibility each month |
| Health insurance premium invoices (if self-employed or S-corp shareholder) | Supports self-employed health insurance deduction |

**Retention guidance:** Keep all documents in Categories 1 through 6 for at least 7 years from the return due date for the year to which they relate. Keep property records (Category 5) until you dispose of the property, plus 7 years. Keep entity formation documents (Category 3), S-corp election letters, and all filed returns permanently.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Work through the steps in order. Record your entries in the Tax Document Intake and Strategy Roadmap Implementation Worksheet as you go.

### Phase 1: Gather Your Documents

**Step 1: Download your IRS Wage and Income Transcript.**
Go to irs.gov/account, log in or create an account, and download your Wage and Income Transcript for the most recent completed tax year. Print it or save it as a PDF. This transcript lists every Form W-2, 1099-NEC, 1099-MISC, 1099-INT, 1099-DIV, 1099-R, and 1099-K the IRS received for you. Compare it against the income documents you have in hand. If the transcript shows a 1099 you do not have, contact the issuer to request a copy.

**Step 2: Collect every income document in Category 1.**
Use the checklist in the worksheet. For each document type, mark it Present, Pending, or N/A. Do not skip this step for sources that seem small. Even $50 in unreported 1099 income can trigger an IRS notice. If you are past January 31 and a W-2 or 1099 has not arrived, contact the issuer directly. If the issuer cannot provide it, your advisor can use your IRS transcript as a substitute.

**Step 3: Collect your prior-year federal return and entity returns.**
Pull your most recent filed Form 1040 and, if applicable, your most recent Form 1120-S or Form 1065 with all schedules. From your Form 1040, note on the worksheet: (a) total federal income tax (line 24), (b) AGI (line 11), (c) taxable income (line 15), (d) filing status, and (e) whether you took the standard deduction or itemized. From your entity return, note: gross revenue, officer compensation paid (Box 7 of Form 1120-S or Line 10 of Schedule K-1), and net ordinary business income.

**Step 4: Gather business expense documentation for the current year.**
Work through Category 2. Pull business bank statements and credit card statements for every month of the year. Export a transaction report from your bookkeeping software (QuickBooks, Wave, Xero, or similar) if you use one. If you do not use bookkeeping software, sort your bank and card statements by expense category. Look specifically for: mileage logs, home office measurements, accountable plan reimbursement requests, receipts for equipment or technology purchases over $500, and receipts for business meals with the attendees and business purpose noted.

**Step 5: Verify entity and payroll documents are complete.**
If you have an S-corp, confirm that you have: the IRS Form 2553 acceptance letter, payroll tax returns (Form 941 for each quarter and Form 940 annual), and W-2s showing your wages. If any of these are missing, contact your payroll provider or your state's business filing office. Note gaps on the worksheet.

**Step 6: Gather property, investment, and benefit documents.**
Complete Categories 5 and 6. For each rental property, pull income and expense records by property. For investments, pull brokerage statements showing proceeds and cost basis for any securities sold. For HSA: pull Form 5498-SA (HSA contributions) and Form 1099-SA (HSA withdrawals) from your HSA trustee. Confirm you have proof of HDHP enrollment for every month you contributed to the HSA.

### Phase 2: Organize and Cross-Check

**Step 7: Create one folder per document category.**
Whether physical or digital, create six folders matching the six categories above. Place each document in the correct folder. A digital setup (a cloud folder shared with your advisor, with subfolders) is faster and eliminates the risk of lost originals. Name the folders exactly as listed: Income Documents, Business Expense Documents, Entity and Payroll Records, Prior-Year Returns and IRS Account, Property and Investment Records, Retirement and Health Benefit Records.

**Step 8: Cross-check total income on the IRS transcript against all sources you have gathered.**
On the worksheet, list every income source from your transcript and confirm you have a corresponding document in your Income Documents folder. If the transcript shows income from a source you cannot identify, flag it for your advisor. It may be a reporting error, a forgotten account, or a sign of identity theft.

**Step 9: Identify any missing documents and create a "Pending" action list.**
For each document marked Pending in your worksheet, write: (a) what the document is, (b) who you need to contact to get it, and (c) the deadline by which you need it before your advisor meeting. Resolve all Pending items before the meeting when possible.

**Step 10: Confirm your IRS estimated tax payment records match what you actually paid.**
Pull your estimated tax payment confirmations from IRS Direct Pay or EFTPS. Then pull your IRS Account Transcript, which shows all payments the IRS has received. Confirm that every payment you made appears on the transcript. If a payment is missing from the transcript but you have the confirmation number, save the confirmation as proof and flag it for your advisor.

### Phase 3: Build Your Strategy Roadmap

**Step 11: Record your current-year financial position on the worksheet.**
Fill in the following fields in the Strategy Roadmap section of the worksheet:
- Filing status
- Entity structure
- Estimated 2026 gross revenue
- Estimated 2026 net business income (after expenses)
- Estimated 2026 adjusted gross income
- Current marginal federal income tax bracket (see "The Numbers" section)
- Estimated 2026 self-employment tax (if applicable)
- Whether you currently itemize or take the standard deduction

This financial snapshot is what determines which strategies apply at your income level.

**Step 12: Match your situation to the available strategy tiers.**
Use the three-tier framework below. Identify which tier you fall into based on your estimated 2026 net business income, then review the strategies listed for that tier. Check off each strategy on the worksheet as Implemented, Eligible but Not Implemented, or Not Applicable.

**Tier 1: Net business income under $50,000 (sole proprietor or single-member LLC)**

Priority strategies at this income level:
- Maximize above-the-line deductions: traditional IRA contribution ($7,500 if under age 50; $8,600 if 50 or older), HSA contribution (if HDHP enrolled), and half of self-employment tax deduction (calculated on Schedule SE).
- Track and deduct all legitimate business expenses on Schedule C (home office, vehicle, equipment, professional development, subscriptions, insurance).
- If you pay contractors, confirm Form W-9 is on file for each one and that 1099-NEC forms will be issued by January 31.
- QBID (Section 199A): Available. Your income is likely well below the phase-out threshold. Up to 20% of net QBI is deductible. Claim on Form 8995.
- S-corp election: May not be cost-effective at this income level given the cost of payroll compliance. Ask your advisor whether your income trajectory justifies it.

**Tier 2: Net business income $50,000 to $200,000 (any entity type)**

All Tier 1 strategies apply, plus:
- **S-corp election:** If you are currently a sole proprietor or single-member LLC and net profit consistently exceeds $40,000 to $50,000, an S-corp election may save a significant amount in self-employment and FICA taxes by allowing profit above reasonable compensation to be taken as a distribution rather than wages. Discuss with your advisor; the election generally must be filed by March 15 to be effective for the current tax year, though late election relief under IRS Revenue Procedure 2013-30 may be available.
- **Accountable plan:** Formalize an accountable plan policy to reimburse yourself tax-free for home office, mileage, cell phone, equipment, and other legitimate business expenses.
- **Augusta Rule (IRC Section 280A(g)):** If you own a home and hold qualifying business meetings there, your business can rent your home for up to 14 days per year at market rates. The rent is a business deduction and you receive that income tax-free. Requires a written rental agreement and documented meeting agendas.
- **Solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA:** At this income level, a solo 401(k) often allows larger contributions than a SEP-IRA because it accepts both employee elective deferrals ($24,500 in 2026, plus catch-up) and employer contributions (up to 25% of compensation), for a combined limit of $72,000 (or $80,000 with catch-up contributions at age 50+). A SEP-IRA accepts employer contributions only. For a self-employed sole proprietor the effective deductible contribution is approximately 20% of net self-employment earnings (because the IRS calculation requires adjusting for SE tax and the circular nature of the contribution itself; the stated 25% rate applies to W-2 compensation, not directly to net self-employment income). The dollar cap is $72,000. Use the worksheet in IRS Publication 560 to calculate your exact SEP-IRA contribution limit.
- **Hire family members:** If children ages 7 to 17 perform genuine services for the business, sole proprietors and qualifying partnerships can pay them wages, which the business deducts and the child pays tax on at their own (lower) rate. A dependent child's standard deduction for 2026 is the greater of $1,350 or the child's earned income plus $450, capped at the single standard deduction of $16,100. In practice, a child with only wage income from the business and no other income can earn up to $16,100 in 2026 before owing any federal income tax. Wages paid by a sole proprietor to a child under 18 are exempt from Social Security and Medicare taxes. Confirm the exact figure with your advisor under IRC Section 63(c)(5) and Rev. Proc. 2025-32.
- **HSA maximization:** If enrolled in an HDHP, maximize HSA contributions ($4,400 self-only or $8,750 family for 2026, plus $1,000 catch-up at age 55+).

**Tier 3: Net business income above $200,000 (any entity type)**

All Tier 1 and Tier 2 strategies apply, plus:
- **QBID phase-out awareness:** The QBID begins phasing out once taxable income exceeds $403,500 (married filing jointly) or $201,750 (all other filers). For SSTB owners, the deduction is fully eliminated at taxable income above $553,500 (MFJ) or $276,750 (others). Strategies that reduce taxable income below these thresholds (retirement plan contributions, timing of income, etc.) may preserve a QBID you would otherwise lose.
- **Defined benefit plan:** At high income levels, a solo defined benefit plan allows annual contributions significantly larger than a 401(k) or SEP-IRA, potentially deducting $100,000 to $200,000 or more per year depending on your age and income. Requires an enrolled actuary to set contribution amounts. Contact your advisor before establishing one.
- **Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** If your MAGI exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (MFJ), passive income (rental income, interest, dividends, capital gains) is subject to an additional 3.8% NIIT. Strategies to reduce NIIT exposure include ensuring rental activities qualify as non-passive (real estate professional status) and structuring investments in tax-advantaged accounts.
- **Cost segregation:** If you own commercial real estate or residential rental property and have not had a cost segregation study, a study reclassifies building components into shorter depreciation lives, accelerating deductions. This is particularly valuable when combined with the current 100% bonus depreciation rate for qualifying property placed in service after January 19, 2025.
- **SALT deduction planning:** The state and local tax (SALT) deduction is capped at $40,400 for most filers in 2026. If you live in a high-income-tax state, a pass-through entity tax (PTET) election at the entity level may allow your S-corp or partnership to pay state income taxes at the entity level, deducting them as a business expense and bypassing the individual SALT cap. Confirm with your advisor whether your state offers a PTET election and whether it is beneficial in your situation.
- **Timing and income-shifting strategies:** At higher incomes, the marginal rate is 32% to 37% federally. Deferring income into a lower-income year (or accelerating deductions into a higher-income year) produces larger savings. This includes timing of large equipment purchases (Section 179 or bonus depreciation), retirement plan contributions, and optional income.

**Step 13: For each Eligible but Not Implemented strategy, estimate the potential savings.**
For each strategy you checked as eligible but not yet implemented, write a rough savings estimate in the worksheet using this approach:

- Estimated savings from a deduction or exclusion = dollar amount of the deduction multiplied by your marginal tax rate. Example: $10,000 deduction at 24% marginal rate = $2,400 in federal income tax savings.
- Estimated FICA/SE tax savings from S-corp distributions = distributions above reasonable compensation multiplied by 15.3% (the SE tax rate). Example: $50,000 in distributions above salary x 15.3% = $7,650 potential SE tax savings.
- Estimated QBID savings = net QBI x 20% x marginal rate. Example: $150,000 QBI x 20% = $30,000 deduction x 24% = $7,200 in tax savings.

These are rough estimates for prioritization. Your advisor will calculate exact figures.

**Step 14: Rank eligible-but-not-implemented strategies by deadline sensitivity and estimated savings.**
Not all strategies can be implemented after December 31. Place the following in your calendar before reviewing with your advisor:

- S-corp election for current tax year: generally March 15 (or within 75 days of entity formation for a new entity).
- Solo 401(k) plan establishment: December 31 of the tax year (contributions may follow by the extended return due date).
- Augusta Rule rental days: all 14 must occur before December 31.
- Accountable plan reimbursements: under the safe-harbor "fixed date" method in Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g), submitting reimbursement requests within 60 days of the expense is treated as within a reasonable period of time. This is a safe harbor; the regulation's actual standard is "a reasonable period of time" based on facts and circumstances. Your plan document should specify a submission deadline; 60 days is the most commonly used figure because it falls squarely within the safe harbor.
- HSA contributions: by April 15 of the following year for the prior tax year.
- SEP-IRA contributions: by the extended due date of the return (October 15 for individuals who file an extension using Form 4868).
- Traditional IRA contributions: by April 15 of the following year (extensions do not extend the IRA contribution deadline).

Order your strategy list: deadline-sensitive items first, then highest estimated savings.

**Step 15: Complete the Strategy Roadmap section of the worksheet.**
The finished roadmap is a table with five columns: Strategy Name, Current Status (Implemented/Eligible/N/A), Estimated Annual Savings, Implementation Deadline, and Next Action Required. Fill in one row per strategy. This is the output you will bring to your advisor meeting.

### Phase 4: Advisor Handoff

**Step 16: Organize your documents for the advisor meeting.**
Before the meeting: (a) confirm all six document folders are complete and all Pending items are resolved or flagged; (b) print or send digital access to the completed worksheet; (c) write a brief summary (one paragraph is enough) describing any unusual events in the current year: a sale, a new hire, a major purchase, a change in business structure, receipt of an inheritance, etc. Unusual events often create both tax risks and planning opportunities your advisor needs to know about.

**Step 17: At the meeting, work through the roadmap together.**
Go through the Strategy Roadmap section of the worksheet with your advisor row by row. For each Eligible but Not Implemented strategy, get a clear answer: Is this strategy available to me given my specific facts? What is the exact implementation step? What is the deadline? Who is responsible (you, your advisor, your bookkeeper, your payroll provider)?

**Step 18: After the meeting, update the worksheet with confirmed actions and deadlines.**
Record every action item that came out of the meeting. Write the responsible party's name, the specific deliverable, and the due date for each item. Keep a copy in your tax vault. Schedule a follow-up if items remain open.

## Timing And Deadlines

**Ongoing throughout the year:**
- Keep expense receipts and note the business purpose at the time of purchase, not after the fact.
- Log mileage contemporaneously (date, destination, miles, business purpose). Mileage logs reconstructed after the fact are weak documentation.
- Collect W-9s from contractors before you pay them, not at year-end when you are trying to issue 1099s.

**January deadlines:**
- January 31: Employers must provide Form W-2 to employees and Form 1099-NEC to contractors (for 2025 calendar year wages and payments). If you are an employer or business payer, this is your deadline to send these documents. If you are a recipient, this is the deadline by which you should receive them.

**February deadlines:**
- February 2, 2026 (for tax year 2025 documents): Due date for employers to furnish W-2s and 1099-NECs for wages and nonemployee compensation paid in 2025. (Note: February 2 rather than January 31 because January 31, 2026 falls on a Saturday.)
- February 17, 2026: Due date for furnishing Form 1099-B and 1099-S to recipients.

**March deadlines:**
- March 15: S-corp (Form 1120-S) and partnership (Form 1065) returns due for calendar year entities. Schedule K-1 must reach shareholders and partners by this date. File Form 7004 to request a 6-month extension to September 15.
- March 15: General deadline for a calendar-year LLC or corporation to make an S-corp election (Form 2553) effective for the current tax year.

**April deadlines:**
- April 15: Individual Form 1040 due. File Form 4868 to request an automatic 6-month extension to October 15. An extension extends the filing deadline but NOT the payment deadline. If you will owe tax, estimate it and pay it by April 15 to avoid penalties.
- April 15: Q1 estimated tax payment due for tax year 2026.
- April 15: Deadline for traditional IRA and Roth IRA contributions for prior tax year. Extensions do not extend this deadline.

**June deadline:**
- June 15: Q2 estimated tax payment due.

**September deadlines:**
- September 15: Q3 estimated tax payment due.
- September 15: Extended S-corp (Form 1120-S) and partnership (Form 1065) returns due.

**October deadline:**
- October 15: Extended individual Form 1040 due.
- October 15: Deadline for SEP-IRA contributions for prior tax year (if you filed an extension). No extension is available for this deadline beyond the extended return date.
- October 15: Solo 401(k) contributions for prior year (employee and employer portions) may also be made by this date if the return is on extension. The plan itself must have been established by December 31 of the prior year.

**December 31 year-end cutoffs:**
- Last day for Augusta Rule rental days.
- Last day to establish a solo 401(k) plan for the current year.
- Last day for any qualified business expense to be incurred and paid if it is to be deducted in the current year.
- Last day to run final payroll (including for child employees) that counts for the current year.

**January 15 (following year):**
- Q4 estimated tax payment due (for calendar year 2026, the Q4 payment is due January 15, 2027).

### Self-Employment Tax (Tax Year 2026)

- **Rate:** 15.3% total (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare)
- **Social Security wage base:** $184,500. Only net earnings up to $184,500 are subject to the 12.4% Social Security portion. Net earnings above $184,500 are subject only to the 2.9% Medicare portion.
- **Net earnings subject to SE tax:** 92.35% of net self-employment income. This factor equals 1 minus 7.65% and reflects the statutory reduction under IRC Section 1402(a)(12): a self-employed person is not taxed on the portion of earnings that represents the employer-equivalent share of FICA (7.65%), because an employee in the same position would not have that amount included in their wages subject to payroll tax. A separate, additional deduction of 50% of the SE tax paid is then allowed as an above-the-line deduction on Form 1040, reducing AGI.
- **SE tax deduction:** 50% of SE tax paid is deductible above the line on Form 1040, reducing AGI
- **Additional Medicare Tax:** An extra 0.9% applies to self-employment income above $200,000 (single) / $250,000 (married filing jointly) / $125,000 (married filing separately). Not matched by employer.

### Qualified Business Income Deduction (Section 199A): Tax Year 2026

- **Maximum deduction:** 20% of qualified business income, plus 20% of qualified REIT dividends and qualified PTP income
- **Minimum deduction:** $400, available if QBI is at least $1,000 and you materially participate in the business
- **Full deduction available if taxable income is at or below:** $403,500 (married filing jointly) / $201,750 (all other filers)
- **Phase-in range (W-2 wage limitation begins for non-SSTBs; SSTB deduction phases out):** $403,500 to $553,500 (MFJ) / $201,750 to $276,750 (all other filers)
- **SSTB deduction completely eliminated above:** $553,500 (MFJ) / $276,750 (all other filers)
- **Status:** Made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, July 4, 2025)
- **Forms:** Form 8995 (income below the phase-in threshold) or Form 8995-A (income at or above the threshold)

### Section 179 Expensing (Tax Year 2026)

- **Maximum deduction:** $2,560,000
- **Phase-out threshold:** The maximum deduction is reduced dollar-for-dollar when the total cost of Section 179 property placed in service during 2026 exceeds $4,090,000
- **SUV limit:** $32,000 for sport utility vehicles placed in service in 2026
- **Form:** Form 4562 (Depreciation and Amortization)

### Bonus Depreciation (Tax Year 2026)

- **Rate:** 100% for qualifying property placed in service after January 19, 2025, restored by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, July 4, 2025)
- **Qualifying property:** Personal property (5-year, 7-year, 15-year assets) used in a trade or business with a recovery period of 20 years or less; does not include regular residential or commercial real property (39-year or 27.5-year assets) except through cost segregation
- **Form:** Form 4562

### SALT Deduction Cap (Tax Year 2026)

- **Standard cap:** $40,400 for most filers ($20,200 for married filing separately)
- **Phase-out:** The cap is reduced for taxpayers with MAGI above $505,000 ($252,500 for married filing separately), but cannot go below $10,000 ($5,000 MFS)
- **Sunset:** The elevated cap applies for tax years 2025 through 2029 per the One Big Beautiful Bill Act; reverts to $10,000 absent further legislation after 2029
- **Form:** Schedule A (Form 1040)

### Annual Gift Exclusion (Tax Year 2026)

- **Per-recipient annual exclusion:** $19,000 (gifts up to this amount per recipient per year do not count against your lifetime exclusion and require no gift tax return)
- **Lifetime estate and gift tax exclusion:** $15,000,000 per person ($30,000,000 for married couples using portability)

### Child Standard Deduction (Tax Year 2026)

A child who can be claimed as a dependent has a standard deduction equal to the greater of: (1) $1,350, or (2) the child's earned income plus $450, up to a maximum of the single standard deduction ($16,100 for 2026). A child employed in the family business who has only earned income from the business and no other income can earn up to $16,100 in 2026 before owing any federal income tax. This formula is the rule under IRC Section 63(c)(5); the $1,350 minimum, the $450 add-on, and the $16,100 cap are the 2026 figures per Rev. Proc. 2025-32. Confirm the application to your child's specific facts with your advisor.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure caveats:**
- The intake documents you need and the strategies available to you depend heavily on your entity type. A sole proprietor files Schedule C; an S-corp owner files Form 1120-S and receives a Schedule K-1. Do not mix up forms or rules between entity types.
- If you recently changed your entity structure (for example, from a sole proprietorship to an S-corp, or from an LLC to a corporation), you may need to file returns for both structures in the same year. Confirm with your advisor which forms are required.
- Single-member LLCs are treated as disregarded entities by default (Schedule C), but may have elected to be taxed as a corporation (Form 1120) or S-corporation (Form 1120-S). Check your IRS election acceptance letters to confirm your actual tax classification.

**Payroll caveats:**
- S-corp owners must run actual payroll and file Form 941 each quarter. If you have not filed Form 941, the IRS may determine that the S-corp election is not being honored, which can result in reclassification of distributions as wages subject to SE tax and penalties for late payroll tax deposits.
- Wages paid to children under age 18 by a sole proprietor (or a partnership where both partners are the child's parents) are exempt from Social Security, Medicare, and FUTA taxes. This exemption does NOT apply to S-corps or C-corps. All wages paid by a corporation are subject to FICA regardless of the employee's age.
- Health insurance premiums paid by an S-corp for a greater-than-2% shareholder-employee must be included in the shareholder's W-2 Box 1 wages and then deducted by the shareholder on Form 1040 as self-employed health insurance. Failing to run the premiums through W-2 wages may disqualify the deduction.

**State tax caveats:**
- States vary widely in conformity to federal tax rules. Common state nonconformity areas include: (a) the QBID (many states do not recognize the Section 199A deduction at all); (b) bonus depreciation (many states require a depreciation addback and use a different depreciation schedule); (c) S-corp income (some states impose a separate entity-level tax on S-corps in addition to the pass-through income on the owner's personal state return); (d) HSA deductibility (some states, notably California and New Jersey, do not allow an HSA deduction).
- If your business has employees, customers, or activity in states other than your home state, you may have economic nexus that creates a state income tax filing obligation in those states. This is a separate analysis your advisor will need to perform.
- The Augusta Rule is a federal income exclusion. Some states may tax rental income received under this arrangement. Confirm with your advisor.
- Pass-through entity tax (PTET) elections are available in most states as a workaround to the individual SALT cap. If your state offers a PTET election and you have an S-corp or partnership, ask your advisor whether the election is beneficial before the deadline (which varies by state, typically in the spring or fall of the tax year).

**Current-year legislative changes:**
- The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) made several changes effective for 2025 and beyond: permanent QBID, 100% bonus depreciation restoration, raised SALT cap through 2029, new deductions for tips, overtime, senior taxpayers, and vehicle loan interest. These changes are incorporated into the figures in this playbook. Monitor irs.gov for any technical corrections or additional guidance as implementing regulations are issued.
- The SALT cap of $40,400 is scheduled to revert to $10,000 after 2029 absent additional legislation. Year-end planning during 2029 may involve accelerating state and local tax payments into that year before the cap drops.

**IRS information return matching:**
- The IRS matches every 1099 and W-2 it receives with what you report on your return. If a 1099 appears in your transcript but not in your return, the IRS will likely send a notice proposing additional tax. The most common situation is a Form 1099-K from a payment processor that was overlooked. Every 1099 on your transcript must either appear on your return as income or have a documented explanation for why it is excluded or offset.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Starting the intake too late in tax season.** Waiting until April to begin gathering documents creates a time crunch that leads to missing items, rushed filing, and missed strategy opportunities. Many strategies (particularly solo 401(k) plan establishment and S-corp elections) require action before December 31 of the year in question. The intake process should ideally begin in January and be completed well before March.

2. **Assuming your 1099s are complete because you remember who paid you.** Payment processors (Stripe, PayPal, Square) may issue Form 1099-K for revenue you received through their platforms even if you also received Form 1099-NEC from the same clients. Always check your IRS Wage and Income Transcript to find every 1099 on file. Do not rely solely on what you received in the mail.

3. **Overlooking income from Schedule K-1 sources.** Partnerships and S-corps often file extensions, meaning your K-1 may not arrive until September or October. If you filed an individual return without waiting for a K-1, you may need to file an amended return (Form 1040-X). Keep a list of every entity from which you expect a K-1 and do not file your personal return until all K-1s have been received and reviewed.

4. **Confusing gross receipts on Form 1099-K with taxable profit.** A 1099-K reports every dollar that flowed through a payment processor (including amounts refunded to customers, platform fees, and chargebacks). Taxable profit is gross receipts minus allowable business expenses. If you use the 1099-K total as your income without subtracting expenses, you will overpay taxes. Track gross receipts and expenses separately in your bookkeeping.

5. **Not collecting W-9s from contractors before paying them.** You need each contractor's name, address, and Taxpayer Identification Number to issue a correct Form 1099-NEC. If you paid a contractor $600 or more and cannot get a W-9 from them after making three requests, you may be required to apply backup withholding (24% of payments) going forward. Collect W-9s before or at the time of the first payment.

6. **Missing the March 15 deadline for an S-corp election.** If you intended to have your LLC taxed as an S-corp starting January 1, 2026, the election (Form 2553) must generally be filed by March 15, 2026. Missing this deadline means waiting until 2027 (or applying for late election relief, which requires a valid reason for the delay). Do not wait until tax season to have this conversation with your advisor.

7. **Failing to separate personal and business bank accounts.** Commingling personal and business funds creates two problems: (a) it is harder to identify and document deductible expenses, and (b) it undermines the legal separateness of an LLC or corporation, which may affect liability protection. Every business entity should have a dedicated bank account and, ideally, a dedicated business credit card.

8. **Not verifying that estimated tax payments appear on the IRS transcript.** Payment confirmation emails from IRS Direct Pay and EFTPS can get buried in email. If you do not confirm that each payment was credited by the IRS, you may inadvertently miss a payment, incur underpayment penalties, and not realize it until the IRS sends a notice months later.

9. **Leaving the strategy roadmap blank and relying on your advisor to ask the right questions.** Your advisor is working with many clients and does not know what changed in your situation this year unless you tell them. The worksheet forces you to surface information (new income sources, new hires, new assets, new plans) that triggers strategy conversations. A blank intake form produces a generic return. A complete intake form produces a strategy-driven return.

10. **Ignoring the tax consequences of a business structure change mid-year.** If you changed from a sole proprietorship to an S-corp, sold a business interest, dissolved an entity, or brought in a new partner during 2026, the tax implications are significant and the documentation requirements change. Flag any structure changes explicitly for your advisor during the intake meeting.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and confirm with your advisor before taking any of the following actions:

- **Before filing your return without all K-1s:** If you have not received Schedule K-1 from every entity in which you hold an interest, do not file. If the entity is on extension, file an extension for your personal return as well (Form 4868). An incomplete return that omits K-1 income is worse than a late return.
- **Before claiming the QBID on Form 8995-A (income in or above the phase-out range):** The calculation for taxpayers with income above the phase-in threshold is complex. SSTB owners who are above the upper limit receive no deduction. Have your advisor verify the QBID calculation before filing.
- **Before making an S-corp election:** Confirm timing, your specific reasonable compensation level, state implications, and that your payroll infrastructure is ready. An election that goes into effect before payroll is running creates immediate compliance problems.
- **Before treating your business as a different entity type than the one on file with the IRS:** If you believe you are a sole proprietor but you previously filed Form 8832 or Form 2553, your tax classification may be different from what you assume. Confirm your current classification before preparing any return.
- **Before using Form 1099-K gross receipts as your income without reviewing expenses:** Gross receipts minus expenses equals taxable income. Confirm this calculation with your bookkeeping records before reporting.
- **Before claiming a deduction for a contractor you paid cash without a signed W-9 or 1099:** Cash payments require documentation just like any other business expense. If you cannot substantiate the payment, the deduction is at risk.
- **Before amending a prior-year return to add a strategy:** Amended returns (Form 1040-X) can be beneficial but can also open a closed year to scrutiny. Confirm with your advisor before filing any amendment.
- **Before concluding that you do not owe estimated taxes:** If your 2026 net business income is growing, you may be underwithheld relative to prior-year safe harbor rules. An underpayment penalty can be avoided by paying the lesser of 90% of current-year tax or 100% of prior-year tax (110% if prior-year AGI exceeded $150,000). Have your advisor confirm your required Q3 and Q4 payments before each due date.
