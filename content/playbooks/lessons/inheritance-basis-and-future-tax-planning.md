# Inheritance, Basis, and Future Tax Planning

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

When you inherit property, the IRS resets your tax basis (the starting number used to calculate gain or loss when you sell) to the property's fair market value on the date the original owner died. This "stepped-up basis" can eliminate decades of built-up, untaxed gain in a single moment, and the planning choices you make before and after an inheritance determine how much of that benefit you actually capture. This playbook walks you through every relevant rule, every required action, and the proactive steps you can take now to position your family to receive and preserve as much of this benefit as possible.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is for you if any of the following describe your situation:

- You recently inherited real estate, investment accounts, business interests, or other appreciated property.
- A family member in poor health owns highly appreciated assets and you want to understand the tax implications before death occurs.
- You own appreciated property and are thinking about how to transfer it to heirs with the least possible tax friction.
- Your estate (or a parent's estate) may be at or near the federal estate tax threshold and you want to understand how estate tax and basis interact.
- You are a surviving spouse and want to understand how community property or joint tenancy rules affect your cost basis going forward.
- You received a Schedule A (Form 8971) from an estate executor and need to understand what to do with it.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not rely on this playbook alone if any of the following apply:

- **You received property via a lifetime gift, not inheritance.** Gifted property generally carries the donor's original basis (called "carryover basis"), not a step-up. The rules here are substantially different. Ask your advisor about Publication 551 gift-basis rules.
- **The inherited property was originally gifted to the decedent by you or your spouse within one year before the decedent's death.** In that narrow situation, IRC Section 1014(e) blocks the step-up: your basis is the decedent's original adjusted basis, not the date-of-death fair market value. This is a specific anti-abuse rule. Get advisor guidance immediately.
- **The property sits inside a retirement account (IRA, 401(k), 403(b), etc.).** Inherited retirement accounts do not receive a stepped-up basis because contributions were pre-tax. Distributions are taxed as ordinary income. A separate set of distribution rules applies (10-year rule, required minimum distributions). Discuss with your advisor.
- **The property is classified as "income in respect of a decedent" (IRD).** IRD is income the decedent earned but did not yet receive before death (such as unpaid wages, outstanding installment-sale payments, or deferred compensation). IRD does not get a step-up; instead it is taxed as ordinary income when collected by the estate or beneficiary. Common IRD items include Series EE savings bond interest and accounts receivable for cash-basis businesses.
- **The estate involves property held in a foreign country.** International basis and reporting rules add layers not covered here.
- **You are involved in a Generation-Skipping Transfer.** GST tax rules and dynasty trust planning require specialized guidance beyond this playbook.

## Key Terms

**Basis (also called "tax basis" or "cost basis"):** The IRS's measure of what you have invested in a property. When you sell, your taxable gain equals the selling price minus your basis. A higher basis means a smaller taxable gain. A lower basis means a larger taxable gain.

**Adjusted basis:** Your original basis after applying all required increases (capital improvements) and decreases (depreciation deductions, insurance reimbursements) over the time you held the property.

**Stepped-up basis:** When you inherit property, the IRS resets your basis to the property's fair market value on the date the original owner died, even if that person paid far less decades ago. The accumulated, untaxed gain from the owner's lifetime is erased. This is the core rule of IRC Section 1014.

**Stepped-down basis:** The same reset rule can produce a step-down if the property lost value before death. If you inherit property worth less than what the decedent paid, your basis is the lower fair market value at death.

**Fair market value (FMV):** The price at which property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither under compulsion, and both with reasonable knowledge of the relevant facts. FMV is the standard for setting inherited basis.

**Decedent:** The person who died and whose property is being inherited.

**Estate:** The total property, assets, and liabilities left behind by a decedent. The estate is a legal entity that is administered (managed and distributed) by an executor or personal representative.

**Executor (also called "personal representative"):** The individual or institution appointed to manage and distribute a decedent's estate according to the will or state law.

**Form 706 (United States Estate and Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax Return):** The federal estate tax return that an executor must file when a decedent's taxable estate exceeds the applicable exclusion amount. For decedents dying in 2026, that threshold is $15,000,000.

**Applicable exclusion amount (also called "estate tax exemption"):** The dollar amount that can pass from a deceased person's estate without owing federal estate tax. For 2026, this amount is $15,000,000 per person.

**Form 8971 and Schedule A:** When an executor is required to file Form 706, federal law requires the executor to file Form 8971 with the IRS and furnish a Schedule A (Form 8971) to each beneficiary. Schedule A tells the beneficiary the estate-tax value of the property they received. Beneficiaries must use this value as their starting basis: they cannot claim a higher basis than what Schedule A reports.

**Consistent basis requirement (IRC Section 1014(f)):** The rule that a beneficiary's initial basis in inherited property cannot exceed the value reported on the estate tax return (Form 706). If Schedule A (Form 8971) reports a value, that value governs.

**Long-term capital gain:** Profit from selling a capital asset (property, investments, business interests) that you held for more than one year. Long-term gains are taxed at lower federal rates (0%, 15%, or 20%) than ordinary income. Inherited property is automatically treated as long-term regardless of how long you actually hold it after inheriting.

**Short-term capital gain:** Profit from selling an asset held one year or less. Taxed as ordinary income at rates up to 37%. Inherited property does NOT receive short-term treatment even if sold the next day.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** An additional 3.8% federal tax on investment income (including capital gains) for taxpayers whose modified adjusted gross income exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). This tax layers on top of the regular capital gains rate.

**Alternate valuation date:** An election available to the executor in limited circumstances to value the estate's assets six months after the date of death instead of on the date of death. The election is only permitted if it would both reduce the gross estate and reduce the federal estate tax. Once made, it cannot be revoked.

**Portability:** A rule allowing a surviving spouse to "inherit" any unused portion of the deceased spouse's federal estate tax exemption. To use portability, the executor of the deceased spouse's estate must file a timely Form 706, even if no estate tax is owed. This is a time-sensitive election with major future planning value.

**DSUE (Deceased Spousal Unused Exclusion):** The portion of the deceased spouse's $15,000,000 exemption that was not used by their estate. If portability is elected, the surviving spouse can stack the DSUE on top of their own exemption, potentially shielding up to $30,000,000 from estate tax.

**Community property:** A marital property system used in nine states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin) where most property acquired during marriage is owned equally by both spouses. Community property has a special stepped-up basis rule: at the death of either spouse, the entire community property asset (both halves) receives a step-up to fair market value, not just the deceased spouse's half.

**Separate property:** Property owned before marriage, or received by gift or inheritance during marriage, that belongs entirely to one spouse. Only the deceased spouse's interest in separate property receives a basis step-up; the surviving spouse's separate property interest does not.

**Joint tenancy with right of survivorship (JTWROS):** A form of ownership where each co-owner holds an equal, undivided interest and the surviving owner automatically inherits the deceased owner's share outside of probate. In a married JTWROS (not community property), generally only the deceased spouse's half receives a step-up.

**Income in respect of a decedent (IRD):** Income that the decedent was entitled to receive before death but had not yet collected. IRD is taxed as ordinary income to whoever receives it and does not receive a stepped-up basis.

**Depreciation:** Annual deductions claimed on income-producing property that reduce your adjusted basis over time. When you inherit depreciated property, your stepped-up basis resets to current fair market value and the prior depreciation taken by the decedent is wiped clean. You start fresh.

**Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain:** When you sell inherited real estate that has been depreciated (whether by you after inheriting or by the decedent before death), a portion of the gain attributable to prior depreciation is taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25%, not at the lower long-term capital gains rates.

**Appraisal:** A formal written valuation of property prepared by a qualified, independent professional. An appraisal is the standard method for establishing fair market value at the date of death for non-publicly-traded assets (real estate, closely-held business interests, personal property, collectibles).

## Required Client Inputs

Gather all of the following before starting the implementation steps.

**Information about the decedent and the estate:**
- Date of the decedent's death
- Whether a will or trust governs the distribution of assets
- The name and contact information of the executor or personal representative
- Whether Form 706 was (or will be) required to be filed
- Whether Form 8971 and Schedule A have been (or will be) issued to you as a beneficiary

**Information about each inherited asset:**
- The type of asset: real estate, brokerage account, business interest, vehicle, personal property, etc.
- The decedent's original purchase price and date of acquisition (you will not use this as your basis, but you will need it to confirm the step-up occurred and to document records)
- Any appraisal, brokerage statement, or account valuation as of the date of death
- Whether the property was held as community property, joint tenancy with right of survivorship, or sole ownership
- Whether any depreciation was previously claimed on the property (relevant for real estate)
- Whether the property generates ongoing income (rental income, dividends, business income)

**Information about your own tax situation:**
- Your expected taxable income for the year you plan to sell any inherited assets
- Your filing status (single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, head of household)
- Whether your modified adjusted gross income is likely to exceed $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), which is the Net Investment Income Tax threshold

**If you are the surviving spouse:**
- Whether the deceased spouse had any unused estate tax exemption
- Whether the executor filed (or plans to file) a Form 706 to elect portability

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect and keep each of the following. Retention guidance follows the list.**

Documents to collect:
- A certified copy of the death certificate
- A copy of the will, trust document, or applicable court order governing distribution of the estate
- Letters testamentary or letters of administration (the court document appointing the executor)
- Schedule A (Form 8971) if issued by the executor (this document establishes your required starting basis when the estate filed Form 706)
- A copy of Form 706 if the estate filed one (ask the executor for a copy; you are entitled to it as a beneficiary)
- A qualified appraisal of all non-publicly-traded inherited assets as of the date of death (real estate, closely held business interests, collectibles, artwork, vehicles)
- Brokerage or financial institution statements showing account values and individual security values as of the date of death (for publicly traded assets, the date-of-death price is the average of the high and low trading prices that day)
- A written record of your basis calculation for each asset: FMV at date of death, reduced by any depreciation you claim going forward
- Any property tax assessment records, prior appraisals, or comparable sales data used to support the date-of-death FMV if no formal appraisal was obtained
- Closing statements or settlement statements if inherited real estate is sold

Retention guidance: Keep all basis documentation for as long as you own each asset, plus at least seven years after the tax year in which you sell it. For real estate, keep records indefinitely until sale plus seven years. For estates that filed Form 706, the IRS can generally assess additional estate tax within three years of filing, but basis disputes may arise in later years; permanent retention is safest.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Immediately After the Inheritance (Within the First 90 Days)

**Step 1: Identify every asset you inherited and its form of ownership.**
List each inherited item: real estate addresses, brokerage account numbers, business interests, cash accounts, personal property. For each, note whether it was held as community property, joint tenancy with right of survivorship, or sole ownership by the decedent. Your basis rules differ by ownership form (see The Numbers section).

**Step 2: Establish fair market value at the date of death for each asset.**

- For publicly traded stocks, mutual funds, and ETFs: the FMV is the average of the high and low trading prices on the date of death. Your brokerage should update the cost basis in your account automatically after the transfer; verify the number they used against the actual date-of-death price.
- For real estate: order a qualified appraisal from a licensed real estate appraiser. The appraisal should be dated as of the date of death. Do not rely on assessed value, Zillow estimates, or informal comparables. Only a formal appraisal will hold up with the IRS.
- For closely held business interests (LLC, S-corporation, partnership): engage a business valuation professional to produce a qualified appraisal as of the date of death.
- For personal property, collectibles, vehicles, and artwork: obtain a qualified appraisal from an appropriate specialist.
- Record the resulting FMV for each asset. This number is your starting basis.

**Step 3: Determine whether an estate tax return (Form 706) will be filed.**
Ask the executor directly. If the decedent's gross estate (including all assets, life insurance payable to the estate, and prior taxable gifts) exceeds $15,000,000 (the 2026 threshold), Form 706 must be filed. Even if the estate is below that threshold, the executor may choose to file Form 706 solely to elect portability (see Step 5).

**Step 4: Obtain Schedule A (Form 8971) if one was issued to you.**
If the estate filed (or is required to file) Form 706, the executor must file Form 8971 with the IRS and send you a Schedule A within 30 days of the earlier of: the date Form 706 was due (including extensions), or the actual date Form 706 was filed. When you receive Schedule A, the value shown in column (h) is your maximum allowable starting basis for that property. You cannot use a higher value. Store this document permanently.

**Step 5: If you are a surviving spouse, confirm the portability election.**
Ask the executor whether they plan to file Form 706 to elect portability of your deceased spouse's unused exemption (DSUE). The Form 706 must be filed within nine months of the date of death (extendable to 15 months using Form 4768). This is a one-time, time-sensitive decision with enormous future tax value. If the executor is not planning to file, bring this to your advisor immediately.

**Step 6: Document your basis for each inherited asset in writing.**
Create a simple log (the attached worksheet is designed for this) showing: the asset name, the date of death, the date-of-death FMV, the basis amount you will use, the source of the FMV figure (appraisal, brokerage statement, Form 8971 Schedule A), and whether the asset generates ongoing income. Keep this log with your other permanent records.

### Phase 2: During Ownership (Ongoing)

**Step 7: Adjust basis as required during ownership.**
Your starting basis at death is not static. You must increase it for capital improvements (a new roof, a room addition, a major system replacement) and decrease it for any depreciation you claim on income-producing property. Track every adjustment with supporting receipts or invoices. The Inheritance Basis worksheet has a running-total column for this purpose.

**Step 8: Track depreciation carefully for any inherited real property you rent.**
If you rent inherited real estate, you must claim depreciation each year on the building portion (not the land). The depreciation is calculated on your stepped-up basis, not the original cost. Depreciation reduces your adjusted basis dollar for dollar. When you eventually sell, the previously depreciated amounts will be subject to unrecaptured Section 1250 gain tax at a maximum 25% federal rate.

**Step 9: Keep records of income generated by inherited assets.**
Rental income, dividends, interest, and business distributions from inherited assets are taxable in the year received. Your basis in the asset does not affect whether this income is taxable. Report it each year on your income tax return.

### Phase 3: When Selling Inherited Assets

**Step 10: Calculate your gain or loss before you sell.**
Before agreeing to a sale price, compute: sale price minus selling costs (commissions, closing costs) minus your adjusted basis. This is your taxable gain. Use the capital gains rate tables in The Numbers section to estimate your federal tax liability. Factor in the NIIT if your income is above the threshold.

**Step 11: Confirm the automatic long-term holding period.**
All inherited property is treated as held long-term, regardless of how long you actually owned it after the inheritance. This means even if you sell one month after inheriting, your gain is taxed at long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15%, or 20%) rather than as short-term ordinary income. Report the sale on Form 8949 (Part II, long-term) and carry the results to Schedule D of Form 1040.

**Step 12: Report the sale correctly on your tax return.**
On Form 8949, enter the description of the property, the date you acquired it (use the decedent's date of death), the date you sold it, the sale proceeds, your adjusted basis, and the resulting gain or loss. Check the applicable box (D, E, or F for long-term transactions from reported 1099-B; or J, K, or L for transactions not reported on 1099-B). Carry the totals to Schedule D (Form 1040).

### Phase 4: Proactive Future Planning

**Step 13: Review your own estate plan and basis situation.**
If you own highly appreciated assets, work with your advisor to understand what your heirs would inherit and what basis reset they would receive. Assets held until death receive a full step-up; assets given away during life carry over your original (low) basis. In most cases, holding appreciated assets until death is more tax-efficient than gifting them during life, unless you need to remove them from your estate for estate tax reasons.

**Step 14: Consider asset location for appreciated versus depreciated property.**
If you own both highly appreciated assets (low basis, large embedded gain) and assets that have declined in value (high basis, embedded loss), discuss with your advisor which assets to hold until death for the step-up and which to sell or reposition now. Assets with losses generally do not benefit from the inherited basis reset (they step down) and may be better sold during your lifetime to realize the loss.

**Step 15: If you are in a community property state, understand the full double step-up.**
In community property states, both halves of community property receive a stepped-up basis at the death of either spouse. A community property asset with an adjusted basis of $200,000 and a fair market value of $600,000 at the date of one spouse's death would give the surviving spouse a basis of $600,000 in the entire asset. The surviving spouse could immediately sell it with no capital gain. Your advisor can confirm whether your state is a community property state and whether your assets qualify.

**Step 16: If you relocated from a community property state, confirm property characterization.**
Property acquired while living in a community property state may retain its community property character even if you later moved to a non-community property state. This can be favorable (potential double step-up) or create complications. Discuss with your advisor.

## Timing And Deadlines

**Date of death:** The clock starts here. FMV is measured as of this date for stepped-up basis.

**30 days after Form 706 is filed or due (whichever is earlier):** The executor must file Form 8971 with the IRS and furnish Schedule A to beneficiaries by this date.

**9 months after date of death:** Form 706 is due (if required). Automatic 6-month extension available using Form 4768, extending the deadline to 15 months.

**9 months after date of death (or 15 months with extension):** Portability election deadline. If the executor does not file a timely Form 706, the DSUE is permanently lost for the surviving spouse unless simplified relief applies. Do not miss this window.

**6 months after death (alternate valuation date):** If the alternate valuation date election will be made, the estate's assets are valued on this date instead of the date of death. The executor decides; beneficiaries cannot override this.

**Year you sell:** Report the sale on your individual income tax return (Form 1040 with Form 8949 and Schedule D) for the year the sale closes.

**Annual:** If you rent inherited real estate, file Schedule E (Form 1040) annually to report rental income and claim depreciation. Update your adjusted basis records annually to reflect depreciation claimed.

**7 years after sale:** Minimum record retention for all supporting documents after the year of sale.

## The Numbers

All figures below are verified for tax year 2026 unless otherwise noted. Sources are cited in the Sources section.

**Federal estate tax exemption (applicable exclusion amount), 2026:** $15,000,000 per person. Estates of decedents dying in 2026 owe no federal estate tax on the first $15,000,000 of taxable estate value (up from $13,990,000 in 2025). Source: IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32; Public Law 119-21 (One, Big, Beautiful Bill).

**Federal estate tax rate:** 40% on the value of the taxable estate exceeding the applicable exclusion amount. Source: IRC Section 2001(c); confirmed in Form 706 instructions.

**Annual gift tax exclusion, 2026:** $19,000 per recipient per year. You can give up to $19,000 to any individual in 2026 without using any of your lifetime exemption or owing gift tax. Gifts to a non-citizen spouse: $194,000 in 2026. Source: IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32.

**Portability / DSUE maximum, 2026:** Up to $15,000,000 of a deceased spouse's unused exemption can be transferred to the surviving spouse if a timely Form 706 is filed. Combined with the surviving spouse's own exemption, a married couple may shelter up to $30,000,000 from federal estate tax. Source: IRC Section 2010(c)(4); IRS FAQ on estate taxes.

**Form 706 filing threshold, 2026:** File if the gross estate plus adjusted taxable gifts and specific gift tax exemption exceeds $15,000,000. Source: IRS Estate Tax page (irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estate-tax).

**Form 706 due date:** 9 months after the date of death. 6-month automatic extension available (Form 4768), giving 15 months total. Tax owed is still due by the original 9-month deadline regardless of the extension. Source: Form 706 Instructions (09/2025).

**Form 8971 filing deadline:** 30 days after the earlier of the Form 706 due date (including extensions) or the actual filing date. Penalty for failure to file or furnish Schedule A: the general IRC Section 6721/6722 information reporting penalties apply, plus a 20% accuracy-related penalty under IRC Section 6662 if the beneficiary uses a basis inconsistent with Schedule A. Source: Form 8971 Instructions (08/2025).

**Inconsistent basis penalty:** 20% of the tax underpayment resulting from using a basis higher than the Schedule A value. If the basis is reported at 200% or more of the correct amount (gross valuation misstatement), the penalty increases to 40%. Source: IRC Section 6662(h); Form 8971 Instructions.

**Long-term capital gains tax rates (federal), 2026:**

| Filing Status | 0% Rate: Taxable Income Up To | 15% Rate: Taxable Income | 20% Rate: Taxable Income Above |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single | $49,450 | $49,451 to $545,500 | $545,500 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $98,900 | $98,901 to $613,700 | $613,700 |
| Married Filing Separately | $49,450 | $49,451 to $306,850 | $306,850 |
| Head of Household | $66,200 | $66,201 to $579,600 | $579,600 |

Source: IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32 (as confirmed by IRS Topic 409 and Tax Foundation 2026 Bracket data).

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT), 2026:** 3.8% on the lesser of (a) net investment income or (b) the amount by which modified adjusted gross income exceeds the applicable threshold. Thresholds: $250,000 for married filing jointly; $200,000 for single and head of household; $125,000 for married filing separately. These thresholds are not adjusted for inflation. Source: IRC Section 1411; IRS Net Investment Income Tax page (irs.gov/individuals/net-investment-income-tax).

**Combined maximum federal rate on long-term capital gains, 2026:** 23.8% (20% capital gains rate plus 3.8% NIIT), before state taxes.

**Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain:** Taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25% for real property on which depreciation was previously claimed. Source: IRC Section 1(h)(1)(D); IRS Topic 409.

**Short-term capital gains rate:** Taxed as ordinary income at rates ranging from 10% to 37% depending on your total taxable income and filing status. Inherited property does not receive short-term treatment.

**Holding period for inherited property:** Automatically treated as held long-term under IRC Section 1223(9), regardless of how long you actually held the property after inheriting it. Source: IRC Section 1223(9); IRS Form 8949 Instructions (2025).

**Community property states (where the full double step-up applies):** Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. Source: IRS Publication 555 (12/2024); IRS Publication 551 (12/2025).

**Carryover basis exception:** If you or your spouse gave property to the decedent within one year before the decedent's death, your basis is the decedent's adjusted basis at death, not FMV. Source: IRC Section 1014(e); IRS Publication 551 (12/2025).

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**State estate and inheritance taxes.** Twelve states and the District of Columbia impose a state-level estate tax, several with exemptions far below the federal $15,000,000 threshold. Five states impose a separate inheritance tax (Kentucky, Maryland, Nebraska, New Jersey, Pennsylvania). Iowa fully repealed its inheritance tax effective January 1, 2025, for deaths on or after that date (Iowa Code Section 450.98; S.F. 619). State rates and thresholds vary widely. Your federal stepped-up basis analysis does not change, but your overall estate plan must account for state-level exposure. Confirm the rules in every state where you or the decedent held real property.

**State capital gains taxes.** Most states tax capital gains at ordinary income rates. A few states (including Washington) impose a capital gains tax at a flat rate above certain thresholds. Your federal basis step-up reduces the federal gain computation; state gain is calculated under state rules, which may differ. Confirm with your advisor for each state where you file.

**S-corporation and partnership interests.** Inherited interests in pass-through entities (S-corporations, partnerships, LLCs taxed as partnerships) receive a stepped-up basis in the ownership interest (your outside basis). However, the step-up does not automatically increase the basis of assets held inside the entity (the inside basis). Your advisor may recommend a Section 754 election to align inside and outside basis. This election must generally be made on the entity's timely filed tax return for the year of transfer. Missing this window is a common and costly mistake.

**Trusts.** Property in a revocable living trust at the decedent's death generally receives a stepped-up basis because the assets are included in the decedent's estate. Property in certain irrevocable trusts may not receive a step-up if it was not included in the taxable estate. Confirm which type of trust held the inherited property before assuming a step-up applies.

**Grantor retained annuity trusts (GRATs) and irrevocable life insurance trusts (ILITs).** These common estate planning vehicles are designed to remove assets from the taxable estate, but removing assets from the estate generally means those assets do not receive a stepped-up basis. There is a tradeoff between estate tax savings and income tax cost; your advisor must model both.

**Alternate valuation date.** If the executor elected the alternate valuation date (six months after death), your basis is the FMV on the alternate date, not the date of death. Confirm with the executor which election was made. The Schedule A (Form 8971) will reflect whichever date was used.

**Assets placed in service before 1987.** These may be subject to ACRS (Accelerated Cost Recovery System) depreciation rules rather than MACRS. The depreciation analysis for inherited pre-1987 assets requires additional review.

**TCJA sunset provisions.** The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 was substantially extended by the One, Big, Beautiful Bill (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025). The $15,000,000 estate tax exemption reflects this extension. Future legislation could alter these figures. Monitor for legislative changes affecting tax years beyond 2026.

**Installment obligations received from an estate.** If you inherit the right to receive installment payments (from a prior sale the decedent made on installment terms), those payments are IRD: they do not receive a stepped-up basis and are taxed as ordinary income when collected.

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Accepting the brokerage's automated basis without verifying it.**
Brokerages often transfer inherited accounts and program in a cost basis, but they use the closing price on the date of death rather than the IRS-required average of the high and low prices. Verify every brokerage-assigned basis against the actual date-of-death trading range.

**Mistake 2: Skipping the appraisal for real estate or business interests.**
Without a contemporaneous, qualified appraisal, you have no defensible FMV figure if the IRS questions your claimed basis. An appraisal obtained years after the fact is far harder to defend than one obtained within months of death.

**Mistake 3: Assuming a step-up when none exists.**
Not every inherited asset gets a step-up. Retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s), IRD items, and property subject to the one-year gift-back rule do not receive stepped-up basis. Treating them as if they do understates your gain and creates penalties.

**Mistake 4: Missing the portability window.**
If the executor does not file Form 706 within the 9-month (or 15-month extended) deadline, the surviving spouse permanently loses the deceased spouse's unused exemption, potentially losing up to $15,000,000 of estate tax shelter. This is irreversible once the window closes.

**Mistake 5: Reporting inherited property as short-term.**
Some tax preparers or software programs ask how long you held the property and, if you held it only briefly, classify the gain as short-term. Inherited property is always long-term under IRC Section 1223(9). Incorrectly reporting it as short-term means you overpay taxes.

**Mistake 6: Using a basis higher than the Schedule A (Form 8971) value.**
If you received a Schedule A and use a higher basis on your return, you are subject to a 20% accuracy-related penalty (or 40% if the overstatement is large enough). The Schedule A value is your ceiling.

**Mistake 7: Failing to track ongoing depreciation adjustments.**
After inheriting rental real estate, many taxpayers forget that claiming depreciation each year reduces their basis. They then compute their gain incorrectly at sale, often understating the gain (which triggers IRS scrutiny) or overstating it (which means overpaying).

**Mistake 8: Ignoring the Section 754 election for pass-through entity interests.**
Inheriting a partnership or S-corporation interest without triggering a Section 754 election (where applicable) leaves the inside basis of the entity's assets misaligned with your outside basis. This can result in double taxation when the entity later sells those assets. Discuss this with your advisor before the entity files its next tax return.

**Mistake 9: Selling in a high-income year without timing analysis.**
Because the 0% capital gains rate applies to substantial income levels (up to $98,900 for married filing jointly in 2026), timing a sale into a lower-income year can eliminate federal capital gains tax entirely. Selling in a high-income year when you could have waited may be unnecessary and costly.

**Mistake 10: Confusing the step-up with the elimination of state taxes.**
The federal stepped-up basis reduces federal capital gains. State capital gains taxes, state estate taxes, and state inheritance taxes are calculated separately under state rules. A complete analysis requires reviewing both levels.

## Advisor Review Gates

Contact your advisor before acting if any of the following apply to your situation. These are decision points where the consequences of the wrong choice are significant and hard to reverse.

**Gate 1: Before the portability election deadline.**
If you are a surviving spouse and there is any doubt about whether the executor will file a timely Form 706 to elect portability, call your advisor within 60 days of the date of death. The election cannot be made retroactively except through a costly and uncertain private letter ruling process.

**Gate 2: Before ordering appraisals for real estate or business interests.**
Your advisor or estate attorney can help ensure the appraiser meets IRS qualified-appraiser standards and that the appraisal report contains all required elements. A deficient appraisal may not be accepted by the IRS.

**Gate 3: Before filing if you receive a Schedule A (Form 8971).**
Have your advisor review Schedule A before you use the values on your tax return. If the executor reported a value you believe is incorrect, there are procedures for contesting it, but they must be pursued before you file.

**Gate 4: Before selling any inherited property.**
Run the gain calculation (see Step 10) with your advisor. Confirm your adjusted basis, consider the timing of the sale relative to your income level and capital gains rate bracket, and evaluate whether the NIIT will apply.

**Gate 5: Before making a Section 754 election (or deciding not to).**
If you inherited a partnership or S-corporation interest, this election has lasting consequences for both you and the other owners. It must generally be made on the entity's timely filed tax return for the year you inherited the interest.

**Gate 6: Before giving away inherited property.**
Once you give away inherited property, the recipient receives your (stepped-up) basis as their carryover basis. In most cases this is favorable. But if the property has declined in value since the date of death (below your stepped-up basis), it may be better to sell it yourself, recognize the loss, and gift cash. Structure of the transfer matters.

**Gate 7: If the inherited property includes a closely held business.**
Business interests require valuation discounts (minority discount, lack of marketability discount), possible Section 6166 installment payment of estate tax, and Section 754 election analysis. Do not proceed without advisor guidance.

## Using The Download(s)

Use this worksheet beginning at Step 2 of the implementation process. The worksheet contains:

## Before You Act

Before you file a tax return that includes inherited property sales, make any election related to the estate, change the basis you report for inherited assets, sell or transfer inherited property, or take any action described in the Advisor Review Gates above: confirm the specific facts of your situation with your advisor. The rules described in this playbook apply to most standard inheritance situations, but your situation may have features (trust structures, multi-state property, closely held business interests, prior installment sales, or prior gifting patterns) that change the analysis. This page is educational. Your advisor's written guidance for your specific facts governs.
