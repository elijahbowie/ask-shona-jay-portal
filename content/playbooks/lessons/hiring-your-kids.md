# Hiring Your Kids

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

When your business hires your child as a genuine employee and pays them reasonable wages for real work, those wages are a deductible business expense for the company and taxable income to the child at the child's (typically much lower) rate. Because a child who is claimed as your dependent can still use their own standard deduction against earned income, a significant portion of the wages may be taxed at zero percent on the child's return. The strategy shifts income from the parent's higher tax bracket into the child's lower bracket, reduces the parent's self-employment or business income, and in certain entity structures eliminates payroll taxes (Social Security and Medicare) on those wages entirely.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is best suited for:

- **Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs taxed as sole proprietors** who have a child under age 18. This group gets the largest benefit: wages paid to a child under 18 are not subject to Social Security or Medicare taxes when paid by a sole proprietorship, and wages paid to a child under 21 are exempt from Federal Unemployment Tax (FUTA).
- **Husband-and-wife partnerships where both partners are the child's parents.** The same FICA and FUTA exemptions that apply to sole proprietors extend to a partnership in which every partner is a parent of the child.
- **S-corporation and C-corporation owners** whose children can perform genuine, documentable work. The payroll tax exemptions do NOT apply in a corporate structure, but the income-shifting benefit (wages deducted at the business rate, taxed at the child's rate) still applies.
- **Any business owner** with a child who can perform tasks the business legitimately needs: content creation, filing, photography, social media management, clerical work, assisting at events, inventory, data entry, or other real business functions.

The child must be old enough to perform the work in a meaningful way. The IRS has accepted children as young as seven in documented cases where the work was age-appropriate and properly substantiated. Use judgment: if a reasonable outside business would not pay a child of that age to do the work, the IRS will not either.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **When the work is not real.** Paying a child for chores around the home, attending a family event, or doing tasks that have no business purpose is not deductible and may be recharacterized as a gift with no business deduction allowed.
- **When the wages are not reasonable.** Paying a child $50 per hour for tasks that an unrelated worker earns $12 per hour to perform will attract scrutiny. Compensation must match what the market pays for the same work.
- **When there are no records.** If you cannot document what work was done, when it was done, and how many hours were worked, the IRS can disallow the deduction on audit. Without records, do not start.
- **When the business cannot run payroll.** The child must be a W-2 employee (not a 1099 contractor, as the IRS generally will not respect a parent-child independent contractor relationship). The business needs an Employer Identification Number, a payroll process, and the ability to file quarterly payroll tax returns.
- **When the child's wages would exceed reasonable compensation for the work performed.** No deduction is allowed for wages above a reasonable market rate. Excess pay above what the services are worth is treated as a non-deductible gift.
- **When state child labor laws prohibit the work.** State law governs what hours, tasks, and ages are permissible for employed minors. This page does not address state labor law; confirm with your advisor and the applicable state labor authority.
- **When the child has significant unearned income that creates a kiddie tax problem.** The kiddie tax (explained below in Key Terms) taxes a child's unearned investment income above $2,700 at the parent's rate. Wages (earned income) are not subject to the kiddie tax, but a child with large investment accounts may have a complex tax situation; confirm with your advisor.

## Key Terms

**Earned income:** Wages, salaries, tips, and other compensation a person receives for work actually performed. When a child works for a family business and receives a paycheck, those wages are earned income. Earned income is distinct from unearned income (interest, dividends, capital gains). This distinction matters because the kiddie tax does not apply to earned income, and a child's standard deduction can shelter a large portion of earned income from tax.

**Standard deduction (child):** A deduction that reduces taxable income. For a child who can be claimed as a dependent by a parent, the 2026 standard deduction is the greater of: (1) $1,350, or (2) the child's earned income plus $450, up to the regular single-filer standard deduction of $16,100. In plain terms: if your child earns $15,650 or more from the business, their standard deduction rises to $16,100 and fully shelters that amount from federal income tax. At lower wage amounts, the formula means a dependent child can earn up to $15,650 in wages and owe zero federal income tax (Source: Rev. Proc. 2025-32; IRS Topic 551).

**Standard deduction (single filer, 2026):** $16,100 for a single individual who cannot be claimed as a dependent. This is the cap on the dependent child's standard deduction formula.

**Kiddie tax:** A rule under Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 1(g) that taxes a child's net unearned income above $2,700 at the parent's marginal tax rate rather than the child's lower rate, for children under age 19 (or full-time students under age 24 with earned income that is half or less of their own support). The kiddie tax applies only to unearned income (investment income). It does NOT apply to wages. Wages earned from working in the family business are taxed at the child's own rate and are not affected by the kiddie tax.

**FICA:** Federal Insurance Contributions Act taxes, consisting of Social Security tax (6.2% employee plus 6.2% employer, on wages up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500) and Medicare tax (1.45% employee plus 1.45% employer, on all wages with no wage base limit). Total FICA on both sides is 15.3% on wages within the Social Security wage base.

**FICA exemption for children:** Under IRC Section 3121(b)(3)(A), wages paid to a child under age 18 by a parent's sole proprietorship or by a partnership in which every partner is a parent of the child are exempt from Social Security and Medicare taxes. This means neither the employer side nor the employee side of FICA applies on those wages, saving up to 15.3 cents on every dollar paid to the child (when wages are within the Social Security wage base).

**FUTA (Federal Unemployment Tax Act):** A federal tax paid only by the employer to fund unemployment benefits. The FUTA tax rate is 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages per year, before a credit of up to 5.4% for timely state unemployment tax payments, resulting in a net rate of 0.6% in most states. Under IRC Section 3306(c)(5), wages paid to a child under age 21 by a parent's sole proprietorship or qualifying partnership are exempt from FUTA.

**Reasonable compensation:** The wage amount that a similar business would pay to an unrelated employee for performing the same or substantially similar work under the same circumstances. The IRS requires that wages paid to family members be reasonable for the services actually rendered. Wages in excess of reasonable compensation may be disallowed as a deduction and recharacterized as a non-deductible gift. Reasonableness is determined by the duties performed, hours worked, the complexity of the work, and what the local market pays for comparable work.

**Bona fide employee:** A real employee relationship, as opposed to a nominal or fictional one. For a child to be a bona fide employee, the child must actually perform the work, the parent's business must have a genuine need for that work, the hours and tasks must be documented, and the pay must be issued through payroll (W-2, not cash or gift). If any of these elements is missing, the IRS can recharacterize the wages as a gift and disallow the deduction.

**W-2:** IRS Form W-2, Wage and Tax Statement. The annual form an employer gives each employee showing wages paid and income taxes withheld. All child employees must receive a W-2 by January 31 of the following year.

**Form 941:** Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return. Used to report wages paid and payroll taxes withheld each quarter. Sole proprietors using the FICA exemption for a child under 18 still file Form 941 (or may qualify for annual Form 944), but report those wages as exempt from Social Security and Medicare.

**Form 940:** Employer's Annual Federal Unemployment (FUTA) Tax Return. Filed annually to report and pay FUTA taxes. Wages paid to children under 21 by a qualifying sole proprietorship or parent partnership are reported as exempt from FUTA.

**EIN (Employer Identification Number):** A nine-digit number the IRS assigns to a business entity for tax reporting purposes. You must have an EIN before you can run payroll. Apply on IRS Form SS-4 or online at IRS.gov.

**IRC Section 162:** The Internal Revenue Code provision that allows a deduction for "ordinary and necessary" expenses paid or incurred in carrying on a trade or business. Wages paid to a child employee who performs genuine services are deductible under IRC Section 162 if the compensation is reasonable.

**Tax bracket (2026):** The rate at which the next dollar of income is taxed. For 2026, the 10% bracket applies to a single filer's taxable income from $0 to $12,400; the 12% bracket applies from $12,400 to $50,400. A child earning wages from the family business and having no other taxable income (after the standard deduction) will owe tax only on the amount over their standard deduction, at the 10% or 12% rate, compared to the parent who may be in the 22%, 24%, 32%, or higher bracket. (Source: IRS newsroom; Rev. Proc. 2025-32.)

**Self-employment tax:** The equivalent of FICA (Social Security and Medicare) that a sole proprietor pays on net self-employment income. The combined self-employment tax rate is 15.3% on the first $184,500 of net earnings (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare) for 2026, plus 2.9% Medicare on amounts above that (plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax above $200,000). When a sole proprietor pays wages to a child instead of keeping those funds as net profit, the sole proprietor reduces net self-employment income and saves self-employment tax on those wages -- in addition to the income tax savings.

**Schedule H:** IRS form used by household employers. This is generally NOT the correct form for family business employment. If the child is performing business-related work (not household services), payroll is run through the business using Form 941 or 944, not Schedule H.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before beginning:

1. **Your business entity type:** Sole proprietorship, single-member LLC taxed as a sole proprietor, partnership, S-corporation, or C-corporation. The entity type determines whether the FICA exemption applies.
2. **Your child's age:** FICA exemption applies under age 18; FUTA exemption applies under age 21. If the child will turn 18 during the year, FICA applies on wages paid after the birthday.
3. **Your child's Social Security Number (SSN):** Required for payroll setup and W-2 filing.
4. **Whether you already have an EIN:** You cannot run payroll without one. If you do not have one, you will need to apply on Form SS-4 before proceeding.
5. **Whether your business already has a payroll system:** If not, you will need one (a payroll service, or IRS EFTPS access plus quarterly filing capability).
6. **Whether your state has a state income tax:** If so, confirm state withholding requirements for minor employees with your advisor or payroll provider.
7. **A list of real tasks your child can perform for the business:** You will need this to establish a written job description and to justify the wage rate. Tasks should be things your business actually needs, that the child is capable of, and that an outside worker would also be paid to do.
8. **The market rate for those tasks:** Look at what a comparable, unrelated worker in your area would earn for the same services. That market rate is your ceiling for reasonable compensation.
9. **The hours per week or month your child will realistically work:** Your compensation must be grounded in actual hours.
10. **Whether the child has other sources of income or unearned income:** If the child has investment accounts, rental income, or other unearned income, the kiddie tax analysis becomes relevant. Share this information with your advisor before setting wages.

## Required Documents And Records

### To establish employment (do once, before first paycheck)

- **Written employment agreement or job description:** A dated document describing the position title, list of duties, agreed wage rate (hourly or per-project), and effective date. This does not need to be elaborate, but it must predate the first paycheck.
- **Form W-4, Employee's Withholding Certificate:** The child completes this form at the start of employment. Keep the signed original in the payroll file.
- **Employer Identification Number (EIN) documentation:** Keep a copy of your EIN confirmation letter (CP 575 or 147C letter from the IRS).
- **State new hire reporting:** Most states require employers to report new hires to a state agency within a specified number of days. Your payroll provider or state Department of Labor website has the form and deadlines. Confirm with your advisor.
- **State and local registration (if applicable):** Some states require registration as an employer before running payroll. Confirm with your advisor.

### To support each pay period (ongoing)

- **Timesheets or work logs:** A contemporaneous (recorded at the time, not reconstructed) record for each pay period showing the dates worked, the tasks performed, and the hours spent on each task. Paper, spreadsheet, or a time-tracking app all qualify. This is your most important audit document.
- **Payroll records:** A record for each payroll period showing gross wages, withholding amounts, and net pay. Your payroll service will generate these, or you can maintain a payroll register.
- **Proof of payment:** Bank records or cancelled checks showing the actual transfer of funds from the business account to the child. The wages must actually be paid; a paper entry with no cash movement is not deductible.

### Annual filings and records

- **Form W-2:** Issued to the child and filed with the Social Security Administration by January 31 of the following year.
- **Form W-3:** Transmittal form filed with the SSA along with Copy A of Form W-2. Due January 31.
- **Form 941 (quarterly):** Filed each quarter to report wages and payroll taxes withheld. Due dates: April 30 (Q1), July 31 (Q2), October 31 (Q3), January 31 (Q4). If all payroll taxes were deposited on time, you get 10 additional calendar days to file.
- **Form 940 (annual):** Filed annually to report FUTA tax. Due January 31 of the following year (with the same 10-day extension if taxes were timely deposited).
- **EFTPS (Electronic Federal Tax Payment System) deposit records:** All federal employment tax deposits must be made electronically through EFTPS. Keep deposit confirmation numbers.

### Retention period

Keep all payroll records for a minimum of four years after the fourth quarter of the year in which the taxes were due (per IRS employment tax recordkeeping guidance). Keep the W-4 and employment agreement for the same period after employment ends.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Set up employment (do once)

**Step 1 -- Confirm eligibility and strategy design with your advisor.**
Before anything else, confirm: (a) your entity type qualifies for the FICA/FUTA exemptions you are counting on, (b) your child is old enough and capable of performing real work, (c) the wages you intend to pay are reasonable for the market and the tasks, and (d) the income shift makes sense given the child's other income and the parent's tax situation. Do not proceed without this confirmation.

**Step 2 -- Obtain an EIN if you do not already have one.**
Go to IRS.gov and apply for an EIN online using the Form SS-4 application (takes about 15 minutes). You will receive your EIN immediately online. Write it down and save the confirmation. You cannot run payroll without an EIN.

**Step 3 -- Set up payroll.**
Choose one of these options:
- A payroll service provider (ADP, Gusto, QuickBooks Payroll, Patriot Payroll, or similar). These services automate tax withholding calculations, EFTPS deposits, quarterly Form 941 filings, and annual W-2 production. They also handle state payroll registrations in most states.
- Manual payroll with EFTPS access and quarterly self-filing. This requires you to calculate withholding manually using IRS Publication 15-T withholding tables, register for EFTPS at eftps.gov, make deposits by the required deadlines, and file Form 941 quarterly yourself. This option is free but labor-intensive.

For most clients, a payroll service is worth the monthly cost to avoid errors and late-filing penalties.

**Step 4 -- Register with your state for payroll (if required).**
Most states with income tax require employer registration before the first payroll. Your payroll provider will typically handle this, or your advisor can confirm the requirement and process for your state.

**Step 5 -- Have the child complete Form W-4.**
Print the current Form W-4 from IRS.gov. The child (or the parent if the child is too young to complete it themselves) fills in the child's name, address, and Social Security Number, and selects their filing status. A child with no other income who will earn below the standard deduction amount can claim "exempt" from withholding on the W-4 if their total income will be below the filing threshold. Your advisor or payroll provider can confirm the correct W-4 elections.

**Step 6 -- Write a job description and employment agreement.**
Write a one-page document (dated and signed by both the business and the child, or the parent on the child's behalf if the child is a minor) covering:
- The child's name and SSN
- The position title (e.g., "Social Media Assistant," "Office Assistant," "Marketing Photographer")
- A list of specific duties the child will perform
- The wage rate (hourly or per-project)
- The expected hours per week or per month
- The start date

File this in the payroll folder. This document establishes the bona fide employment relationship and the agreed wage rate before any money moves.

**Step 7 -- Complete state new hire reporting.**
Report the new hire to your state's new hire registry within the deadline required by your state. Your payroll provider may do this automatically; confirm.

### Phase 2: Run payroll (recurring, each pay period)

**Step 8 -- Have the child perform the work and keep a timesheet.**
The child must actually do the work. For each pay period, the child (or the parent, contemporaneously) records the date, the task completed, and the time spent. This is the single most important record to maintain. Do not pay wages for periods with no timesheet.

**Step 9 -- Calculate the paycheck.**
Multiply hours worked by the hourly rate to get gross wages. Use your payroll system or Publication 15-T to determine the withholding amount based on the child's W-4. For a child working for a sole proprietor and under age 18, no Social Security or Medicare withholding is taken. Federal income tax withholding is required regardless of age or entity type, unless the child claimed "exempt" on the W-4. Your payroll system will calculate these amounts automatically.

**Step 10 -- Issue the paycheck.**
Write a check or initiate a bank transfer from the business account to the child's account in the amount of net pay. Keep a record of the payment (bank statement or cancelled check). The wages are real money: it must actually leave the business account and go to the child.

**Step 11 -- Deposit payroll taxes (if applicable).**
If the child is under 18 and employed by a sole proprietor, Social Security and Medicare taxes are zero; only income tax withholding (if any) is owed. If income tax was withheld, deposit it through EFTPS on the schedule the IRS has assigned to you (monthly or semi-weekly depositor). Your payroll service handles this automatically. If the child claimed "exempt" from withholding and no tax was withheld, no deposit is required.

**Step 12 -- Record the wages as a business expense.**
In your bookkeeping system (QuickBooks, Wave, FreshBooks, or similar), record the payroll as wages expense under a category such as "Employee Wages" or "Payroll Expense." This records the deduction. The wages reduce your net business income dollar for dollar.

### Phase 3: Quarterly and annual tasks

**Step 13 -- File Form 941 each quarter.**
By April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31, file Form 941 reporting wages paid and any taxes withheld. If the child is under 18 and you are a sole proprietor, Social Security and Medicare wages are zero; report those wages in the exempt lines. Your payroll service generates and files Form 941 automatically.

**Step 14 -- File Form 940 annually.**
By January 31, file Form 940 reporting FUTA wages. Wages paid to a child under 21 by a qualifying sole proprietor or parent partnership are exempt from FUTA; report them in the exempt lines. If any FUTA tax was owed (for children over 20, or in corporate structures), deposit it quarterly when the cumulative liability reaches $500.

**Step 15 -- Issue Form W-2 by January 31.**
Your payroll system or CPA generates the W-2. The child receives Copy B and C by January 31. Copy A goes to the SSA with Form W-3 by the same date.

**Step 16 -- File the child's tax return (if required).**
The child must file a return if their gross income exceeds the lesser of the applicable filing threshold or the standard deduction for their situation. For 2026, a dependent child with only earned income must file if earned income exceeds the greater of $1,350 or their earned income plus $450 (up to $16,100). In practice, a child earning up to $15,650 in wages will owe zero federal income tax after the standard deduction, but must still file if their gross income exceeds the filing threshold. Your advisor prepares this return. Confirm state filing requirements separately.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

### Entity-specific caveats

- **Sole proprietorships and single-member LLCs (taxed as sole proprietors):** Full FICA and FUTA exemptions apply for children under 18 (FICA) and under 21 (FUTA). Self-employment tax savings are also captured because wages reduce the net profit subject to self-employment tax.
- **Husband-and-wife partnerships where BOTH partners are the child's parents:** The exemptions extend to this structure only if every partner is a parent of the child. If there is any other partner in the partnership, the exemptions do not apply and full payroll taxes are owed.
- **S-corporations:** No FICA or FUTA exemption. Full payroll taxes apply on all wages regardless of the child's age. The income-shifting deduction still works.
- **C-corporations:** Same as S-corporations -- no FICA or FUTA exemption for child employees. Full payroll taxes apply.
- **Multi-member LLCs taxed as partnerships (with non-parent partners):** No FICA or FUTA exemption. Full payroll taxes apply.

### Payroll caveats

- **The child must receive actual wages through payroll.** Cash payments, gifts of money, or payments described as "allowances" are not deductible wages. The child must receive a Form W-2 at year-end.
- **No 1099 for a child.** The IRS will not respect a parent-child independent contractor arrangement. If you issue a 1099 instead of a W-2, the IRS can reclassify the arrangement as employment, assess employment taxes, and disallow the contractor treatment.
- **The wages must be paid to the child, not to a custodial account you control.** The money must transfer to an account in the child's name (or a custodial account in which the child is the beneficiary).
- **Income tax withholding is required regardless of the FICA exemption.** Even if no Social Security or Medicare tax is withheld, you must still withhold federal income tax if the child's W-4 does not claim exemption.

### State caveats

- **State income taxes:** If your state has income taxes, the child's wages are generally subject to state income tax withholding. Confirm the withholding requirement and registration process with your advisor.
- **State payroll taxes (SUTA):** State unemployment insurance taxes are separate from FUTA. Some states may offer a family employment exemption similar to the federal FUTA exemption; others do not. Confirm with your state's labor or revenue agency.
- **State child labor laws:** Every state regulates the hours, types of work, and conditions of employment for minors. These laws are separate from tax law. Before starting employment, confirm that the tasks, hours, and work environment comply with your state's child labor laws. Violations can result in significant penalties unrelated to tax.
- **Multi-state businesses:** If your child performs work in a state different from your primary state of business, additional state tax registration and withholding may apply.

### Current-year caveats

- **OBBBA (One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, Public Law 119-21):** This legislation permanently extended many TCJA provisions and made the current income tax brackets permanent. The income-shifting benefit of this strategy is not expected to sunset. Confirm with your advisor that no provisions affecting family employee arrangements were changed.
- **2026 FICA rates and wage base are verified:** Social Security: 6.2% per side; Medicare: 1.45% per side; Social Security wage base: $184,500.
- **The FICA and FUTA exemptions under IRC Sections 3121 and 3306 are statutory provisions** that have been in place for decades and were not changed by the OBBBA. Confirm with your advisor that no legislative changes affect these exemptions for the current year.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Paying for work that does not exist.** Putting a child on payroll for "social media management" when no social media content was actually created is not deductible and is potentially fraudulent. The work must happen.

2. **No timesheet.** The single most common audit problem. Without a contemporaneous record of what the child did and when, the IRS can disallow the entire deduction. Start keeping timesheets before the first paycheck.

3. **Wages above market rate.** Paying a child $80,000 for tasks that a part-time outside employee earns $20,000 for will result in the excess being disallowed as a deduction and recharacterized as a gift. Research comparable wages before setting the rate.

4. **Paying in cash without a paper trail.** Wages paid in cash with no bank transfer, no payroll record, and no W-2 are not deductible. The IRS will treat them as personal gifts.

5. **Skipping payroll setup.** Deducting wages on Schedule C or the business return without actually running payroll, withholding taxes, and filing Form 941 will attract scrutiny and may result in employment tax penalties.

6. **Assuming the FICA exemption applies when the business is an S-corp.** A very common error. S-corporation status eliminates the exemption. If you recently converted from a sole proprietorship to an S-corp, confirm the impact on this strategy with your advisor.

7. **Forgetting that the exemption ends on the child's 18th birthday.** The FICA exemption ends the day the child turns 18, not at year-end. If your child turns 18 in April, FICA applies to all wages paid after that birthday.

8. **Issuing a 1099 instead of a W-2.** Family members who are employees must receive W-2s, not 1099s. A 1099 creates self-employment income on the child's return and may be reclassified on audit.

9. **Not checking state child labor law.** Even if the federal tax treatment is perfect, having an underage employee performing prohibited work or prohibited hours violates labor law and can result in penalties and legal liability.

10. **No written job description before the first paycheck.** An after-the-fact description drafted at audit is far less convincing than one dated and signed before employment began.

11. **Forgetting to file Form W-2 and W-3 with the SSA.** Issuing a W-2 to the child without filing Copy A and Form W-3 with the Social Security Administration results in penalties of $60 to $310 per return (2026 rates), depending on how late the filing is. Your payroll service handles this; confirm it is included in your service.

12. **Using the child's wages to pay the child's personal expenses (clothing, entertainment, tuition) that benefit the parent.** The money must actually be paid to the child and used by the child. If the parent retains control and uses it for the parent's benefit, the IRS can disallow the deduction.

## Advisor Review Gates

Do not proceed through the following decision points without first confirming with your advisor:

1. **Before setting up employment:** Confirm your entity type, the FICA and FUTA treatment that applies, and whether income-shifting makes sense given your current-year income, the child's other income, and the kiddie tax analysis.

2. **Before setting the wage rate:** Have your advisor review the planned wage rate against market comparables and confirm it is reasonable for the work the child will actually perform.

3. **Before the first paycheck:** Confirm that the employment agreement and job description are in place, payroll is set up, the W-4 is on file, and any required state registrations have been completed.

4. **If your child turns 18 during the year:** Confirm with your advisor or payroll provider that FICA withholding begins on wages paid after the birthday and that the quarterly Form 941 is adjusted correctly.

5. **If your business structure changes:** A change from sole proprietor to S-corp (or any other entity change) affects the FICA and FUTA treatment. Confirm the impact with your advisor before continuing to pay wages to a child employee.

6. **Before filing the child's tax return:** Confirm the correct W-4 status, whether the child must file, and whether any state filing obligations apply.

7. **Any time wages paid to a child exceed $20,000 in a year:** Higher wage amounts increase audit risk and require stronger documentation of reasonableness. Have your advisor review the full compensation picture.

## Before You Act

**Stop here before you issue the first paycheck to your child.**

The tax benefits of this strategy are real and substantial, but they depend entirely on the employment being genuine, the wages being reasonable, and the records being kept from the very beginning. The IRS scrutinizes family employment arrangements precisely because they are easy to abuse and hard to defend without solid documentation.

If you issue the first paycheck before the job description is written, before the W-4 is signed, before payroll is set up, or before you have confirmed that the FICA exemption actually applies to your entity, you may pay wages that are not deductible, owe employment taxes you did not withhold, and face penalties for late filings.

Before you act:
- Confirm your entity type and the applicable payroll tax treatment with your advisor.
- Confirm the wage rate is reasonable by reference to market comparables.
- Confirm state child labor law permits the work before the child does a single hour.
- Set up payroll, complete the W-4, and write the job description -- all before the first paycheck.
- If the child will turn 18 during the year, put a calendar reminder on the birthday so FICA withholding begins on the next payroll after that date.

This page and the attached Quick Reference Guide teach the strategy. Your advisor applies it to your specific facts, entity structure, income levels, and state.
