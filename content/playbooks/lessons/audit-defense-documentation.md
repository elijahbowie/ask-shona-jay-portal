# Audit Defense Documentation

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Audit defense documentation is the practice of building and maintaining a complete, organized paper trail that proves every item on your tax return -- before the IRS ever asks for it. A strong documentation system does two things: it dramatically reduces the chance your return is selected for a closer look, and it lets you respond quickly and confidently if it is. Business owners who maintain contemporaneous records (records created at or near the time of the transaction) are far better positioned to resolve audits with no change to their tax bill than those who reconstruct records after receiving an audit notice.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook applies to you if any of the following is true:

- You own or operate a business, regardless of entity type (sole proprietorship, S corporation, C corporation, partnership, or LLC).
- You claim deductions for business expenses including travel, meals, vehicle use, home office, or depreciation on assets.
- You employ workers or pay independent contractors.
- You have received an IRS examination notice, a correspondence audit letter, or a CP2000 notice (a notice proposing changes based on information the IRS received from third parties such as banks or payment processors).
- You want to put systems in place now so that a future audit does not disrupt your business or result in unnecessary penalties.

This playbook is also useful for sole proprietors filing Schedule C (the form attached to Form 1040 used to report profit or loss from a business) and S corporation shareholders who take both salary and profit distributions.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- You are a W-2 employee with no self-employment or business activity. Your documentation needs are simpler; consult the standard recordkeeping guides for individual filers.
- Your only concern is a personal, non-business tax item such as a charitable contribution or medical expense. Those deductions have their own documentation rules and are not the primary focus here.
- You have already received a Notice of Deficiency (the formal IRS letter, sometimes called the "90-day letter," proposing additional tax and giving you 90 days to petition the U.S. Tax Court). If you are at that stage, stop and contact your advisor immediately -- the deadlines are statutory and cannot be extended.
- Your situation involves a potential fraud allegation. Nothing in this playbook substitutes for immediate representation by a qualified tax professional in a fraud or criminal investigation context.

## Key Terms

**Audit (Examination):** An official IRS review of your tax return to verify that the income, deductions, and credits you reported are accurate and supported by records.

**Correspondence Audit:** The most common type of audit, conducted entirely by mail. The IRS sends a letter requesting specific documents. You reply with copies.

**Office Audit:** An in-person meeting at an IRS facility where an examiner reviews your records.

**Field Audit:** An in-person examination at your home, your place of business, or your representative's office. Usually reserved for larger, more complex returns.

**Statute of Limitations:** The window of time during which the IRS can legally assess additional tax. For most returns, this is 3 years from the date you filed (or the return due date, whichever is later). See "The Numbers" section for extended periods.

**Assessment Statute Expiration Date (ASED):** The specific date on which the IRS's right to assess additional tax expires for a given return year.

**Notice of Deficiency (90-Day Letter):** A statutory letter (IRS Letter 3219) formally proposing additional tax. You have 90 days from the date of the letter to petition the U.S. Tax Court without first paying the proposed amount. This deadline cannot be extended.

**30-Day Letter (Letter 525 or Letter 915):** A letter the IRS sends after a proposed audit adjustment, giving you 30 days to agree, request an Appeals conference, or provide additional documentation. This comes before the Notice of Deficiency.

**Form 4549:** The IRS form titled "Income Tax Examination Changes," enclosed with a 30-day letter, that lists the specific adjustments the examiner is proposing.

**Form 886-A:** The IRS explanation sheet attached to Form 4549 that describes the reasons for each proposed adjustment.

**IRS Office of Appeals:** An independent IRS function that offers taxpayers a conference to dispute audit findings before going to court. This is the only level of administrative appeal within the IRS.

**Tax Court:** The U.S. Tax Court is the primary forum for disputing an IRS deficiency before paying it. You file a petition within 90 days of the Notice of Deficiency.

**Contemporaneous Records:** Records created at or near the time of the expense or transaction. The IRS gives these far more weight than reconstructed records created months or years later.

**IRC Section 274(d):** The Internal Revenue Code section imposing strict substantiation rules for travel, meals, vehicle, and gift expenses. The Cohan rule (a judicial doctrine allowing courts to estimate deductions when exact records are missing) does not apply to these categories; the IRS will disallow the deduction entirely if the required documentation is missing.

**Cohan Rule:** A judicial doctrine, originating from a Second Circuit court case, that allows courts to estimate some business deductions when exact records are unavailable. It does not apply to IRC Section 274(d) expenses (travel, meals, vehicles, gifts) or to listed property.

**IRC Section 6662:** The Internal Revenue Code section imposing accuracy-related penalties for negligence or substantial understatement of tax. See "The Numbers" for the penalty rates.

**Reasonable Cause:** A legal standard under which the IRS may waive penalties if you can show you acted in good faith and took reasonable steps to comply with the tax law. Strong documentation is evidence of reasonable cause.

**Form 2848:** The IRS Power of Attorney form that authorizes a representative (attorney, CPA, or enrolled agent) to act on your behalf before the IRS.

**Enrolled Agent (EA):** A tax professional licensed by the IRS who has passed a comprehensive examination or worked for the IRS and is authorized to represent taxpayers in all matters before the IRS.

**Discriminant Information Function (DIF) System:** The IRS's computer scoring system that compares your return to statistical norms for similar businesses. Returns with scores outside the norm are more likely to be selected for examination. The IRS does not publish the specific formulas.

**CP2000 Notice:** An IRS notice proposing changes to your return based on a mismatch between what you reported and what third parties (banks, brokers, clients) reported to the IRS. Not the same as a full audit, but requires a documented response.

**Document Upload Tool (DUT):** The IRS's secure online portal for submitting audit response documents electronically. Use the QR code on your specific IRS notice to access the correct DUT portal.

**Form 8829:** The IRS form used to calculate and claim the home office deduction for Schedule C filers. Requires documentation of business use percentage.

**Listed Property:** Assets subject to stricter IRS substantiation rules, including vehicles, computers, and certain other property that can be used for both business and personal purposes.

**Per Diem Rate:** An IRS-approved daily allowance rate that business travelers can use to estimate lodging and meal expenses instead of tracking actual costs. Using per diem does not eliminate the need to document business purpose, destination, and travel dates.

**First Time Abatement (FTA):** An administrative IRS waiver that removes certain penalties for taxpayers who have a clean compliance history for the prior 3 years. Not available for accuracy-related penalties under IRC 6662.

**Audit Reconsideration:** An IRS process that allows you to reopen a closed audit if you have new information that was not considered, or if you did not respond to the original audit notice. A formal audit reconsideration request does not require a special form -- a letter with supporting documentation is sufficient.

**Form 843:** The IRS form used to claim a refund or request abatement of penalties after the tax has been assessed and paid.

**Form 911:** The Taxpayer Advocate Service assistance request form. Filing this form requests help from the Taxpayer Advocate Service, an independent organization within the IRS that assists taxpayers experiencing financial hardship or significant delays.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before starting your documentation system:

1. Copies of all filed tax returns for the past 7 years (or since the business began, if shorter). You need these to understand what you have already claimed and to verify the applicable statute of limitations for each year.
2. A complete list of all deduction categories you claim: travel, meals, vehicle, home office, depreciation, payroll, contractor payments, etc.
3. Bank statements and credit card statements for all accounts used for business, going back to the open statute of limitations years.
4. All Forms 1099-NEC, 1099-MISC, 1099-K, and W-2 you have issued or received.
5. Payroll records if you have employees, including all Form 941 (Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return) filings.
6. Property records for any assets (real estate, equipment, vehicles) used in the business, including purchase agreements, closing statements, and depreciation schedules.
7. Entity formation documents (articles of incorporation, operating agreement, S corporation election on Form 2553) if you operate through a corporation, partnership, or LLC.
8. Any prior IRS notices, audit reports, or correspondence you have already received.

If you received an audit notice, also gather: the specific IRS notice or letter (note the letter number, the tax year(s) under examination, the deadline for response, and the contact information for the examiner or the IRS return address).

## Required Documents And Records

### Income Records (keep for at least 3 years from filing; 6 years if unreported income could exceed 25% of gross)

- Cash register tapes or point-of-sale system reports
- Bank deposit slips and bank statements
- Receipt books and sales invoices
- Credit card charge slips and merchant processing statements
- Forms 1099-K, 1099-NEC, and 1099-MISC received
- Signed client contracts and invoices

### Business Expense Records (keep for at least 3 years from filing)

- Canceled checks or bank statements clearly identifying the payee and amount
- Vendor invoices and receipts (required for all expenses; receipts showing amount and merchant are especially important for any expense of $75 or more)
- Credit card statements with itemized descriptions
- Account statements

### Travel and Meals Records (IRC Section 274(d) -- strict rules apply; keep for at least 3 years)

For each trip or meal, document all five elements at or near the time of the expense:
1. Amount of the expense
2. Date of travel or meal
3. Location (destination for travel; restaurant name and city for meals)
4. Business purpose (the specific business reason, not just "business meeting")
5. Business relationship (names and business relationship of people present at meals)

Maintain a mileage log for vehicle expenses. The log must show date, destination, business purpose, and business miles for each trip. The 2026 standard business mileage rate is 72.5 cents per mile (IRS Notice 2026-10).

### Home Office Records (keep for at least 3 years; property records until the year of sale plus 3 years)

- Measurements of the dedicated office space and total home square footage
- Utility bills, mortgage statements or rent receipts, homeowners or renters insurance, property tax records
- Photos of the space showing exclusive, regular business use
- Form 8829 for each year the deduction is claimed

### Asset and Property Records (keep until 3 years after the year you dispose of the asset)

- Purchase invoices, real estate closing statements, canceled checks
- Records of all improvements and their costs
- Depreciation schedules showing the method used and amounts deducted each year
- Sale or disposal documentation

### Employment and Payroll Records (keep for at least 4 years after the tax becomes due or is paid, whichever is later)

- All Form W-2s issued, with proof of delivery
- All Form 1099-NEC and 1099-MISC issued to contractors
- Payroll registers and records of wages, bonuses, and benefits paid
- Filed Form 941 (quarterly payroll tax returns) and Form 940 (annual FUTA return)
- Worker classification documentation for independent contractors (signed contracts, evidence that the contractor controls how the work is done, contractor's own business license if applicable)

### S Corporation Owner Records (keep for at least 3 years; longer for salary-versus-distribution decisions)

- Board minutes or written resolutions documenting officer compensation decisions
- Payroll records showing salary paid to shareholder-employees
- Comparable wage data or compensation studies supporting the reasonableness of the salary (the IRS can reclassify distributions as wages subject to employment taxes if the salary is below what a non-owner doing the same work would be paid)

### General Retention Summary

| Record Type | Minimum Retention Period |
|---|---|
| Standard income and expense records | 3 years from filing date |
| Employment tax records | 4 years after tax due or paid |
| Records if unreported income exceeds 25% of gross | 6 years from filing date |
| Property records | 3 years after the year you dispose of the property |
| Worthless securities or bad debt deduction records | 7 years from filing date |
| Records if no return was filed | Indefinitely |
| Records if a fraudulent return was filed | Indefinitely |

### Format Requirements

You may keep records in paper or electronic format. The IRS accepts electronic records, including scanned copies of receipts and digital accounting software files, provided the records are legible, accurate, and maintained with an audit trail linking each entry to the underlying source document. Revenue Procedure 98-25 (IRS guidance on machine-sensible records) requires that electronic records be capable of being read and tested by the IRS. If you submit electronic accounting software backup files during an audit, submit the original backup file, not an exported report or spreadsheet copy, so the IRS can verify the integrity of the data.

Do not send original documents to the IRS. Always send copies.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Set Up Your Documentation System (do this now, before any audit notice)

**Step 1: Separate business and personal finances completely.**
Open a dedicated business checking account and a dedicated business credit card. Run all business income and expenses through these accounts. The single most common documentation problem in IRS audits is commingled (mixed) personal and business transactions that must be sorted out during the examination. Output: a clean separation of business and personal accounts.

**Step 2: Choose a recordkeeping method and stick with it.**
Select one of the following: accounting software (cloud-based or desktop), a bookkeeper or CPA who maintains your records, or a structured spreadsheet system. The IRS does not require a specific system, but it must clearly and accurately reflect your income and expenses. Output: a single, consistent source of truth for your financial records.

**Step 3: Create a filing system for source documents.**
Organize receipts and supporting documents by category and tax year. Many business owners scan all receipts using a smartphone app and store them in a cloud folder organized by year and category (for example: 2025 / Meals / January). Retain the electronic copies permanently and the paper copies until you confirm the electronic copies are legible and complete. Output: a filing system where any receipt or document can be located within five minutes.

**Step 4: Build your mileage log.**
If you deduct vehicle expenses, begin a contemporaneous mileage log today. Record the date, starting and ending odometer reading (or total business miles), destination, and business purpose for every business trip. A smartphone app, calendar entries, or a paper log in your vehicle all work. Reconstruct this log annually using maps or calendar records if needed, but contemporaneous logs are far stronger in an audit. Output: a mileage log updated each time you drive for business.

**Step 5: Document meals and travel in real time.**
For every business meal or trip, record all five IRC Section 274(d) elements (amount, date, location, business purpose, business relationship) within a few days of the expense. A photo of the receipt with a note written on it or a brief entry in a calendar or expense app satisfies this requirement. The Cohan rule does not protect these expenses -- missing documentation means a disallowed deduction. Output: a meals and travel log with contemporaneous entries.

**Step 6: Photograph and measure your home office (if applicable).**
If you claim a home office deduction, measure the square footage of the dedicated business space and the total home square footage. Photograph the space to document exclusive business use (meaning no personal use -- a spare bedroom with a guest bed does not qualify). Calculate the business use percentage by dividing business square footage by total home square footage. This percentage is used on Form 8829. Keep these records as long as you claim the deduction plus 3 years. Output: measured square footage, photos, and Form 8829 for each year.

**Step 7: Set up an annual documentation review.**
At least once a year, before filing your return, pull every receipt and record that supports your largest deductions and confirm they are complete. Use the Audit Defense Documentation Implementation Worksheet (see "Using The Download(s)") to run through each category. Output: a completed annual checklist confirming all documentation is in order before filing.

### Phase 2: Responding to an Audit Notice

**Step 8: Read the notice immediately and identify the deadline.**
When you receive any IRS letter, note the date of the letter, the tax year(s) under examination, the response deadline, the type of notice (correspondence audit, office audit, field audit, CP2000), and the specific items under examination. The deadline is measured from the date on the letter, not the date you received it. For correspondence audits, the IRS can grant a one-time automatic 30-day extension if you request it in writing before the original deadline.

**Step 9: Do not ignore the notice and do not respond alone.**
Contact your advisor before responding. If the notice relates to complex issues (business income, S corporation distributions, employment taxes, or any penalty exceeding $5,000), file Form 2848 to authorize your advisor to communicate with the IRS on your behalf. Your advisor can communicate directly with the examiner, request an extension, and ensure your response does not inadvertently expand the scope of the examination.

**Step 10: Gather only the documents requested.**
Pull the specific records for the specific items and tax years listed in the notice. Do not volunteer additional information or provide documents covering years or issues not mentioned in the notice. Organize the documents in the same order as the IRS requested them. Label each document with the item it supports.

**Step 11: Prepare a written response.**
Write a clear, factual cover letter that: identifies your name, taxpayer identification number (Social Security Number or Employer Identification Number), the tax year under examination, the notice or letter number, and lists each item requested along with the enclosed document that responds to it. Keep the tone neutral and factual.

**Step 12: Submit copies (never originals) and document your submission.**
Mail copies of all documents via certified mail with return receipt requested, or submit through the IRS Document Upload Tool (DUT) using the QR code on your notice. Retain a complete copy of everything you submit, along with proof of submission (certified mail receipt or DUT confirmation).

**Step 13: Keep a communication log.**
Record every interaction with the IRS: date, method (phone, mail, upload), the name and badge number of any IRS employee you spoke with, and a summary of what was discussed or agreed upon.

**Step 14: Review the proposed changes (Form 4549) carefully.**
If the IRS sends Form 4549 (Income Tax Examination Changes) with a 30-day letter, compare each proposed adjustment to your records. If you agree with all adjustments, sign and return the form and pay any additional tax, interest, and penalties owed. If you disagree with any adjustment, do not sign.

**Step 15: Exercise your appeal rights if you disagree.**
If you disagree with any proposed adjustment:
- Within 30 days of receiving the 30-day letter: request a conference with the IRS Office of Appeals.
- If you receive a Notice of Deficiency (90-day letter): you have 90 days from the date of the letter to file a petition with the U.S. Tax Court (150 days if the letter is addressed to an address outside the United States). This deadline is set by statute and cannot be extended.
- For disputes of $50,000 or less per tax year: consider the Tax Court's simplified "S" case procedures, which are less formal and typically faster. Note that S case decisions cannot be appealed to a higher court by either party.

**Step 16: Consider audit reconsideration if new information exists.**
If the audit closed without your full participation (for example, you did not respond to the original notice and the IRS made automatic adjustments), or if you have new documentation that was not reviewed, you can request an audit reconsideration. Write a letter to the IRS identifying the closed audit, explaining why you are requesting reconsideration, and enclosing copies of the new documentation along with a copy of Form 4549 from the original audit if available. The IRS will respond within approximately 30 days, though the process can take several months. If the reconsideration is denied, you can request an Appeals conference.

## Timing And Deadlines

| Event | Deadline |
|---|---|
| Response to correspondence audit notice | Date shown on the notice (typically 30 days from notice date) |
| Extension request for correspondence audit | Before the original deadline; grants one additional 30-day period |
| Response to 30-day letter (Letter 525 or 915) | 30 days from the letter date to request Appeals conference |
| Petition to U.S. Tax Court after Notice of Deficiency | 90 days from the date of the notice (150 days if addressed outside the U.S.); cannot be extended |
| Audit reconsideration request | No hard deadline, but file as soon as possible; the IRS may require payment arrangements if tax has been assessed |
| Refund claim after paying assessed tax (Form 843) | 3 years from the original return filing date or 2 years from payment, whichever is later |
| Standard IRS assessment window | 3 years from the filing date (or due date, whichever is later) of the return under examination |
| Extended assessment window (substantial omission) | 6 years from the filing date if unreported income exceeds 25% of gross income |
| Employment tax records must be kept | At least 4 years after the tax becomes due or is paid |

**Recurring cadence:**
- Monthly: Reconcile bank and credit card statements; scan and file receipts; update mileage log.
- Quarterly: Review deduction categories; confirm all 1099-NEC and payroll forms are on track for filing.
- Annually (before filing): Complete the Audit Defense Documentation Implementation Worksheet; confirm all records are organized and retained.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Sole proprietors (Schedule C):** Schedule C filers are examined at higher rates than other business structures. The DIF scoring system flags expense ratios outside industry norms. Round-number deductions (exactly $5,000 for advertising, for example) raise examiner scrutiny because real transactions rarely produce perfectly round amounts. Document every Schedule C deduction with a receipt or contemporaneous record.

**S corporations:** The IRS specifically targets S corporation returns where shareholder-employees take distributions (non-wage profit payments) without paying themselves a reasonable salary first. Distributions are not subject to payroll taxes; salaries are. If the IRS determines a salary was unreasonably low, it can reclassify distributions as wages and assess back payroll taxes, interest, and penalties. Document officer compensation decisions in board minutes or written resolutions and retain comparable wage data supporting the reasonableness of the salary paid. Your advisor can help determine what the IRS considers reasonable.

**Partnerships and multi-member LLCs:** Partner draws and guaranteed payments are treated differently from wages. Document each partner's capital account, basis calculations, and any special allocations in the partnership agreement. The IRS can audit a partnership at the entity level under TEFRA (the Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act of 1982) or BBA (Bipartisan Budget Act) partnership audit rules, which can result in adjustments at the partnership level that flow through to partners. Consult your advisor on whether you have a BBA election in place.

**Workers classified as independent contractors:** If the IRS reclassifies your independent contractors as employees, you may owe back FICA taxes (Social Security and Medicare taxes), federal unemployment taxes, interest, and penalties. Document worker classification by retaining signed independent contractor agreements, evidence that contractors control how they perform the work (behavioral control), evidence that the financial terms are those of a business relationship rather than an employment relationship (financial control), and the contractor's business information such as a business license or EIN.

**State income tax audits:** Most states have their own audit programs and their own statutes of limitations. Many states begin their own examination when the IRS adjusts your federal return. If the IRS changes your federal taxable income, you are generally required to report that change to the state within a specified period (this varies by state). Confirm the reporting requirement for your specific state with your advisor.

**Current-year caveats:** The TCJA (Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, signed December 2017) made significant changes to business deductions including entertainment expenses (entertainment is no longer deductible; only qualifying business meals remain at 50%), depreciation, and the qualified business income (QBI) deduction under IRC Section 199A. Regarding depreciation: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, signed July 2025) permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation (additional first-year depreciation under IRC Section 168(k)) for most qualifying property -- property with a recovery period of 20 years or less -- acquired after January 19, 2025. The prior TCJA phasedown schedule no longer applies to property acquired after that date. See IRS Notice 2026-11 and the Treasury/IRS OBBBA guidance at irs.gov/newsroom for details. Regarding meals: beginning in 2026, employer-provided meals that were previously deductible at 50% (or under Section 119 or the de minimis fringe benefit rules) are no longer deductible by the employer under OBBBA -- the 50% employer deduction for on-premises and convenience-of-employer meals is eliminated for amounts paid or incurred after December 31, 2025. The 50% deduction for ordinary business meals with clients and business associates continues. Confirm that any deductions you are claiming are still available under current law with your advisor, particularly any provision that was set to expire or change after 2025.

**Cryptocurrency and digital assets:** For tax year 2026, custodial brokers and exchanges must report gross proceeds on Form 1099-DA. If you receive a Form 1099-DA, the IRS will match those amounts against your return. Document every digital asset transaction with: date of acquisition, cost basis (what you paid), date of sale or exchange, proceeds received, and the wallet address or exchange account involved. Discrepancies between Form 1099-DA and your return will trigger an automated matching notice.

**1099-K reporting:** Payment processors (credit card companies, PayPal, Venmo, Stripe, and similar platforms) report gross payments to the IRS on Form 1099-K. The IRS matches these figures to your reported income. If you receive a Form 1099-K showing amounts that include personal transactions, document the non-business amounts separately so you can reconcile the gross figure to your actual taxable income. The OBBBA retroactively reinstated the pre-ARPA reporting threshold: for 2025 and later years, third-party settlement organizations (TPSOs such as PayPal, Venmo, and Stripe) are required to issue Form 1099-K only when the gross amount of reportable transactions exceeds $20,000 AND the number of transactions exceeds 200 for the year. Both conditions must be met. Important exception: payment card transactions (credit and debit card payments processed through a merchant acquirer) are not subject to any minimum threshold and must be reported regardless of dollar amount. See IRS Newsroom, "IRS issues FAQs on Form 1099-K threshold under the One, Big, Beautiful Bill; dollar limit reverts to $20,000" (Fact Sheet 2025-08).

## Common Mistakes

**Waiting until after the audit notice to organize records.** Reconstructed records carry far less weight than contemporaneous ones. The time to build your documentation system is before you receive any notice.

**Mixing personal and business expenses on the same account.** Commingled accounts require the IRS examiner to make assumptions about which transactions are deductible, and those assumptions favor the IRS.

**Sending original documents to the IRS.** Always send copies. Originals submitted to the IRS can be lost and are difficult to retrieve.

**Providing more documents than requested.** Responding to a narrow audit with a broad document dump can inadvertently raise new questions and expand the scope of the examination. Respond precisely to what was asked.

**Missing the response deadline without requesting an extension.** If you miss the deadline on a correspondence audit, the IRS will disallow the questioned items and issue a Notice of Deficiency. At that point your options narrow significantly.

**Ignoring a Notice of Deficiency.** If the 90-day deadline passes without action, the IRS assesses the tax and you lose the right to contest it in Tax Court without first paying. The only remaining options are to pay, file a refund claim, and sue in district court or the Court of Federal Claims, or to pursue audit reconsideration -- all more costly and time-consuming than responding to the original notice.

**Claiming a home office deduction without documenting exclusive use.** The space must be used regularly and exclusively for business. Occasional personal use destroys the deduction for the entire space. No partial credit is available for mixed-use spaces (with limited exceptions for storage or daycare providers).

**Keeping a mileage log that shows only totals, not individual trips.** The IRS requires a record of each business trip: date, destination, and purpose. An annual mileage total with no supporting detail does not satisfy the IRC Section 274(d) substantiation requirement.

**Failing to document the business relationship and purpose for meals.** "Business meeting" is not sufficient. Identify who was present, their company and role, and the specific business topic discussed.

**Failing to report income shown on information returns (1099s, W-2s).** The IRS's automated matching system compares every information return to your filed return. Unreported income is one of the fastest ways to trigger an examination.

**Not keeping property records after selling an asset.** Capital gains and depreciation recapture calculations require you to know your original cost, improvement costs, and depreciation claimed. If you cannot document your basis, the IRS can treat your entire sale proceeds as gain.

**Not involving a qualified representative early enough.** In audits involving employment taxes, S corporation compensation, or any proposed adjustment exceeding $10,000, the cost of representation is almost always less than the cost of navigating the examination alone.

## Advisor Review Gates

Pause and consult your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

- You receive any IRS notice, letter, or phone call claiming to be from the IRS. (The IRS initiates audits by mail only; IRS agents do not demand immediate payment by phone. Verify any phone contact by calling the IRS directly at 1-800-829-1040 before responding.)
- Your audit involves a proposed adjustment of more than $5,000.
- The notice covers more than one tax year.
- The notice involves employment taxes, payroll reclassification, or independent contractor classification.
- The notice involves S corporation distributions or officer compensation.
- You are considering signing Form 4549 to agree to the proposed adjustments. Your advisor should review the form before you sign.
- You receive a 30-day letter and are considering whether to request an Appeals conference. The decision to appeal has strategic implications.
- You receive a Notice of Deficiency. The 90-day deadline is statutory and approaching.
- You are considering Tax Court or other litigation.
- The audit involves criminal fraud allegations of any kind.
- You have lost records that are central to your defense and are considering reconstructed documentation.
- You are operating in a state that has separate audit procedures triggered by the federal examination.
- You want to authorize someone to represent you before the IRS. Use Form 2848 (Power of Attorney) and have your advisor assist with completing it.

## Using The Download(s)

**Audit Defense Documentation Implementation Worksheet**

Use this worksheet at two points in the process:

1. **Annual documentation review (before filing):** Work through the worksheet once per year, before you file your tax return. The worksheet walks you through each major deduction category -- income records, travel and meals, vehicle, home office, payroll, assets -- and asks you to confirm that the supporting documents are complete, organized, and retained in the correct location. Complete the worksheet and retain it with your tax records as evidence of your documentation review process.

2. **Audit response preparation:** If you receive an audit notice, use the worksheet immediately to identify which documents you have and which may be missing or incomplete. The worksheet's category-by-category structure matches the IRS's typical request format for correspondence audits and will help you build your response packet efficiently.

How to fill it out: Work through each section in order. For each deduction category, note the total amount claimed, the number of supporting documents you have, the location where they are stored (folder name, cloud location, filing cabinet label), and any gaps. Flag any category where documentation is incomplete so you can address it before filing or before responding to a notice.

## Completion Checklist

Use this checklist to confirm you have completed the core implementation steps.

- [ ] Business and personal bank accounts are fully separated.
- [ ] A consistent accounting or recordkeeping system is in place and up to date.
- [ ] A filing system for source documents (paper or digital) is set up by year and category.
- [ ] A contemporaneous mileage log is being maintained for every business vehicle trip.
- [ ] All five IRC Section 274(d) elements (amount, date, location, business purpose, business relationship) are recorded for every business meal within a few days of the expense.
- [ ] Home office square footage is measured and documented; photos of the dedicated space are on file.
- [ ] Asset records (purchase, improvements, depreciation) are retained and organized.
- [ ] Employment and payroll records are retained for at least 4 years after the tax becomes due or is paid.
- [ ] S corporation officer compensation decisions are documented in writing (board minutes or written resolutions).
- [ ] All Forms 1099-NEC, 1099-K, and W-2 received are reconciled to reported income.
- [ ] The Audit Defense Documentation Implementation Worksheet has been completed for the current tax year.
- [ ] All records are retained for at least the applicable statute of limitations period (see the table in "Required Documents And Records").
- [ ] Electronic records are stored in a format that can be read and produced to the IRS if requested.
- [ ] You know the response deadline on any pending IRS notice and have calendared it.
- [ ] Form 2848 has been completed if you are authorizing a representative for a pending examination.
- [ ] Your advisor has reviewed any proposed IRS adjustment (Form 4549) before you signed it.

## Before You Act

Before filing, making any deduction claim, changing how you classify workers, reimbursing yourself or employees, adjusting officer compensation in an S corporation, or responding to any IRS notice, confirm your specific facts with your advisor. The rules governing documentation, substantiation, and audit response are detailed and depend heavily on your particular business structure, the type of expenses involved, and the specific examination at issue.

The figures, deadlines, and procedures in this playbook are accurate as of the date of publication based on verified IRS sources. Tax law changes. Deadlines are absolute. Do not act on a statutory deadline (such as the 90-day petition window after a Notice of Deficiency) without first speaking with your advisor.

If you are experiencing financial hardship as a result of an IRS audit or collection action, contact the Taxpayer Advocate Service by submitting Form 911 or by calling 1-877-777-4778. The service is free.
