# Missing Refunds and IRS Account Review

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

When a federal tax refund does not arrive on time or appears to have gone missing, there is a defined set of IRS tools and forms that let you diagnose exactly what happened and take corrective action. This playbook walks you through checking your refund status, reading your IRS Online Account transcript to identify why a refund was delayed or offset, tracing a lost check or misdirected direct deposit, responding to an identity verification hold, recovering a refund taken by the Bureau of the Fiscal Service to pay an old debt, and claiming a prior-year refund before the statute of limitations expires. Acting within the correct windows protects your money.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook applies to you if any of the following describe your situation:

- **You filed a federal tax return and the refund has not arrived within the normal processing window:** E-filed returns with direct deposit typically arrive within 3 weeks. Paper-filed returns take 6 or more weeks. If you are outside those windows, this playbook applies.
- **You never received a direct deposit and it has been more than 5 calendar days past the deposit date shown in Where's My Refund:** The deposit may have been rejected by your bank or sent to a wrong account.
- **You expected a paper refund check and it has been more than 4 weeks since the IRS issued it:** Beginning September 30, 2025, the IRS generally stopped issuing paper checks to individuals; this affects how a missing payment is recovered.
- **Your refund was smaller than expected:** Part of your refund may have been offset (applied) to a past-due debt you or your spouse owe.
- **You received an IRS CP05 or CP05A notice:** These notices tell you the IRS is holding your refund pending a review of your income or withholding information.
- **You have an unfiled or unclaimed refund from a prior tax year (2022 or earlier):** There is a strict deadline to claim it before the money is permanently forfeited.
- **Your Where's My Refund status shows "We cannot provide any information about your refund" and you filed more than 4 weeks ago:** This may indicate a processing issue that requires further action.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Your refund is still within the normal processing window:** E-file plus direct deposit -- fewer than 21 days. Paper return -- fewer than 6 weeks. The IRS will not start a trace until these windows have passed. Wait and check again.
- **You are still in an active audit or the IRS is reviewing your return:** A refund may be held during an examination. This playbook covers administrative refund issues, not audit disputes. Contact your advisor.
- **You owe a balance for the same tax year:** The IRS will apply your overpayment against any balance due before issuing a refund. If your return showed both an overpayment and a balance due, no refund is owed until the net position is resolved.
- **Your refund is being held because of an unfiled prior-year return:** The IRS can hold current-year refunds when older returns are missing. Your advisor can help sequence the filings.
- **You received a CP3219A (Statutory Notice of Deficiency):** That notice governs a formal tax adjustment, not a missing refund. See the IRS Account, Transcript, Notice, and Refund Workflow playbook.

## Key Terms

**Where's My Refund:** The free IRS online tool at irs.gov/refunds that lets you check the status of your federal income tax refund. Also available as the IRS2Go mobile app. No sign-in is required; you enter your Social Security number or ITIN (Individual Taxpayer Identification Number), filing status, and exact refund amount from your return. Status updates once per day, usually overnight.

**IRS2Go:** The official IRS mobile application for iOS and Android. Includes the same Where's My Refund functionality as the website and also links to IRS Direct Pay and Free File.

**SSN (Social Security Number):** A nine-digit number issued by the Social Security Administration used to identify you on your tax return and with IRS tools.

**ITIN (Individual Taxpayer Identification Number):** A tax processing number issued by the IRS to people who are not eligible for an SSN. Format is the same as an SSN (nine digits). Used in place of an SSN for tax filing purposes.

**IRS Online Account:** The individual taxpayer self-service portal at irs.gov where you can view your tax account transcripts, payment history, IRS notices, refund status, and balance due. Access requires identity verification through ID.me, a federally certified third-party identity service.

**Tax Account Transcript:** An official IRS record that shows the full history of your account for a given tax year: when your return was filed, what credits and payments were posted, whether a refund was issued or held, and any notices mailed. It uses three-digit transaction codes to record each action.

**Transaction code (TC):** A three-digit number on your Tax Account Transcript that identifies a specific IRS action. The codes most relevant to missing refunds are: TC 150 (return filed and processed), TC 806 (withholding credit posted), TC 846 (refund issued), TC 826 (refund offset to pay a prior debt), TC 570 (refund hold placed), TC 571 (refund hold released), TC 971 (notice mailed or miscellaneous account action), and TC 841 (direct deposit was rejected and the refund was reversed).

**Form 3911 (Taxpayer Statement Regarding Refund):** The IRS paper form used to initiate a refund trace when a refund check was lost, stolen, or never received, or when a direct deposit was not received more than 5 calendar days after the deposit date. Current revision: October 2022. Available free at irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f3911.pdf.

**Refund trace:** The IRS process of contacting the U.S. Bureau of the Fiscal Service (for checks) or the receiving bank (for direct deposits) to determine what happened to an issued refund. Initiated through the Where's My Refund tool, the IRS toll-free line, or Form 3911.

**Bureau of the Fiscal Service (BFS):** A bureau of the U.S. Department of the Treasury that processes all federal payments, including IRS refunds, and manages the Treasury Offset Program (TOP). Contact BFS at 800-304-3107 for offset inquiries. Website: fiscal.treasury.gov.

**Treasury Offset Program (TOP):** The federal program under which the Bureau of the Fiscal Service automatically reduces (offsets) a federal tax refund to pay certain past-due debts. Eligible debts include past-due federal taxes, state income taxes, child support, and certain federal student loans.

**Refund offset:** The reduction or elimination of your refund because the Bureau of the Fiscal Service applied it to a debt covered by the Treasury Offset Program. The BFS mails a separate notice identifying the amount offset and the agency that received the funds.

**Injured spouse:** A term used when one spouse on a joint return is not responsible for the other spouse's pre-marital or separately owed debt that triggered an offset. The "injured" spouse can claim their share of the joint refund back using Form 8379.

**Form 8379 (Injured Spouse Allocation):** The IRS form filed by the spouse who is not responsible for the debt that caused the offset, in order to recover their proportionate share of the joint refund.

**CP05 notice:** An IRS letter informing you that the IRS is holding your refund while it reviews your income, withholding, tax credits, or business income. No documents are required yet; the IRS is conducting an internal review.

**CP05A notice:** A follow-up to the CP05 notice, asking you to provide supporting documents (pay stubs, W-2s, 1099s) to verify the items the IRS is reviewing. There is a response deadline on the face of the notice.

**CP53E notice:** An IRS letter issued when a direct deposit refund was sent to an account that rejected or returned the deposit, and the IRS needs your updated direct deposit information. Under the 2025 paper-check phase-out rules, the IRS generally will not automatically reissue a rejected electronic refund as a paper check; the CP53E gives you 30 days to provide new account information through your IRS Online Account.

**PATH Act hold:** A statutory requirement under the Protecting Americans from Tax Hikes (PATH) Act (Section 201) that prevents the IRS from issuing refunds containing the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) or Additional Child Tax Credit (ACTC) before mid-February each year, regardless of when you filed.

**EITC (Earned Income Tax Credit):** A refundable federal tax credit for low-to-moderate-income workers. Refundable means the credit can generate a refund even if you owe no tax.

**ACTC (Additional Child Tax Credit):** The refundable portion of the Child Tax Credit. Subject to the same PATH Act mid-February hold as the EITC.

**Form 1040-X (Amended U.S. Individual Income Tax Return):** The form used to correct a previously filed Form 1040. If an error on your original return caused you to miss a refund you were entitled to, you may be able to recover it by filing a Form 1040-X within the statute of limitations.

**RSED (Refund Statute Expiration Date):** The legal deadline by which you must file a claim to receive a refund or credit. Under IRC Section 6511, the RSED is generally the later of: (1) three years from the date you filed the return, or (2) two years from the date you paid the tax. After the RSED passes, the money is permanently forfeited to the U.S. Treasury.

**IRC Section 6511:** The Internal Revenue Code provision governing the time limit for claiming a tax credit or refund. The general rule is the later of 3 years from filing or 2 years from payment.

**Taxpayer Advocate Service (TAS):** An independent organization within the IRS that assists taxpayers experiencing economic harm or who have been unable to resolve issues through normal IRS channels after 30 or more days. Free, confidential, and available in every state. Toll-free: 877-777-4778. Form 911 (Request for Taxpayer Advocate Service Assistance) can be filed to request help.

**Form 911 (Request for Taxpayer Advocate Service Assistance):** The form used to request free assistance from the Taxpayer Advocate Service when you have been unable to resolve your issue with the IRS and the delay is causing financial hardship.

**Executive Order 14247:** The March 2025 executive order titled "Modernizing Payments To and From America's Bank Account" that directed the IRS to stop issuing paper refund checks to individual taxpayers, effective September 30, 2025. Replaced by electronic delivery as the default.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before starting any step in this playbook:

- Your Social Security number or ITIN
- Your filing status for the return in question (single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, head of household, qualifying surviving spouse)
- The exact dollar amount of the refund shown on your filed return (required for Where's My Refund; this is the amount on line 35a of Form 1040)
- The tax year of the return in question (for example, tax year 2025, filed in 2026)
- The date you filed the return (or mailed it if filed on paper), and the IRS acceptance confirmation number if you e-filed
- Any IRS notice or letter you received related to the refund (CP05, CP05A, CP53E, offset notice, or any other)
- Your bank account routing number and account number (only if the IRS asks you to provide or update direct deposit information through CP53E or your Online Account)
- For a joint return where a refund was offset by your spouse's debt: each spouse's Social Security number and income information (needed for Form 8379)
- For a prior-year unclaimed refund: a copy of the unfiled or incorrectly filed return for that year, plus your W-2s, 1099s, and other income documents for that year

## Required Documents And Records

**Documents to collect before starting the refund trace or review process:**

- A copy of the filed tax return for the year in question (Form 1040, all pages and schedules)
- All Forms W-2, 1099-INT, 1099-DIV, 1099-NEC, 1099-MISC, 1099-B, 1098, and other information returns for that tax year (the IRS uses these to verify your withholding and income)
- The IRS e-file acceptance email or acknowledgment, if you e-filed (confirms the date the IRS accepted your return)
- The certified mail receipt or postmark proof, if you filed by paper (confirms the date the IRS received your return)
- The BFS offset notice, if you received one (identifies the agency that received the offset and the contact information for disputes)
- Any IRS notices related to the refund (CP05, CP05A, CP53E), with the response deadline noted

**Records to create and keep after each action:**

- Screenshots or printouts of each Where's My Refund status check, with the date of each check noted
- A downloaded copy of your Tax Account Transcript for the relevant year, saved with a date-stamped filename (for example: "2025-TaxAccountTranscript-pulled-20260624.pdf")
- A copy of any Form 3911 you file, signed and dated
- The fax confirmation page or certified mail tracking number for any Form 3911 you submit (this is your proof of the date you initiated the trace)
- A copy of any Form 8379 you file, signed and dated
- Written confirmation from your bank if you contacted them about a rejected or misdirected deposit
- Retain all records related to a specific tax year for at least seven years from the original filing date for that year

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Part A: Check Your Refund Status

**Step 1: Determine how long ago you filed and whether you are past the normal window.**

Before taking any action, confirm that the normal processing window has actually passed:

- E-filed return with direct deposit: allow up to 21 days from the date the IRS accepted your return.
- E-filed return with no direct deposit information on file (as of September 30, 2025, the IRS will hold the refund and mail a CP53E notice; see Step 6): allow at least 6 weeks.
- Paper-filed return: allow at least 6 weeks from the date the IRS received your mailed return.
- Amended return (Form 1040-X): allow 8 to 16 weeks. Track separately at irs.gov/filing/wheres-my-amended-return or by calling 866-464-2050 (available 3 weeks after filing). Note that this playbook focuses on original return refunds; amended return refunds follow a different process.

If you are still within the normal window, wait and proceed to Step 2 to confirm status.

**Step 2: Check your refund status using Where's My Refund.**

Go to irs.gov/refunds or open the IRS2Go mobile app. Enter your SSN or ITIN, your filing status, and the exact refund amount from your return (line 35a of Form 1040). You do not need to sign in; the tool works without an account.

When you can check:
- E-filed return: status is available 24 hours after the IRS accepts your return.
- Prior-year e-filed return: allow 3 days after submission.
- Paper-filed return: allow 4 weeks after mailing before checking.

The tool shows one of three statuses:
1. Return Received: the IRS has your return and is processing it.
2. Refund Approved: the IRS has approved the refund and is preparing to send it.
3. Refund Sent: the refund was issued. The tool will show the date and the method (direct deposit or check).

**Step 3: If Where's My Refund shows "Refund Sent" but you have not received it, note the exact date shown and the delivery method, then proceed to Part B.**

If the status shows direct deposit was sent more than 5 calendar days ago and the funds are not in your account, or if the status shows a check was issued more than 4 weeks ago and you have not received it, you are past the waiting period and can initiate a trace.

**Step 4: If Where's My Refund says "We cannot provide any information about your refund" and you filed more than 4 weeks ago (paper) or more than 3 weeks ago (e-file), pull your Tax Account Transcript.**

This message can mean the return is still processing, that there is a hold on the account, or that the return has not been fully entered into the IRS system yet. The Tax Account Transcript (Step 5) will tell you exactly what is happening.

### Part B: Pull Your Tax Account Transcript to Diagnose the Issue

**Step 5: Log in to your IRS Online Account and pull the Tax Account Transcript.**

Go to irs.gov and sign in to your Online Account (requires ID.me identity verification if you have not already registered; see the IRS Account, Transcript, Notice, and Refund Workflow playbook for account setup steps). Under "Tax Records," select "Tax Account Transcript" and choose the tax year in question.

Read the transcript for the following transaction codes:

| Code | What it means |
|---|---|
| TC 150 | Your return was processed. The date is when the IRS accepted it. |
| TC 806 | Your withholding credit was posted. The amount should match your W-2 box 2 withholding total. |
| TC 846 | A refund was issued. The date is the issue date. Note whether there is an amount next to TC 846; a zero means the refund may have been fully offset. |
| TC 841 | The direct deposit was rejected and the electronic refund was reversed. The IRS received the money back. |
| TC 826 | Your refund (or part of it) was applied to offset a prior debt. The amount and creditor period are listed. |
| TC 570 | A hold was placed on your refund, pending review. It may resolve on its own; monitor for TC 571. |
| TC 571 | The hold placed by TC 570 was released. If followed by TC 846, a refund was then issued. |
| TC 971 | A notice was mailed or a miscellaneous account action occurred. When paired with TC 570, a notice was sent (often a CP05 or CP05A). |

**If TC 846 is present:** A refund was issued. The issue date tells you when. If you did not receive it, proceed to Part C (trace) or Part D (offset).

**If TC 841 is present:** The direct deposit was rejected. The IRS received the refund back. Proceed to Step 6 for CP53E instructions.

**If TC 826 is present:** Your refund was offset. Proceed to Part D.

**If TC 570 is present with no TC 571:** A hold is in place. Monitor weekly. If a notice code (TC 971) is also present, check your mail and your Online Account for a CP05 or CP05A. Proceed to Part E.

**If TC 150 is not present yet:** Your return has not been fully processed. Continue to monitor.

### Part C: Initiate a Refund Trace for a Missing Direct Deposit or Check

Use this section when TC 846 is present on the transcript (refund was issued) but you did not receive it, or when TC 841 is present (deposit was rejected and the IRS received the funds back but has not yet reissued them).

**Step 6: If you received a CP53E notice (rejected direct deposit), respond within 30 days.**

The CP53E notice is sent when the IRS issued a direct deposit refund and the bank rejected it. Under Executive Order 14247 (effective September 30, 2025), the IRS generally will not automatically reissue a rejected electronic refund as a paper check. The notice asks you to:

1. Log in to your IRS Online Account at irs.gov.
2. Navigate to the "Payments" or "Refund" section and provide updated bank account routing and account numbers.
3. If you do not have a bank account, the IRS is developing alternatives including prepaid debit cards and digital wallets. Contact the IRS at 800-829-1040 to discuss options. The FDIC's BankOn program (fdic.gov/banktrack) can help you open a free or low-cost account.

If you do not respond to a CP53E within 30 days and there are no other issues with your return, the IRS will issue a paper check as a last resort.

**Step 7: If you provided incorrect bank account or routing number information on your return, act quickly.**

If the return has not yet posted to the IRS system, call 800-829-1040 (Monday through Friday, 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. local time) to ask the IRS to stop the direct deposit before it is sent.

If the deposit has already been sent and rejected by the bank, wait for the CP53E notice and follow Step 6.

If the deposit was sent to someone else's valid account because you entered another person's account number: contact that financial institution directly to request return of the funds. The IRS cannot compel the bank to return money deposited to a valid account. If the bank refuses, the matter becomes a civil dispute between you and the institution. The IRS does not assume responsibility for taxpayer or preparer data entry errors.

**Step 8: If a direct deposit was not received and it has been more than 5 calendar days past the deposit date, initiate a trace.**

You have three options to initiate a trace:

Option A: Use Where's My Refund (irs.gov/refunds). When the status shows "Refund Sent," there is a link to start a refund trace if the deposit was not received.

Option B: Call the IRS automated refund line at 800-829-1954. Married-filing-jointly filers cannot use the automated line and must call 800-829-1040 to speak with a representative.

Option C: File Form 3911 (Taxpayer Statement Regarding Refund). Download the form free at irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f3911.pdf. Complete Part I (refund information) and Part II (your information). Sign and date. Mail or fax to the IRS Refund Inquiry Unit that serves your state (see Step 9).

**Step 9: Send Form 3911 to the correct IRS Refund Inquiry Unit.**

The mailing address and fax number depend on your state. The IRS Form 3911 page at irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-3911 contains the current state-by-state list. As of the December 2025 review of that page, the four main regional units are:

| Region | Mailing address | Fax |
|---|---|---|
| Northeast states | 310 Lowell St., Stop 666, Andover, MA 01810 | 855-253-3175 |
| Southeast states | 5333 Getwell Rd., Stop 8422, Memphis, TN 38118 | 855-580-4749 |
| Southwest states | 3651 S. IH 35, Stop 6542, Austin, TX 78741-7855 | 855-203-7538 |
| West Coast states | 3211 S. Northpointe Dr., Stop B2007, Fresno, CA 93725 | 855-332-3068 |

Always verify the specific assignment for your state against the current instructions at irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-3911 before submitting, as assignments change. If the IRS sent you a notice about your refund, send Form 3911 to the address on that notice.

**Step 10: Understand the trace timeline and outcomes.**

After the IRS receives your Form 3911 (or your phone call trace request), the resolution depends on the situation:

For a check that was never cashed: the Treasury cancels the original check and issues a replacement. Allow 6 to 8 weeks for the IRS to complete the trace and then another 4 weeks for a replacement check. Under the paper-check phase-out rules, the replacement may be issued electronically if the IRS has your bank account information.

For a check that was cashed by someone else: the Bureau of the Fiscal Service will send you a claim package. You complete the package and return it. The BFS reviews the signature on the cashed check against your signature. The review takes up to 6 weeks, and a replacement refund is issued only if BFS determines the check was forged or stolen.

For a direct deposit trace: the IRS contacts the bank. Banks have up to 90 days from the date the trace is initiated to respond, and full resolution may take up to 120 days.

Keep the fax confirmation page or certified mail tracking number from your Form 3911 submission. This is your proof of the date you initiated the trace.

### Part D: Understand and Respond to a Refund Offset

Use this section when TC 826 appears on your Tax Account Transcript, or when you received a smaller refund than expected and received a BFS offset notice.

**Step 11: Identify the debt that caused the offset.**

The Bureau of the Fiscal Service handles all Treasury Offset Program reductions. The BFS will mail you a separate notice showing:
- The original refund amount
- The amount offset
- The agency or creditor that received the offset funds
- Contact information for that agency

Debts that can trigger an offset include: past-due federal income taxes, state income taxes, court-ordered child support, certain federal student loans, and other federal agency debts.

If you did not receive the BFS notice, call the BFS offset line at 800-304-3107 (TTY/TDD: 800-877-8339; hours: Monday through Friday, 7:30 a.m. to 5 p.m. Central time). The automated system will tell you whether a debt caused a reduction and, if so, which agency holds the debt.

**Step 12: If the offset came from your spouse's debt and you filed jointly, evaluate Form 8379 (Injured Spouse Allocation).**

If you filed a joint return and your portion of the refund was applied to your spouse's separately owed debt (for example, your spouse's pre-marital student loan or child support obligation from a prior relationship), you may be able to recover your share of the joint refund.

File Form 8379. You can:
- File it with your original joint return (write "INJURED SPOUSE" in the top left corner of the return if mailing).
- File it electronically as an attachment to Form 1040-X (even if you are not otherwise amending the return, per the 2026 Form 8379 instructions).
- File it by itself after the offset has already occurred.

Processing times for Form 8379 (once submitted):
- Filed with a joint return electronically: approximately 11 weeks.
- Filed with a joint return on paper: approximately 14 weeks.
- Filed separately after the original return was processed: approximately 8 weeks.

File Form 8379 within 3 years from the original return filing date or 2 years from the date the tax was paid, whichever is later. This deadline is the same as the general RSED (IRC Section 6511).

**Step 13: If you believe the offset was applied in error, dispute it with the holding agency directly.**

The IRS cannot reverse an offset that the BFS applied on behalf of another agency. Contact the agency identified in the BFS offset notice using the contact information on that notice. Each agency has its own dispute and appeal process.

### Part E: Respond to an Identity Verification or Review Hold

Use this section when TC 570 (with or without TC 971) appears on your transcript, or when you received a CP05 or CP05A notice.

**Step 14: If you received a CP05 notice, wait for the review to conclude.**

The CP05 notice means the IRS is reviewing your income, withholding, tax credits, or business income before releasing your refund. You are not required to send documents at this stage. No action is needed until the IRS sends a follow-up notice. The IRS does not provide a fixed timeline for resolving a CP05 review; monitor your Online Account weekly for a new transaction code or notice.

**Step 15: If you received a CP05A notice, respond by the deadline printed on the notice.**

The CP05A requests supporting documents. Read the notice carefully; it lists the specific items the IRS needs. Typical requested documents include:

- Form W-2 from your employer (to verify wages and withholding)
- Pay stubs for the year
- Schedule C or business records (if business income was on the return)
- Supporting documents for credits claimed (EITC, ACTC, education credits)

Submit the requested documents by the date on the notice using the IRS Document Upload Tool (available at the URL on your notice) or by mailing to the address shown on the notice. Responding through the Document Upload Tool is faster than mailing. Keep a copy of everything you submit.

**Step 16: If the hold continues beyond 60 days after you submitted your CP05A response, contact TAS.**

If you have responded to the CP05A and more than 60 days have passed without a refund release or a new notice, or if the delay is causing financial hardship (you cannot pay rent, utilities, food, or medical expenses), contact the Taxpayer Advocate Service:
- Toll-free: 877-777-4778 (Monday through Friday, 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. local time)
- File Form 911 (Request for Taxpayer Advocate Service Assistance) with your local TAS office
- TAS assistance is free and confidential

TAS can intervene when the IRS has not acted within the normal timeframe or when a delay is causing genuine financial hardship.

### Part F: Claim a Prior-Year Refund Before the Deadline Expires

**Step 17: Determine whether you have an unclaimed refund from a prior year.**

If you did not file a return for a prior year and you had taxes withheld from your paycheck (W-2 box 2) or made estimated payments, you may be owed a refund for that year -- but you must file to claim it. If you filed but made an error that resulted in paying more than you owed, you may be able to recover the overpayment by filing Form 1040-X.

**Step 18: Calculate your RSED (the deadline to file and claim the refund).**

Under IRC Section 6511, you must file your claim by the later of:
- Three years from the date you filed the original return for that year, OR
- Two years from the date you paid the tax for that year.

If you never filed a return for that year, the three-year window runs from the original due date of the return (typically April 15 of the following year).

For tax year 2022: the original return due date was April 18, 2023. Three years later is April 15, 2026. The IRS confirmed that the deadline to claim a 2022 refund was April 15, 2026. If that date has passed when you read this, the 2022 window is closed. If you have not yet reviewed tax years 2023 or 2024, calculate those deadlines now.

After the RSED passes, the money becomes the property of the U.S. Treasury and cannot be recovered.

**Step 19: File the return or Form 1040-X to claim the refund.**

To claim a refund for a year you never filed: file the original Form 1040 for that year. Use the version of the form that applies to that tax year (for example, the 2022 Form 1040 for a 2022 return). You can get prior-year forms at irs.gov/forms-instructions or by calling 800-829-3676 (800-TAX-FORM) for free.

To correct an error on a return you already filed: file Form 1040-X. The refund from the 1040-X will follow after the IRS processes the amendment (8 to 16 weeks). You cannot receive a refund larger than the total amount you paid for that year (including withholding, estimated payments, and refundable credits) during the applicable lookback period.

Note: if you owe other federal taxes, child support, state income taxes, or federal student loan debt, the IRS will apply your prior-year refund to those debts before paying you the remainder.

### Part G: Using Your IRS Online Account for Ongoing Account Review

**Step 20: Log in to your IRS Online Account monthly during any open refund issue.**

Your Online Account at irs.gov provides real-time information that Where's My Refund does not. From the dashboard you can:
- Pull the Tax Account Transcript at any time (no wait period once the return has been processed)
- See new IRS notices sent to you digitally (if you have enrolled in paperless notices)
- View your current balance by tax year
- Confirm that payments you made were posted correctly
- Retrieve your Identity Protection PIN (IP PIN) -- a six-digit number that prevents someone else from filing a return using your SSN. Once enrolled, include the IP PIN on every return you file.

**Step 21: Enroll in an IP PIN to protect your refund from identity theft.**

Tax-related identity theft occurs when someone files a return using your SSN before you do, claiming a refund in your name. When that happens, the IRS will reject your legitimate return and your actual refund may be delayed for months while the IRS investigates. An IP PIN prevents this.

To enroll: log in to your Online Account at irs.gov. Go to the IP PIN section of your profile page. Request your six-digit IP PIN. The PIN is valid for one calendar year and is renewed each January. If you lose it, retrieve it through your Online Account. Once enrolled, every federal return you file must include your current-year IP PIN.

| Contact TAS if refund unresolved after normal IRS channels | After 30 or more days of no progress, or immediately if financial hardship exists |

## The Numbers

All figures are verified for 2026 against canonical IRS and Treasury sources cited in the Sources section.

**Refund processing times:**
- E-filed return with direct deposit: typically within 21 days. The IRS processed more than 80% of 2026 e-filed refunds in under 21 days. Average 2026 refund amount: $3,571.
- Paper-filed return: 6 or more weeks from IRS receipt.
- Amended return (Form 1040-X): 8 to 16 weeks; in some cases up to 16 weeks.

**Where's My Refund availability windows:**
- E-filed current-year return: 24 hours after IRS acceptance.
- E-filed prior-year return: 3 days after submission.
- Paper return: 4 weeks after mailing.

**Timing before calling the IRS:**
- E-filed return: 3 weeks after IRS acceptance before calling.
- Paper return: 6 weeks after mailing before calling.

**IRS toll-free numbers for refund issues:**
- Where's My Refund automated line: 800-829-1954
- IRS representative line (required for married-filing-jointly filers, and for complex issues): 800-829-1040 (Monday through Friday, 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. local time)
- BFS offset inquiry line: 800-304-3107 (Monday through Friday, 7:30 a.m. to 5 p.m. Central time)
- Taxpayer Advocate Service: 877-777-4778
- Amended return status: 866-464-2050

**Form 3911 trace timelines:**
- IRS processing time: approximately 6 to 8 weeks after receiving Form 3911.
- Replacement check if original was never cashed: approximately 4 weeks after the original is canceled.
- Direct deposit trace -- bank response time: up to 90 days from trace initiation; full resolution may take up to 120 days.
- Cashed-check-by-wrong-person review: up to 6 weeks from when BFS receives your completed claim package.

**Form 3911 current revision:** October 2022. Available free at irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f3911.pdf.

**Paper check phase-out:** Effective September 30, 2025, the IRS generally stopped issuing paper refund checks to individual taxpayers (Executive Order 14247). Paper checks may still be issued as a last resort in limited circumstances.

**CP53E response window:** 30 days from the notice date to provide updated direct deposit information through your IRS Online Account.

**PATH Act hold:** Refunds containing EITC or ACTC cannot be released before mid-February by law. For the 2026 filing season, the IRS expected most EITC/ACTC refunds to reach direct deposit accounts by March 2, 2026, for filers who chose direct deposit and had no other issues.

**Refund statute of limitations (RSED) -- IRC Section 6511:**
- General rule: the later of 3 years from filing the original return, or 2 years from paying the tax.
- Special rule for bad debt or worthless security losses: 7 years from the return's original due date.
- Tax year 2022 RSED: April 15, 2026.
- Tax year 2023 RSED: April 15, 2027 (adjusted if you filed or paid after the original due date).
- After the RSED passes, the unclaimed refund becomes U.S. Treasury property and cannot be recovered.

**Form 8379 (Injured Spouse) processing times:**
- Filed electronically with joint return: approximately 11 weeks.
- Filed on paper with joint return: approximately 14 weeks.
- Filed separately after the joint return was processed: approximately 8 weeks.

**Unclaimed 2022 refunds:** The IRS estimated in March 2026 that over 1.3 million taxpayers had unclaimed refunds for tax year 2022, totaling approximately $1.2 billion, with a median refund amount of $686.

**Transcript availability after e-filing (current year):**
- Refund or no balance due: allow 2 to 3 weeks after return submission before requesting a transcript.
- Balance due, paid in full with return: 2 to 3 weeks after submission.
- Paper return: allow 6 to 8 weeks after mailing.

**Form 3911 fee:** None. The form is free to file.

**Taxpayer Advocate Service:** Free and confidential. Assists taxpayers who have experienced a delay of more than 30 days to resolve a tax issue, who have not received a response by the date the IRS promised, or who are experiencing economic harm. File Form 911 to request assistance.

**IP PIN:** Six digits, valid for one calendar year. Renewed each January. Required on every federal return once you opt in.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Married filing jointly with a refund offset:** When you file a joint return, the IRS treats the combined overpayment as belonging to both spouses equally unless you demonstrate otherwise. If your spouse's separately owed debt triggered an offset of your joint refund, you must file Form 8379 to claim your share. The IRS will not automatically separate the refund.

**S-corporation shareholders:** Your wages (W-2) and your pass-through profit distributions (Schedule K-1) are both included on your individual Form 1040. The Wage and Income Transcript will show your W-2 withholding but will not separately display K-1 income. If the IRS issues a CP05A questioning your withholding, your advisor can help you document that K-1 income is separate from W-2 wages.

**Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs:** Business income and refunds flow through your personal SSN. All transcripts are pulled from your individual account. If you also have an EIN for employment tax purposes, payroll-related transcript issues are separate from your individual refund.

**State refunds:** This playbook covers federal refunds only. Every state with an income tax has its own refund tracking tool, trace process, offset program, and reclaim deadline. State refund timing and state offset rules operate independently of federal rules. A federal refund issue does not automatically signal or resolve a state refund issue.

**Current-year IRS staffing (2026):** As of June 2026, the IRS has a significantly reduced workforce compared to prior years. Paper return and amended return processing times may exceed the published standard ranges. E-filing and providing direct deposit information on your return remain the most effective ways to receive your refund without delay.

**EITC and ACTC filers:** The PATH Act hold is mandatory; the IRS cannot release your refund before mid-February regardless of when you file. Filing earlier in January does not accelerate the release date. If your refund is still not received by late March, check your Tax Account Transcript for TC codes before calling.

**Military members and combat zone service:** Service in a designated combat zone extends the RSED and other tax deadlines. If you or your spouse served in a combat zone during the relevant tax year, consult your advisor to confirm whether an extended RSED applies to your situation.

**Financial hardship and refund offsets:** If the Treasury Offset Program would take your refund and the loss of that money would prevent you from paying for housing, utilities, food, or medical care, you may qualify for an Offset Bypass Refund (OBR). The IRS can, in limited circumstances, release the portion of your refund needed to address the hardship before applying the rest to the federal debt. You must contact the IRS before the offset occurs; OBR cannot be applied retroactively.

**Bankruptcy:** Filing for bankruptcy may suspend collection actions and affect offset timing. If you are in or considering bankruptcy, route any refund or offset issues to your bankruptcy attorney and your advisor immediately.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Starting a trace too early.**
The IRS will not process a refund trace until the waiting period has passed: 5 days for a direct deposit and 4 weeks for a check. Filing Form 3911 before that window will result in the IRS returning your form. Confirm the issue date from your Tax Account Transcript or Where's My Refund before initiating a trace.

**2. Calling the IRS automated refund line when you filed jointly.**
Married-filing-jointly filers cannot use the automated line at 800-829-1954 for refund traces. You must call 800-829-1040 to speak with a representative, or file Form 3911.

**3. Entering the wrong refund amount in Where's My Refund.**
The tool requires the exact dollar amount from your return (Form 1040, line 35a). If you enter an estimated or rounded amount, the tool will not find your record. Use the actual return.

**4. Discarding the BFS offset notice as junk mail.**
The Bureau of the Fiscal Service sends a separate notice when a refund is offset to pay a debt. It does not come from the IRS directly. Taxpayers sometimes discard it. Read all official government mail during refund season, especially anything from Treasury.

**5. Filing Form 8379 after the RSED has expired.**
The injured spouse claim must be filed within the same statute of limitations window (3 years from filing or 2 years from payment) as any other refund claim. Filing Form 8379 years after an offset is not possible once the RSED passes.

**6. Assuming a held refund means an audit.**
A TC 570 hold or a CP05 notice is not the same as an audit. It is a review by the IRS's automated systems, often triggered by a mismatch between your withholding claim and the data the IRS has from your employer. Most CP05 holds are resolved without any document submission. Wait for the CP05A before sending anything.

**7. Not saving the fax confirmation page or certified mail receipt from Form 3911.**
If you submit Form 3911 and the IRS later claims it has no record of receiving it, the fax confirmation (showing date, time, receiving number, and pages sent) or the certified mail tracking number is your proof. Without it, you may have to restart the trace.

**8. Missing the RSED for a prior-year refund.**
The IRS does not notify you before your RSED expires. It is your responsibility to identify unclaimed prior-year refunds and file the return or amended return before the deadline. After the RSED passes, there is no administrative appeal or waiver; the money is forfeited.

**9. Not checking for an IP PIN hold.**
If someone used your SSN to file a fraudulent return in a prior year, the IRS may have flagged your account for identity verification. Your legitimate return will be held until identity is confirmed. Enrolling in the IP PIN program (available through your Online Account) prevents this from happening in future years.

**10. Expecting the IRS to automatically reissue a rejected direct deposit as a paper check.**
Under Executive Order 14247 (effective September 30, 2025), the IRS generally does not automatically convert rejected electronic refunds to paper checks. You must respond to the CP53E notice within 30 days with updated banking information or explain why electronic payment is not possible.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and contact your advisor before acting if any of the following apply:

- **Your refund has been missing for more than 16 weeks after e-filing or more than 20 weeks after paper filing and you have not been able to resolve it through normal IRS channels.** Your advisor or TAS can evaluate whether a formal hardship claim is appropriate.
- **You received a CP05A notice and you are unsure whether your documents fully support what you claimed on the return.** Submitting incomplete or inconsistent documentation to the IRS can complicate the review. Your advisor can help you assemble and present the correct records.
- **An offset was applied to a debt you do not recognize or believe you do not owe.** Disputing an offset with a federal agency is a separate process with its own deadlines; your advisor can help you identify the correct contact and dispute process.
- **You believe you are owed a refund for a prior year and the RSED is within 90 days.** Acting quickly is critical, and your advisor needs to confirm the exact filing or amendment required before the window closes.
- **A joint refund was offset and you believe the debt belongs only to your spouse.** Form 8379 involves an allocation of income and deductions between spouses; your advisor can calculate the injured spouse allocation correctly.
- **Your direct deposit went to the wrong account and the bank is refusing to return the funds.** This may become a legal matter; your advisor can help you understand your options.
- **You received any IRS notice not covered in this playbook.** Route unfamiliar notices to your advisor before the response deadline.
- **You are in bankruptcy or facing active IRS collection action.** Refund issues in a bankruptcy context require coordination between your advisor and your bankruptcy counsel.

## Using The Download(s)

**Download name:** Missing Refunds and IRS Account Review Implementation Worksheet

**What it contains:** A fillable multi-section tracker organized to match the parts of this playbook: a Refund Status Log, a Transcript Review Checklist, a Refund Trace Record, an Offset Investigation Log, and a Prior-Year RSED Calculator.

**Where to use it in the workflow:**

- **Before Step 1:** Open the worksheet. In the Refund Status Log, record your filing date, filing method (e-file or paper), the exact refund amount from your return, and the date you start tracking.
- **After each Where's My Refund check (Steps 2 and 3):** Record the date, the status message shown, and any projected deposit date shown by the tool. This log is your timeline if you later need to prove the delay.
- **After Step 5 (transcript pull):** Use the Transcript Review Checklist to note every transaction code found on the Tax Account Transcript, the date of each code, and which next step it points to (trace, offset inquiry, hold response, or prior-year claim).
- **When you submit Form 3911 (Steps 8 and 9):** Complete the Refund Trace Record with the date submitted, the method (fax or mail), the fax number or mailing address used, the confirmation number or certified mail tracking number, and the 6-to-8-week follow-up date.
- **When investigating an offset (Steps 11 to 13):** Use the Offset Investigation Log to record the BFS offset notice details, the agency that received the offset funds, the amount offset, and the date you contacted BFS at 800-304-3107.
- **For prior-year unclaimed refunds (Steps 17 to 19):** Use the Prior-Year RSED Calculator tab to enter the original filing date (or return due date if never filed) and confirm your RSED for each open year. Mark as expired or open.

**How to fill it out:** Record each action the day it happens. The dates and confirmation numbers in this worksheet are the evidence you need if the IRS disputes whether you filed on time or what steps you took.

## Before You Act

Before filing Form 3911, submitting a response to a CP05A notice, filing Form 8379, filing a prior-year return to claim an unclaimed refund, or providing updated banking information to the IRS in response to a CP53E, confirm your specific facts with your advisor. The RSED deadlines are permanent; money forfeited after the RSED cannot be recovered. The injured spouse allocation requires careful calculation of each spouse's income and credits. Submitting incorrect documentation to the IRS during a CP05A review can delay or complicate the outcome. This page teaches the process; your advisor confirms how it applies to your specific return and situation.
