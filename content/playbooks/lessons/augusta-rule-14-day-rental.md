# Augusta Rule / 14-Day Rental

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The Augusta Rule is the informal name for the tax benefit in IRC Section 280A(g). It lets the owner of a personal residence rent that home to a legally separate business for up to 14 days per year and exclude the rental income from federal gross income entirely. At the same time, the business that pays the rent deducts the payment as an ordinary business expense under IRC Section 162. The result is a transfer of money from the business (where it would otherwise be subject to business income tax and potentially payroll taxes) to the owner personally (where it is excluded from taxable income), creating a tax benefit on both sides of the transaction when implemented correctly.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy applies to you if ALL of the following are true:

- You own or co-own the home (or have legal title through a trust or other arrangement that your advisor has confirmed qualifies as a personal residence under IRC Section 280A).
- You use that home as a personal residence during the tax year. ("Personal residence" means you use it more than the greater of 14 days per year or 10% of the days it is rented at fair market rate, per IRC Section 280A(d).)
- You own or are a shareholder, partner, or member of a business that is treated as a SEPARATE taxpayer from you for federal income tax purposes: an S corporation, a C corporation, a partnership, or a multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership.
- The business has a legitimate need for meeting space and can hold actual business events at your home.
- Your total rental days to that business will not exceed 14 for the calendar year.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not use this strategy if any of the following apply:

- **You operate as a sole proprietor or a single-member LLC taxed as a disregarded entity.** There is no separate taxpayer to pay you rent. The IRS treats you and your business as the same person, so the payment has no tax effect and no deduction exists.
- **You have already rented your home for 15 or more days in the same tax year (to any party combined).** The Section 280A(g) exclusion applies only when total rental use is fewer than 15 days. Exceeding this threshold by even one day eliminates the exclusion for the entire year, making all rental income taxable.
- **The business events you plan to hold at your home are primarily social, entertainment-focused, or personal in nature.** A dinner party with incidental business talk does not qualify. The event must have a documented, substantive business purpose.
- **You cannot establish a fair market rent rate supported by local commercial venue comparables.** The IRS will scrutinize the rental rate in a related-party transaction. An inflated or unsupported rate risks disallowance of the business deduction and potential audit of the entire arrangement.
- **Your state does not conform to the federal exclusion.** Several states do not automatically follow the federal IRC Section 280A(g) exclusion, meaning state income tax may still apply even when federal income tax does not. Confirm state treatment with your advisor before acting.
- **You live in a state or municipality that imposes occupancy or lodging taxes.** Local taxes may apply even when federal income is excluded. Confirm with your advisor.

## Key Terms

**IRC Section 280A(g):** The subsection of the Internal Revenue Code that creates the 14-day rental exclusion. "IRC" means the Internal Revenue Code, the main body of federal tax law.

**IRC Section 162:** The code section that allows businesses to deduct ordinary and necessary business expenses. The business's rent payment to the homeowner is deductible under this provision, provided the expense is reasonable and the meeting serves a legitimate business purpose.

**Gross income exclusion:** The rental income does not get added to your taxable income at all. You do not report it, you do not deduct expenses against it, and you do not list it on Schedule E (the form used for rental property). The income simply does not appear on your personal tax return (Form 1040).

**Dwelling unit used as a residence:** Under IRC Section 280A(d), your home qualifies as a "dwelling unit used as a residence" if you personally use it more than the greater of 14 days per year or 10% of the days it is rented at fair market rates. Most primary homes meet this standard without any calculation.

**Separate taxpayer:** For the Augusta Rule to work, the business paying rent must be a legal entity that the IRS treats as distinct from you personally. S corporations, C corporations, partnerships, and multi-member LLCs meet this test. Sole proprietorships and single-member LLCs taxed as disregarded entities do not.

**Fair market rent (FMR):** The rate a willing tenant and willing landlord would agree to in an arm's-length transaction, with both having reasonable knowledge of the facts. For the Augusta Rule, FMR is benchmarked against what local hotels, executive suites, or commercial meeting venues charge for comparable space on comparable days, not what vacation rental platforms charge for overnight stays.

**Ordinary and necessary business expense:** Under IRC Section 162, a deductible business expense must be (1) ordinary, meaning common and accepted in your industry, and (2) necessary, meaning helpful and appropriate for your business. Both conditions must be met for the business deduction to stand.

**Schedule E (Form 1040):** The IRS form used to report income and losses from rental real estate, partnerships, S corporations, and certain other sources. When the Augusta Rule applies, you do NOT file or include this rental activity on Schedule E, because the income is excluded from gross income entirely.

**Form 1120-S:** The federal income tax return filed by an S corporation each year. The rent paid to the owner-homeowner is reported as a deductible business expense on this form.

**Form 1120:** The federal income tax return filed by a C corporation. The rent paid is reported as a deductible business expense here.

**Form 1065:** The federal income tax return (information return) filed by a partnership or multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership. The rent paid is reported as a deductible expense here.

**Contemporaneous documentation:** Records created at or near the time of the event, not reconstructed weeks or months later. The IRS places significantly more weight on contemporaneous records in an audit.

**Related-party transaction:** A financial transaction between parties who have a personal or business relationship, such as a business owner and the business they control. The IRS applies heightened scrutiny to related-party transactions to ensure they reflect economic reality and fair pricing.

**IRC Section 280A(g) threshold:** The statute says "fewer than 15 days." This means 14 days or fewer. Reaching 15 rental days in a calendar year eliminates the exclusion for the entire year.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather all of the following before beginning implementation:

1. **Proof of home ownership or legal title** (deed, trust document, or mortgage statement showing the property address and your name).
2. **Confirmation of personal residence use** (you live there; it is not a vacation home or investment property you rarely visit).
3. **Entity documents for the business** (articles of incorporation, operating agreement, or partnership agreement confirming the entity type and that it is a separate taxpayer from you).
4. **Your state of residence** so your advisor can confirm whether your state conforms to the federal IRC Section 280A(g) exclusion.
5. **Your planned rental dates** for the year (the specific dates of each business meeting or event, not to exceed 14 calendar days combined).
6. **Business purpose for each event** (a description of what business topic or decision will be addressed at each meeting).
7. **Local commercial venue rate research** (written quotes or screenshots from at least three comparable local meeting venues, hotel conference rooms, or executive suite rental spaces, gathered before you set your rental rate).
8. **A record of any other rental days** your home has already been or will be rented to any other party during the same calendar year, to confirm you will stay under the 14-day ceiling.

## Required Documents And Records

Create and retain ALL of the following for each Augusta Rule event:

### Before the first rental day of the year
- **Written rental agreement** between you (as homeowner) and the business (signed by both parties, dated before the first rental day it covers). Must state: rental dates and times, rental rate per day, business purpose, description of the space being used, and payment terms.
- **Fair market rent analysis memo** documenting the three comparable venue quotes you gathered, the median rate you calculated, and your rationale for setting the rental rate at or below that median. Save dated screenshots or PDF printouts of the quotes.

### For each individual meeting or event
- **Meeting agenda** prepared and dated before the meeting takes place.
- **Meeting minutes or notes** capturing the substantive business topics discussed and decisions made. Notes do not need to be formal, but they must show real business content.
- **Attendee list** with each person's name and their role (employee, owner, contractor, advisor, partner, etc.).
- **Photographs of the meeting setup** (optional but helpful in an audit).
- **Calendar entry or day-count log** confirming the date and total running count of rental days used in the year.

### After each meeting
- **Payment record** showing the business transferred funds from its bank account to your personal account. Acceptable forms: check with memo notation, ACH transfer with description. Cash payments are not recommended because they are difficult to trace.
- **Bank statement or wire confirmation** showing the payment clearing both accounts.

### Annual record
- **Year-end day-count summary** confirming total rental days used did not exceed 14.

### Retention period
Keep all Augusta Rule records for a minimum of **six years** from the date you file the tax return for the year in question. The standard IRS audit window is three years from filing, but the IRS has six years to assess additional tax if unreported income exceeds 25% of the gross income on your return (IRS Publication 583, IRS Topic 305). Because Augusta Rule rental income does not appear on your personal return at all, the six-year window is the safer standard. Keep business entity returns and supporting records for the same period.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Complete these steps in order. Do not skip ahead to payment before the documentation in earlier steps is in place.

**Step 1: Confirm you qualify.**
Verify that your business entity is a separate taxpayer (S corporation, C corporation, partnership, or multi-member LLC). If you operate as a sole proprietor or single-member disregarded LLC, stop. This strategy does not apply until you have a qualifying entity. Confirm with your advisor.

**Step 2: Confirm your state treatment.**
Ask your advisor whether your state conforms to IRC Section 280A(g). Some states, including California and others, may not automatically exclude this income from state taxable income. Get this answer before you collect any rent.

**Step 3: Identify up to 14 days of legitimate business events.**
Plan the specific dates on which the business will use your home space. Each date must have a real business purpose (board meeting, annual planning session, strategy review, employee training, client advisory session, shareholder meeting, or similar). Entertainment events and social gatherings do not qualify. Write down the purpose for each date before the event occurs.

**Step 4: Research fair market rent.**
Contact at least three local venues: hotel conference rooms, executive suites, coworking event spaces, or comparable commercial meeting venues. Request written quotes by email (so you have a dated record). Match the capacity and amenities of your home to the venues you compare. Calculate the median rate across the three quotes. Set your rental rate at or below that median. Do NOT use residential Airbnb or VRBO listings as comparables; the IRS expects commercial meeting-space benchmarks for business meeting arrangements.

**Step 5: Draft and sign the rental agreement.**
Write a simple rental agreement identifying: the homeowner (you), the tenant (the business entity), the specific dates covered, the rate per day, the total rent for the year, the business purpose, the space being rented, and the payment schedule. Both you (as homeowner) and an authorized officer of the business (also you, in most cases, wearing your business-owner hat) must sign it. Date it before the first rental day it covers.

**Step 6: Prepare a meeting agenda for each event.**
Write and date an agenda before each meeting. The agenda should list the specific business topics to be discussed and decisions to be made. File it with your Augusta Rule records.

**Step 7: Hold the meeting and document it.**
Conduct the actual business meeting. After it concludes, write brief notes or minutes capturing what was discussed, what was decided, and who attended. Add each person's name and role to the attendee list. Photograph the meeting space if you choose. Record the date in your day-count log.

**Step 8: Process payment through the business.**
The business must transfer the rental payment from its business bank account to your personal account. Use a check, ACH, or wire transfer with a memo or description referencing "meeting space rental per Section 280A(g) agreement" and the meeting date(s). Save the bank statement or transfer confirmation.

**Step 9: Record the expense on the business return.**
The rent payment is recorded as an ordinary business expense on the business entity's return: Form 1120-S (S corporation), Form 1120 (C corporation), or Form 1065 (partnership). Your tax preparer will categorize it under rent or meeting expenses. The business gets the deduction under IRC Section 162.

**Step 10: Exclude the income from your personal return.**
Do NOT report the rental income on your Form 1040. Do NOT include it on Schedule E. Do NOT deduct any expenses (utilities, supplies, cleaning) against it, because the statute bars rental expense deductions when the income exclusion applies. The income simply does not appear on your personal return.

**Step 11: Track cumulative days throughout the year.**
Maintain a running count of Augusta Rule rental days. Stop at 14. If a planned event would push you to 15 or more days, do not rent the space for that event. Exceeding 14 days eliminates the exclusion for ALL rental income that year, making the entire year's rent taxable on your personal return.

**Step 12: Repeat annually.**
Conduct fresh fair market rent research each year. Market rates change. Do not reuse the prior year's comparables without verifying they are still current.

## Timing And Deadlines

**No mid-year IRS election is required.** There is no form to file and no deadline to notify the IRS that you intend to use IRC Section 280A(g). The exclusion is claimed (or more precisely, not claimed as income) when you file your personal return.

**Key timing rules:**
- The rental agreement must be signed before the first rental day it covers.
- Meeting agendas must be prepared before each meeting, not reconstructed afterward.
- Meeting notes must be written on or shortly after the meeting date.
- Payment must actually transfer from the business account to your personal account during the tax year (by December 31 of the year the meetings occur) for the business to deduct the expense in that tax year.
- The 14-day ceiling is a calendar-year count (January 1 through December 31).

**Annual rhythm:**
- January to March: Plan your Augusta Rule meeting calendar for the year. Research FMR comparables. Draft the rental agreement.
- Throughout the year: Hold meetings, document them, process payments within the same month the meeting occurs.
- December 31: Ensure all payments for the year have cleared. Confirm your total rental day count is 14 or fewer.
- January to April (filing season): Your tax preparer deducts rent on the business return and excludes the income from your personal return. No special form is filed.
- Ongoing: Retain all records for at least six years from the filing date of the return.

**Note on fair market rent:** The IRS does not publish a safe-harbor dollar amount for Augusta Rule rental rates. The correct rate depends entirely on your local market and property. Your obligation is to support your rate with documented comparable commercial venue quotes gathered before the first rental day of the year. The Tax Court's *Sinopoli v. Commissioner* decision illustrates the risk of inflated rates: the court reduced a claimed deduction of approximately $291,000 to a small fraction because the taxpayers lacked market support for their pricing (the IRS found comparable meeting space locally for approximately $500 per day in that market and fact pattern). Do not set your rate without documented comparables.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity requirement (critical).** The strategy requires a business entity that is a separate taxpayer from you. Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs taxed as disregarded entities cannot use this strategy. If you are considering converting to an S corporation or forming a new entity, discuss the timing with your advisor before implementing the Augusta Rule.

**S corporation payroll.** If you own an S corporation, you are already required to pay yourself reasonable compensation (a W-2 wage) before taking profit distributions. The Augusta Rule rental income is not subject to employment taxes (Social Security, Medicare, or FUTA) because it is rent, not wages. However, confirm with your advisor that your overall compensation structure is compliant with IRS reasonable compensation rules before adding the Augusta Rule layer.

**C corporation deduction timing.** A C corporation deducts the rent in the tax year the payment is made. Confirm with your advisor that the payment clears by the entity's fiscal year end if your C corporation does not use a calendar year.

**Partnership or multi-member LLC.** The business deducts the rent, and the deduction flows through to partners on their Schedule K-1 forms. Confirm with your advisor that the partnership agreement and K-1 reporting are handled correctly.

**State conformity (important).** IRC Section 280A(g) is a federal law. Most states with an income tax conform to the federal exclusion automatically, but some states do not. California, in particular, has a separate conformity date and selective conformity rules that require individual review. New Jersey and Pennsylvania have also been identified as states with partial or unclear conformity by practitioner sources. Do not assume your state follows the federal rule without confirming with your advisor. If your state does not conform, you may owe state income tax on the rental income even though no federal tax applies.

**Local occupancy taxes.** Some cities and counties impose hotel taxes, short-term rental taxes, or occupancy taxes on rental income. These local taxes may apply even when federal income is excluded. Confirm whether your municipality has any such requirements.

**TCJA and QBI.** Augusta Rule rental income is excluded from gross income and does not constitute business income for purposes of the qualified business income (QBI) deduction under IRC Section 199A. This is expected and correct; confirm your overall QBI calculation with your advisor for tax year 2026, as certain TCJA provisions are in the post-OBBBA landscape.

**No double-dipping on expenses.** When you exclude rental income under Section 280A(g), you cannot deduct any home expenses (utilities, depreciation, insurance, supplies) as rental expenses for those days. Your mortgage interest and property taxes remain deductible on Schedule A as personal itemized deductions to the extent otherwise allowable (Publication 527; IRS Topic 415). Do not allocate any portion of those costs to the rental activity.

**No cash payments.** Always transfer rent through traceable business bank channels (check, ACH, wire). Cash transactions are a significant audit red flag in related-party arrangements and are difficult to substantiate.

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Exceeding 14 rental days.**
This is the most costly error. Every rental day beyond 14 eliminates the exclusion for the entire year's rental income, not just the excess days. Track days in real time and stop before you reach 15.

**Mistake 2: Using your home to pay yourself without a real meeting.**
Staging a meeting that has no substance (no agenda, no real decisions, no genuine attendees) is not a business expense. The IRS looks for real business activity. "Paper meetings" with no documentation are a leading cause of Augusta Rule disallowance in audits.

**Mistake 3: Setting the rental rate without market research.**
Charging a rate you chose without documented comparable quotes is indefensible in an audit. Gather three commercial venue quotes before you set the rate, and keep the evidence.

**Mistake 4: Reconstructing documentation after the fact.**
Creating agendas, meeting notes, attendee lists, and rental agreements weeks or months after the events occurred is a serious credibility problem in an audit. All documentation must be created at the time of the event or before.

**Mistake 5: Using the wrong comparable for FMR.**
Residential Airbnb and VRBO rates for overnight vacation stays are not appropriate comparables for a business meeting space. Use hotel conference rooms, executive suites, coworking event spaces, and similar commercial venues.

**Mistake 6: Deducting home expenses against the excluded income.**
If the income is excluded, no expenses are allowed for those rental days. Attempting to deduct utilities, supplies, or depreciation for the Augusta Rule days is incorrect and will be disallowed.

**Mistake 7: Ignoring state tax.**
Assuming the federal exclusion automatically eliminates state income tax is incorrect in some states. Confirm state treatment before you act.

**Mistake 8: Inconsistent reporting across returns.**
The business deduction on the entity return and the exclusion on the personal return must match. If the amounts or dates are inconsistent, the IRS has grounds to question both positions. Confirm that your entity return and personal return tell the same story.

**Mistake 9: Paying rent from a personal account.**
The rent must come from the business's bank account to demonstrate it is a business expense. Payments from your personal account to yourself have no business deduction value.

**Mistake 10: Failing to count all rental days across payors.**
The 14-day ceiling applies to total days your home is rented to ANY party during the year, not just to your business. If you rented your home through Airbnb for 5 days and want to rent to your business for the Augusta Rule, only 9 days remain available. Count all rental days.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before proceeding if any of the following situations apply:

1. **Before first implementation:** Your advisor must confirm your entity type qualifies as a separate taxpayer and that your state conforms to the federal IRC Section 280A(g) exclusion.

2. **Before setting the rental rate:** Show your advisor the three comparable venue quotes you gathered and the rate you propose. Your advisor should confirm the rate is defensible before the rental agreement is signed.

3. **Before signing the rental agreement:** Your advisor should review the agreement to confirm it covers the required elements (dates, rate, business purpose, parties, payment terms).

4. **If you have already rented your home to any other party during the year:** Your advisor must confirm the total days (all parties combined) stay under 15 before you rent to the business.

5. **If your business is a C corporation or partnership with a non-calendar fiscal year:** Confirm payment timing so the deduction falls in the correct tax year.

6. **If you are in California, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, or any state where conformity is uncertain:** Your advisor must confirm the state tax treatment before you collect any rent.

7. **If the business deduction would create or increase a net operating loss (NOL) for the entity:** Discuss the impact with your advisor.

8. **If a local occupancy tax may apply:** Confirm with your advisor or a local tax authority whether short-term rental taxes apply in your municipality.

9. **If your total Augusta Rule income for the year will be significant enough to affect your overall tax picture materially:** Your advisor should model the full-year impact before you finalize the arrangement.

Do not rent your home to your business, sign a rental agreement, set a rental rate, or exclude any income from your personal tax return until you have discussed your specific facts with your advisor.
