# Cost Seg / STR / Real Estate Professional Intake Packet

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

This playbook covers three connected real estate tax strategies that are commonly implemented together: cost segregation studies (accelerating depreciation on a building to generate large deductions), the short-term rental (STR) loophole (using a vacation or short-stay rental property to turn passive losses into active deductions), and real estate professional status (REPS) (a federal designation that lets a qualifying taxpayer treat all rental losses as non-passive and offset any income). All three strategies depend on the same intake information, which is why Beyond Freedom Financial collects it in a single packet. Completing the intake packet correctly and completely is the prerequisite for your advisor to determine which strategies apply to you and what the numbers look like.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is for you if any of the following describe your situation:

- You own residential rental property, a short-term rental (such as an Airbnb or VRBO), or commercial real estate that has been placed in service (meaning it is available for rental or business use) and was not previously subject to a cost segregation study.
- You paid $300,000 or more for a building or made significant leasehold improvements (improvements made to property you lease from someone else) and want to accelerate the tax deductions from that investment.
- You own a short-term rental property where the average guest stay is 7 days or fewer, and you participate actively in operating the property.
- You or your spouse devotes the majority of your working time to real estate activities and want to convert rental losses from passive (losses that can only offset other passive income) to non-passive (losses that can offset wages, business income, and investment income).
- You have rental losses that are currently "suspended" (carried forward but not yet deductible) because your income is too high or you have not met the participation thresholds, and you want to evaluate whether REPS qualification would unlock those losses.
- You have owned a qualifying property for several years without having done a cost segregation study and want to catch up on the missed depreciation through a lookback study.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not pursue these strategies if any of the following apply, without first discussing the exception with your advisor:

- **You are a passive investor only.** If you do not operate or manage your rental properties and rely on a property manager for all decisions, you will not meet the material participation tests required for either the STR loophole or REPS. Passive investors are generally limited to the $25,000 rental real estate allowance (if eligible) or suspended losses carried forward.
- **Your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) exceeds $150,000 and you own only traditional long-term rentals.** The $25,000 passive loss allowance for rental real estate is completely phased out at $150,000 MAGI, and long-term rental losses will be suspended unless you qualify for REPS. A cost segregation study on a passive activity in this situation accelerates a loss you cannot currently use.
- **You or your spouse has full-time W-2 employment consuming more hours than your real estate activities.** REPS qualification requires that MORE THAN HALF of your total personal services for the year are in real property trades or businesses. If a spouse works a full-time job (say, 2,000 hours per year) and also manages rentals (say, 800 hours), the W-2 hours prevent meeting the "more than half" test for that spouse. Only the non-employed spouse can potentially qualify.
- **Your short-term rental property averages more than 7 days per stay.** The STR loophole only applies when the average period of customer use is 7 days or fewer. A property with longer average stays is treated as a traditional rental activity and requires REPS or the $25,000 allowance instead.
- **You plan to sell the property in the near term.** A cost segregation study accelerates deductions today but triggers depreciation recapture (taxed as ordinary income or at the 25% unrecaptured Section 1250 rate) when you sell. If a sale is imminent, the study may not save enough in the current year to justify the recapture cost. Run the numbers with your advisor.
- **You own land only.** Land is never depreciable under any method. Cost segregation applies only to structures and improvements, not to the land beneath them.

## Key Terms

**Cost segregation study:** An engineering-based analysis of a building that breaks the property into its individual components and reclassifies those components that qualify as personal property (shorter-lived assets like fixtures, flooring, specialty electrical, plumbing, and cabinetry) and land improvements (parking lots, driveways, landscaping, sidewalks) into shorter MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System) depreciation lives of 5, 7, or 15 years instead of the standard 27.5 years (residential rental) or 39 years (commercial). The result is larger depreciation deductions in the early years of ownership.

**MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System):** The IRS depreciation system, established by the Tax Reform Act of 1986, that applies to most business and rental property placed in service after 1986. MACRS assigns a "recovery period" (the number of years over which you deduct the cost) and a depreciation method to each class of property. The key classes in a cost segregation context are 5-year property (appliances, carpets, certain equipment), 7-year property (office furniture, fixtures), 15-year property (land improvements), 27.5-year property (residential rental buildings), and 39-year property (commercial buildings).

**Bonus depreciation:** A provision under IRC Section 168(k) that allows a taxpayer to deduct 100% of the cost of qualifying property in the year it is placed in service, rather than spreading it over the MACRS recovery period. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, enacted July 4, 2025) permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. When a cost segregation study reclassifies building components into 5-, 7-, or 15-year property (all of which have recovery periods of 20 years or less), those reclassified components qualify for 100% bonus depreciation and can be fully deducted in the year placed in service.

**Depreciation recapture:** When you sell a property on which you previously claimed depreciation, the IRS "recaptures" part of that benefit by taxing the gain attributable to depreciation at higher rates than the capital gains rate. Personal property reclassified under a cost segregation study (Section 1245 property) is recaptured at your ordinary income tax rate (up to 37% federal). The remaining building structure (Section 1250 property) is recaptured at a maximum federal rate of 25% (called "unrecaptured Section 1250 gain"). Both types of recapture are recognized in the year of sale.

**Section 1245 property:** IRS shorthand for depreciable personal property and certain other property (not including buildings and structural components). When a cost segregation study reclassifies building components into 5- or 7-year property, those components become Section 1245 property. On sale, any gain up to the total depreciation taken is taxed at ordinary income rates.

**Section 1250 property:** IRS shorthand for depreciable real property, including buildings and structural components. The building portion that remains in the 27.5- or 39-year class after a cost segregation study is Section 1250 property. On sale, unrecaptured Section 1250 gain is taxed at a maximum 25% federal rate.

**IRC Section 481(a) adjustment:** When you change the method of accounting for depreciation (as you do when you perform a lookback cost segregation study on a property you have owned for years), the IRS requires you to make a "catch-up" adjustment under Section 481(a) that accounts for all the extra depreciation you would have taken in prior years if the cost segregation had been done from the beginning. This catch-up amount is deducted entirely in the year you file Form 3115. It does not require you to amend prior returns.

**Form 3115 (Application for Change in Accounting Method):** The IRS form used to request a change in how you account for depreciation. Filing Form 3115 is how you implement a lookback cost segregation study on a property you have owned for multiple years. Most cost segregation changes are "automatic consent" changes, meaning you do not need advance IRS approval; you attach Form 3115 to your current-year return and claim the Section 481(a) adjustment. The current version of Form 3115 is the December 2022 revision.

**Form 4562 (Depreciation and Amortization):** The IRS form filed with your tax return to report all depreciation and bonus depreciation deductions claimed during the year, including deductions generated by a cost segregation study. Filed with your Form 1040 (individual), Form 1120-S (S corporation), Form 1120 (C corporation), or Form 1065 (partnership).

**Passive activity:** Under IRC Section 469, a "passive activity" is a trade or business or rental activity in which you do not materially participate. Losses from passive activities can only offset income from other passive activities, not wages or active business income. Most rental real estate is passive by default under IRC Section 469(c)(2).

**Material participation:** Active, regular, and continuous involvement in an activity's operations. The IRS defines material participation through seven tests in Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-5T(a). You meet material participation for an activity if you satisfy any one of the seven tests. The three most commonly used tests are: (1) you spent more than 500 hours in the activity during the year, (2) your participation in the activity was substantially all the participation of anyone (no one else, including paid help, participated more), or (3) you spent more than 100 hours in the activity during the year AND you spent at least as much time as any other person.

**STR loophole (short-term rental exception):** Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A) provides that a property rental is NOT treated as a rental activity under the passive activity rules if the average period of customer use is 7 days or fewer. When an activity is excluded from the "rental activity" category, it is instead treated as a trade or business. Losses from this trade or business are non-passive if you materially participate in the activity, meaning you can use those losses to offset W-2 wages or other active income without needing REPS qualification.

**Average period of customer use:** The total number of days the property is rented during the year, divided by the total number of separate rental contracts (bookings) for the year. A property rented to 40 guests for a combined 250 days would have an average stay of 250 / 40 = 6.25 days, which is 7 days or fewer and qualifies for the STR loophole.

**Real estate professional status (REPS):** A federal tax designation under IRC Section 469(c)(7) that removes the "rental activities are always passive" presumption for a qualifying taxpayer. A taxpayer who qualifies for REPS can treat rental activities as non-passive IF they also materially participate in each rental activity (or make a grouping election). REPS status does not automatically make all rentals non-passive; it is the combination of REPS qualification plus material participation in each property that converts the losses.

**The two REPS tests (both must be met every year):**
- **More-than-50%-of-personal-services test:** More than half of all personal services you perform during the year (across all trades and businesses) are in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate. IRC Section 469(c)(7)(B)(i).
- **750-hour test:** You perform more than 750 hours of services during the year in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate. IRC Section 469(c)(7)(B)(ii).

**Grouping election (REPS grouping):** Under Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-9(g), a real estate professional may elect to treat all rental activities as a single activity for the purpose of determining material participation. Without the election, you must separately meet a material participation test for each property. With the election, your hours across all properties are combined, making it easier to meet a single material participation test. The election is made by attaching a written statement to your tax return. It is binding in future years unless there is a change in facts that justifies revocation.

**Suspended losses:** Passive losses that exceeded passive income in prior years and were not deductible, but were carried forward to future tax years. Suspended losses become fully deductible when you either (a) generate passive income to absorb them, or (b) dispose of your entire interest in the activity in a fully taxable transaction.

**Schedule E (Form 1040):** The form used to report rental income and expenses for traditional rental properties. Rental income and losses from residential rental property flow through Schedule E.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The form used to report self-employment business income and expenses. A short-term rental that is non-passive and in which you provide substantial services may report on Schedule C rather than Schedule E; your advisor determines the correct form based on the services you provide.

**MAGI (Modified Adjusted Gross Income):** For the $25,000 passive activity loss allowance, MAGI is generally your regular adjusted gross income (AGI) without the rental real estate loss deduction itself and without certain other deductions. Your tax return will calculate this; do not subtract the rental loss when estimating MAGI for the allowance threshold.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before your advisor meeting. Bring the completed "Real Estate Strategy Intake Packet" download and the source documents that back up each entry.

**Property information (one set per property):**
1. Full address and property type (single-family home, multi-family apartment building, short-term rental cabin, commercial office building, mixed-use building, etc.)
2. Date the property was placed in service (the date it was first available for rent or business use, not the date of purchase if those differ)
3. Purchase price or construction cost, broken out if possible: land value, building value, personal property included in the purchase
4. Closing settlement statement (HUD-1 or CD -- Closing Disclosure) from original acquisition
5. Total capital improvements made since acquisition, by year, with descriptions and costs
6. Current estimated fair market value (optional but useful for planning)
7. Whether a prior depreciation schedule or cost segregation study exists for this property
8. Whether the property is currently under an IRS examination or audit

**Rental activity information:**
9. For long-term rentals: average length of tenant leases (month-to-month, 1-year, multi-year)
10. For short-term rentals: total rental bookings for the current year-to-date, total rental nights year-to-date, and the platform(s) used (Airbnb, VRBO, direct booking, etc.)
11. Name and contact of any property manager or management company used; their approximate annual hours managing your property

**Time log information (for REPS or STR material participation):**
12. Your current-year log of hours spent on real estate activities, broken out by activity type (see Step-By-Step Implementation for what counts)
13. If you have a W-2 job or business that is NOT real estate: total hours worked at that employment or business in the current year
14. Your spouse's hours in real estate activities, if applicable

**Tax and entity information:**
15. Current tax return entity structure: do you hold properties in your personal name, an LLC, a partnership, an S-corp, a C-corp, or a trust?
16. Most recent Form 1040 (or business return) showing current depreciation schedule
17. Current year estimated taxable income and estimated MAGI
18. Whether you have carried-forward passive activity losses from prior years (found on Form 8582 from your last return)

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect before the cost segregation study:**
- Original purchase settlement statement (HUD-1 or Closing Disclosure) showing allocation of the total purchase price
- Cost basis details for all capital improvements: invoices, contracts, and payment records
- Prior depreciation schedules (from Form 4562 of prior returns) to confirm what has already been claimed
- If the property was received as a gift or inheritance: documentation of the basis determination (appraisal, Form 706 estate return, or gift tax return Form 709)

**Collect during and after the cost segregation study:**
- The cost segregation report itself (the engineering firm's written analysis, which is your primary audit defense document)
- The engineer's credentials and methodology statement (IRS Audit Techniques Guide for cost segregation identifies qualified engineers as those with cost segregation-specific experience and engineering or construction background)
- Updated depreciation schedule reflecting all reclassified components
- Completed Form 3115 (if a lookback study) and the Section 481(a) adjustment calculation

**For STR material participation -- keep all year, every year:**
- Contemporaneous time log (a record created at or near the time of the activity, not reconstructed at year-end): date, hours, and description of each task (guest communications, cleaning coordination, listing management, maintenance, check-in/check-out, supply runs, bookkeeping, etc.)
- Contractor and cleaner time logs, if applicable: track how many hours any paid helper spent on your STR, because you must spend at least as much time as the most active helper to satisfy material participation Test 3
- Booking records confirming average stay length: export from Airbnb/VRBO showing booking dates, check-in, check-out, and number of nights per booking

**For REPS -- keep all year, every year:**
- Contemporaneous time log covering all real property trade or business hours (not limited to rental activities: include acquisition research, property showings, leasing activity, financing work, renovation oversight, property management tasks)
- Documentation of total hours in ALL work activities during the year, to support the more-than-50%-of-personal-services calculation
- Log of each property managed separately (before a grouping election is made)

**Retention guidance:**
- Keep all depreciation records and cost segregation reports for as long as you own the property AND for at least 3 years after the year you dispose of it (the IRS statute of limitations for audit is generally 3 years, but 6 years if income is understated by more than 25%). Keep sale records permanently or for 6 years after the sale year.
- Keep time logs for REPS and STR material participation for at least 6 years after the tax year they relate to, because the IRS aggressively audits these claims and reconstructed logs carry little weight.
- Keep Form 3115 and the Section 481(a) adjustment workpapers for at least 6 years after the year of filing.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

The workflow below is organized in the order most clients complete it. Your advisor may adjust the sequence based on your specific situation.

### Phase 1: Intake and Eligibility Assessment

**Step 1: Complete the Real Estate Strategy Intake Packet.**
Download the "Real Estate Strategy Intake Packet" from the Downloads section of this lesson. Fill in every section. If a field does not apply, write "N/A" rather than leaving it blank. Gather the source documents listed in Required Client Inputs before your advisor meeting. Bring the packet and all supporting documents.

**Step 2: Your advisor reviews the packet and screens for each strategy.**
Your advisor will use the intake packet to determine: (a) which properties qualify for a cost segregation study and whether it makes economic sense given your basis, tax situation, and any planned sale; (b) whether any properties qualify for the STR loophole (average stay at or below 7 days) and whether you currently materially participate; and (c) whether you or your spouse may qualify for REPS, based on your hours and employment situation.

Output: A written strategy memo from your advisor identifying which strategies apply, the estimated tax savings, and the recommended order of implementation.

**Step 3: Determine your entity structure for each property.**
If properties are held in an LLC, confirm with your advisor how the LLC is taxed. A single-member LLC disregarded for tax purposes is treated as if you own the property individually (the LLC does not change the analysis). A multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership reports on Form 1065. An S-corp reports on Form 1120-S. The entity structure affects which return the depreciation flows through and where the passive/non-passive determination is made.

### Phase 2: Cost Segregation Study

**Step 4: Select a qualified cost segregation firm.**
The IRS Audit Techniques Guide for cost segregation identifies an engineering-based, on-site inspection study as the most defensible methodology. Select a firm that employs engineers or construction professionals with demonstrated cost segregation experience. Confirm they will provide a written report with a detailed component-by-component breakdown, unit costs, and citations to MACRS property class assignments. Your advisor can recommend firms they have worked with.

**Step 5: Provide the firm with the property documentation.**
Give the cost segregation firm: (a) the settlement statement showing your purchase price and any allocated values, (b) construction drawings or architect's plans if available, (c) a list of all capital improvements with costs and dates, and (d) access to the property for a physical inspection if it is a new or forward-looking study. For a lookback study on a property you have owned for years, the firm will use historical records, photographs, and engineering estimates where original documents are unavailable.

**Step 6: Review the draft cost segregation report.**
Before your advisor files your return, review the draft report with your advisor. Confirm: (a) the total purchase price used in the study matches your settlement statement; (b) the reclassified components are plausible given the property type (a residential home should not show $500,000 in 5-year personal property if the total building value was $400,000); and (c) the recovery period assigned to each component matches IRS MACRS class lives.

**Step 7: For a lookback study on an existing property, have your advisor prepare Form 3115.**
If the property has been in service for more than one year and you are doing a cost segregation study for the first time, the change in depreciation method requires Form 3115. Your advisor will:
- Identify the correct "designated change number" from Revenue Procedure 2015-13 (the IRS's list of automatic accounting method changes).
- Calculate the Section 481(a) adjustment: the total extra depreciation that would have been claimed from the placed-in-service date through the end of the prior tax year if the cost segregation had been done originally.
- Attach Form 3115 to your current-year federal return. File one copy with the IRS Service Center where your return is filed and one copy with the IRS National Office (Ogden, Utah for most individual filers).
- Claim the entire Section 481(a) adjustment as a deduction on your current-year return. A negative adjustment (more depreciation than you took) is deducted in full in the year of change. A positive adjustment (you took more than you should have) is spread over 4 years.

**Step 8: Confirm bonus depreciation eligibility for reclassified components.**
For property placed in service after January 19, 2025 (or acquired after that date), reclassified components with a MACRS recovery period of 20 years or less (5-year, 7-year, or 15-year property) qualify for 100% bonus depreciation under IRC Section 168(k) as permanently amended by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. For property placed in service before January 19, 2025, the bonus depreciation percentage that applied in the year the property was originally placed in service governs [confirm with your advisor -- could not verify exact percentage for pre-OBBBA years applied on lookback against published IRS guidance for your specific placed-in-service date]. Your advisor will confirm the correct percentage for each component.

**Step 9: File your return with Form 4562 reflecting the updated depreciation.**
Form 4562 is filed with your individual or entity return. It reports the total depreciation claimed in the current year, including the Section 481(a) adjustment catch-up (for lookback studies) and bonus depreciation on newly reclassified components. The figures flow from Form 4562 to Schedule E (for individual landlords) or to the appropriate business return.

### Phase 3: STR Loophole Implementation

Complete this phase only if your advisor confirmed you own at least one property where the average guest stay is 7 days or fewer.

**Step 10: Verify the average period of customer use calculation for each STR property.**
Calculate: total rental nights (all bookings combined) divided by the total number of separate booking contracts for the year. If the result is 7.0 or fewer, the property qualifies for the STR exception under Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A) and is not treated as a rental activity under IRC Section 469. Perform this calculation mid-year and again at year-end to make sure the average has not drifted above 7 days due to longer bookings.

**Step 11: Meet one of the seven material participation tests for each STR property.**
Because the STR is not a rental activity, it is a trade or business. To deduct losses against active income, you must materially participate. Under Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-5T(a), meet any one of these tests:
- Test 1: You spent more than 500 hours in the STR activity during the year.
- Test 2: Your participation in the activity was substantially all the participation of any individual (no contractor, cleaner, or co-owner participated more than a token amount).
- Test 3: You spent more than 100 hours in the activity during the year AND you spent at least as many hours as any other single person (including paid cleaners and property managers).
- Tests 4 through 7 cover significant participation activity aggregation, prior-year participation patterns, facts and circumstances over 100 hours, and participation during any 5 of the prior 10 years for personal service activities. Ask your advisor if you believe you may qualify under these tests.

Tests 1, 2, and 3 are the most commonly used for STR owners. Most active owner-operators with a single property reach Test 3 (100+ hours, more than the cleaner). Test 1 (500+ hours) covers the most aggressive positions and is harder to meet alongside a full-time job.

**Step 12: Maintain a contemporaneous time log from January 1 through December 31.**
Every hour claimed for material participation must be logged with the date, duration, and a specific description of the task. Generic entries ("real estate work, 2 hours") are weak in an audit. Specific entries are better ("Airbnb guest check-in and property walkthrough, 45 minutes; responded to 6 guest messages, 30 minutes; coordinated cleaner turnover and inspected property after checkout, 1.5 hours"). Keep logs in a spreadsheet, calendar, or dedicated time-tracking app. Export or save the log monthly.

**Step 13: Confirm the correct tax form with your advisor (Schedule E vs. Schedule C).**
Most STR owners with average stays of 7 days or fewer who do not provide hotel-like daily services (meals, concierge, daily housekeeping during the stay) report on Schedule E. STR owners who do provide substantial services comparable to a hotel may need to report on Schedule C. Your advisor determines the correct form based on the specific services you provide.

**Step 14: Combine the cost segregation study with the STR (if applicable).**
If a cost segregation study was performed on your STR property and you materially participate, the depreciation losses generated by the study are non-passive in the year of change. This combination (cost seg + STR material participation) can produce very large current-year deductions against active income. Confirm with your advisor that the aggregate loss is accurate and that there are no at-risk limitations (IRC Section 465) that cap the deductible amount.

### Phase 4: Real Estate Professional Status (REPS)

Complete this phase only if your advisor has confirmed that you or your spouse may be able to meet both REPS tests.

**Step 15: Establish which spouse will qualify (if filing jointly).**
The two REPS tests (750 hours and more-than-50% of personal services) must be met independently by one spouse. You cannot combine hours from both spouses to satisfy either test. Identify which spouse is the "real estate spouse" for the year. That spouse must: (a) work more hours in real property trades or businesses than in any other trade, business, or employment, and (b) work more than 750 hours in real property trades or businesses in which they materially participate.

**Step 16: Identify all qualifying real property trade or business activities.**
Qualifying activities under IRC Section 469(c)(7)(C) include: real property development, redevelopment, construction, reconstruction, acquisition, conversion, rental, operation, management, leasing, and brokerage. Hours spent in any of these activities count toward the 750-hour test and the more-than-50% test. Hours spent in activities outside this list (investing, attending general business meetings, non-real-estate employment) do not count.

**Step 17: Track hours from January 1 and verify the pace quarterly.**
There is no partial-year REPS election. If you fall short of 750 hours or the more-than-50% test on December 31, you do not qualify for the year. Check your pace at the end of each quarter. If you are behind and have flexibility to increase real estate activity in the remaining months, do so. Document it.

**Step 18: Consider the grouping election.**
Without a grouping election, you must satisfy a material participation test separately for each rental property. With the grouping election under Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-9(g), all rental activities are treated as one activity, and your combined hours across all properties are used to test material participation. The election is ideal if you own multiple properties and your hours are spread across them. Once made, the election is binding for all future years unless your advisor determines a change in facts justifies revocation. Make the election by attaching a written statement to your tax return that says you are electing to treat all rental real estate interests as a single rental activity under Regulations Section 1.469-9(g).

**Step 19: Confirm no disqualifying W-2 employment.**
If the REPS spouse has any W-2 employment outside of real estate, those W-2 hours count against the more-than-50% test. For example, if the qualifying spouse works 600 hours in a non-real estate W-2 job and 700 hours in real estate, real estate hours are not more than 50% of total personal services (700 of 1,300 = 54%; this does pass the more-than-50% test, but the 750-hour test would also need to be met). Have your advisor run the math before year-end.

**Step 20: File your return with the REPS election and supporting documentation.**
REPS is not elected on a separate form. It is a status determined by facts on your return. Attach the time log to your return (or keep it in your files ready to produce in an audit). If using the grouping election, attach the written statement per Step 18. Your advisor will confirm that Schedule E (or the applicable business return) reflects the losses as non-passive.

### Phase 5: Year-End and Ongoing

**Step 21: Review the tax impact before the filing deadline.**
Before filing your return, confirm with your advisor: (a) that all passive/non-passive classifications are correct given your REPS status and material participation; (b) that Form 3115 (if applicable) has been filed correctly; (c) that bonus depreciation elections or opt-outs have been made intentionally; and (d) that state tax treatment is confirmed (many states do not conform to federal bonus depreciation or REPS rules -- see Entity, Payroll, State, and Current-Year Caveats).

**Step 22: Update your depreciation schedule for the next year.**
After a cost segregation study, your depreciation schedule has multiple components with different lives and methods. Your advisor will update the schedule on Form 4562 for each subsequent year. Keep the cost segregation report accessible throughout your ownership of the property.

**Step 23: Repeat the material participation log every calendar year.**
REPS and STR material participation are determined year by year. Carrying a prior-year log into the current year is not sufficient. Start a fresh log on January 1 of each tax year.

## Timing And Deadlines

- **Cost segregation study:** Can be performed at any time during the year. The results are claimed on the tax return for the year the property was placed in service (new study) or the year Form 3115 is filed (lookback study). No mid-year election is required; the study informs your return, which is due by the standard filing deadline.
- **Form 3115 (lookback study):** Must be filed with the tax return for the year in which you are implementing the method change. You may file your return on extension (automatic 6-month extension to October 15 for individual filers using Form 4868). The Form 3115 must be filed with the return, not separately in advance. A copy must also be sent to the IRS National Office (currently Ogden, Utah) by the due date of the return including extensions.
- **Bonus depreciation election:** Made by filing your return (with Form 4562 reflecting the bonus depreciation deduction). An election OUT of bonus depreciation (to not claim it) must also be made by the return due date including extensions, on an asset-class basis.
- **REPS qualification:** Determined on December 31 of the tax year. No mid-year election exists. If you fall short of 750 hours or the more-than-50% test on December 31, you do not qualify for that year.
- **Grouping election under Reg. 1.469-9(g):** Made with the tax return for the first year it is to be effective. If you want the election to apply to your 2026 return, it must be attached to the 2026 Form 1040 (due April 15, 2027, or October 15, 2027, with extension).
- **Annual time log:** Best practice is to log hours in real time throughout the year. At minimum, review and update monthly. The IRS expects contemporaneous records.
- **STR average stay check:** Calculate mid-year and at year-end. If the average is creeping above 7 days due to longer bookings, alert your advisor so the strategy can be adjusted.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure and passive activity rules:**
The passive/non-passive determination is made at the individual taxpayer level, not the entity level. If your rental properties are held in a partnership or S-corp, the loss flows to you through Schedule K-1 (the partner's or shareholder's share of income and loss), and the passive/non-passive determination is made on your individual Form 1040. The entity itself does not eliminate the passive activity rules. If your property is held in an S-corp and you materially participate in the S-corp's operations, the loss passed through to you is non-passive. Confirm with your advisor whether the entity structure creates any additional limitations.

**C corporations:**
Passive activity rules under IRC Section 469 apply to personal service corporations and closely held corporations, but with different rules than for individuals. If you hold rental property in a C-corp, the analysis is different from the individual REPS or STR analysis. This playbook covers the individual and pass-through entity context only.

**At-risk rules (IRC Section 465):**
The at-risk rules limit your loss deduction to the amount you have "at risk" in the activity (generally, your cash invested plus the adjusted basis of property contributed, plus certain nonrecourse financing for real estate). Even if a loss is non-passive (because you are REPS or STR-qualified), the at-risk rules may further limit the deductible amount. Your advisor will calculate your at-risk amount as part of the return preparation. This is especially relevant for highly leveraged properties.

**State conformity -- bonus depreciation:**
Many states do not conform to federal bonus depreciation. States that decouple from federal bonus depreciation require you to add back the federal bonus depreciation deduction on your state return and instead deduct depreciation using the state's own schedule (often the MACRS straight-line amount). This can result in a state tax bill in the year of the study, even though federal taxes are reduced. Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor before projecting net savings. Common non-conforming states include California, New York, and Illinois [confirm current conformity status with your advisor for your specific state -- state conformity laws change frequently].

**State conformity -- REPS:**
Most states follow the federal passive activity rules and recognize REPS status if the federal tests are met. However, some states have their own passive activity regimes or do not conform to IRC Section 469 in all respects. Confirm state treatment with your advisor.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):**
Non-REPS landlords with passive rental income may be subject to the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) under IRC Section 1411 on net rental income, if their MAGI exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). Non-passive rental income (from STR material participation or REPS) is generally exempt from NIIT. Confirm with your advisor whether NIIT applies to your rental income.

**Self-employment tax:**
Rental income reported on Schedule E is generally NOT subject to self-employment tax (15.3% on net earnings). However, if the STR activity rises to the level of a Schedule C business (because you provide hotel-like services), the net income may become subject to self-employment tax. This is one reason the Schedule E vs. Schedule C determination matters. Confirm with your advisor.

**Current-year note on bonus depreciation:**
The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, July 4, 2025) permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. For property placed in service before January 19, 2025 that is being reclassified through a lookback cost segregation study, the bonus depreciation percentage that applied in the original placed-in-service year governs those reclassified components. The phased-down rates that applied between 2018 and 2026 (80%, 60%, 40%, 20%) are relevant for lookback studies on older properties. Your cost segregation engineer and your advisor will determine the applicable rate for each component based on its placed-in-service date.

**Personal use of the STR property:**
If you personally use a short-term rental property for more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of the rental days in a year, the property may be treated as a vacation home under IRC Section 280A, which limits the deductible expenses to the percentage of time the property was rented (the rental-day ratio). Personal use significantly complicates the STR tax strategy. Minimize personal use, and track it separately from rental days and maintenance days. Confirm the vacation home rules with your advisor if you use the property personally.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Doing a cost segregation study on a passive property with no current-year benefit.**
If you do not qualify for REPS and your income is above $150,000 (phased out of the $25,000 allowance), and the property is not an STR with material participation, the cost segregation losses will be passive and suspended. The study costs money now for a deduction you cannot use until you have passive income or sell the property. Always confirm you can USE the deduction before paying for the study.

**2. Failing to meet the 7-day average test because of a few long bookings.**
A single 30-night booking can drive your average above 7 days if your volume of short bookings is not high enough. Recalculate the average every time you accept a long booking. If the average is at risk of exceeding 7 days, alert your advisor before year-end; the STR loophole depends entirely on meeting this threshold.

**3. Not tracking contractor hours.**
If you rely on a cleaner who works 120 hours per year on your STR and you only work 110 hours, you cannot meet material participation Test 3 (you must work at least as many hours as anyone else). Track your hours AND your contractor's hours from the start. This is a common audit focus point.

**4. Claiming REPS without the more-than-50% test.**
Many clients focus only on reaching 750 hours and miss the equally important requirement that MORE than half of ALL personal services be in real property trades or businesses. If you have a W-2 job, part-time business, or any significant non-real-estate work, your advisor must count all hours and confirm the test is met.

**5. Forgetting to file Form 3115 for a lookback study.**
A lookback cost segregation study that claims the Section 481(a) catch-up adjustment without filing Form 3115 is improper. The IRS may disallow the deduction or treat it as an amended return issue. Your advisor must file Form 3115 with the current-year return and also send a copy to the IRS Service Center and National Office.

**6. Failing to make (or revoke) the grouping election at the right time.**
The grouping election under Reg. 1.469-9(g) must be made on the return for the first year it applies. You cannot retroactively apply a grouping election to prior years. Once made, it is binding. Consider the election carefully when you first qualify for REPS.

**7. Neglecting state tax impact of bonus depreciation.**
Claiming 100% federal bonus depreciation on a $500,000 building reclassification can create a large state addback in a non-conforming state, resulting in an unexpected state tax bill. Clients are sometimes surprised when the federal refund is offset by a state tax payment. Always model both federal and state simultaneously.

**8. Using reconstructed time logs instead of contemporaneous records.**
Creating a time log in December based on memory of the full year is almost always insufficient in an IRS audit. IRS agents and Tax Court judges repeatedly reject reconstructed logs as unreliable. Use a real-time log from January 1.

**9. Over-claiming personal property in the cost segregation report.**
An aggressive study that reclassifies an unreasonably large percentage of the building's cost as 5-year personal property raises audit risk. Industry experience shows that typical residential rentals have 10-25% of building cost reclassified through cost segregation; very high reclassification percentages attract scrutiny. Review the report carefully with your advisor.

**10. Not accounting for depreciation recapture in exit planning.**
The tax benefit of a cost segregation study is a timing benefit (you get the deduction earlier). When you sell, recapture reverses some of that benefit. For short holding periods, recapture can be material. Run an exit analysis with your advisor before committing to a study, especially if you plan to sell within 5 years.

## Advisor Review Gates

Do not act on any of the following without first reviewing the specific facts with your advisor:

1. **Before ordering a cost segregation study:** Confirm that (a) you can actually use the deductions in the current year (non-passive status or sufficient passive income), (b) the study cost is justified by the projected tax savings, and (c) your state's conformity to bonus depreciation is understood.

2. **Before accepting a long STR booking that might push your average stay above 7 days:** Recalculate the average stay. If the average would exceed 7 days, the STR loophole may be lost for the entire year. Alert your advisor before accepting the booking.

3. **Before declaring REPS status on your return:** Verify the 750-hour count and the more-than-50% calculation with your advisor. REPS is one of the most-audited positions in individual income tax; documentation must be complete.

4. **Before making or revoking the grouping election:** Understand the permanent nature of the election and how it interacts with future property acquisitions or disposals.

5. **Before filing Form 3115 for a lookback study:** Confirm the designated change number, the Section 481(a) adjustment calculation, and the dual-filing requirement (return plus National Office copy).

6. **Before selling a property that has been through a cost segregation study:** Run a recapture analysis so you know the full tax cost of the sale before closing.

7. **If you are under audit or have received an IRS notice:** Do not initiate a cost segregation study or file Form 3115 while under examination without reviewing the implications with your advisor. The lookback protection provided by Form 3115 generally does not extend to years already under examination.

8. **If you operate in a state with significant non-conformity:** Confirm the net after-state-tax benefit before proceeding.

## Using The Download(s)

**Real Estate Strategy Intake Packet**

This is a structured questionnaire and checklist that collects all the information your advisor needs to evaluate cost segregation, the STR loophole, and REPS in one place. Use it as follows:

- **When to use it:** Complete this packet before your intake meeting with your advisor. Do not schedule the meeting until the packet is at least 80% complete. Your advisor cannot give you an accurate strategy recommendation without the property data, income estimates, time-log summary, and entity information in the packet.
- **Section 1 (Property Roster):** List every rental property you own, its address, purchase date, purchase price, entity that holds it, and whether a prior cost segregation study was done. Attach the settlement statement for each property.
- **Section 2 (Cost Basis and Improvements):** For each property, list the year-by-year capital improvements with dollar amounts. Attach invoices for any improvement over $5,000.
- **Section 3 (Rental Activity Summary):** For STR properties, enter the total bookings and total rental nights year-to-date. For long-term rentals, enter the lease length and vacancy rate. Attach a booking export from your rental platform if available.
- **Section 4 (Time Log Summary):** Enter your current-year total hours in real estate activities, broken out by property and activity type. If you are pursuing REPS, also enter your total hours in all non-real-estate work for the year.
- **Section 5 (Tax and Entity Section):** Attach the most recent Form 8582 from your last return (this shows your suspended passive losses), and your last Form 4562 (this shows your current depreciation schedule). Note the entity type holding each property.
- **Section 6 (Advisor Signature Block):** Leave blank. Your advisor completes this after reviewing the packet to confirm what strategy options are being considered and what additional items are needed.

Keep a copy of the completed packet in your records. Update it annually as part of your year-end tax planning process.

## Before You Act

Do not file your return, make an election, initiate a cost segregation study, change the reported status of rental losses from passive to non-passive, or represent that you qualify as a real estate professional without first confirming your specific facts with your advisor. The strategies in this playbook are legitimate and powerful, but each depends on meeting precise legal tests that vary by your hours, income, property type, and entity structure. A fact that differs from what you assumed (an average stay of 7.3 days instead of 6.8 days, or 740 hours instead of 760 hours) can eliminate the entire benefit or create a penalty for an improper position. Confirm the numbers, complete the intake packet, and review the advisor sign-off before acting.
