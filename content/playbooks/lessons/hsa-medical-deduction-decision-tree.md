# HSA / Medical Deduction Decision Tree

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

This playbook guides you through two related but separate tax strategies: contributing to a Health Savings Account (HSA) and deducting out-of-pocket medical expenses on Schedule A. Used together correctly, they can reduce your federal income tax on money you are already spending on healthcare. The decision tree in the attached worksheet helps you determine which path or combination of paths applies to your situation for tax year 2026.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook applies to you if any of the following are true:

- You are covered by a High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP) and want to contribute pre-tax dollars to an HSA.
- You have significant unreimbursed medical, dental, or vision expenses that may exceed 7.5% of your income.
- You are self-employed and pay your own health insurance premiums.
- You want to understand whether itemizing medical deductions beats taking the standard deduction.
- You were recently enrolled in a bronze or catastrophic ACA marketplace plan (new HSA eligibility rule effective January 1, 2026).

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not use the HSA strategy if any of the following apply (consult your advisor before contributing):

- You are enrolled in Medicare. Medicare enrollment disqualifies you from making new HSA contributions, even if you maintain HDHP coverage.
- Someone else can claim you as a dependent on their tax return.
- You have non-HDHP health coverage (for example, a spouse's traditional low-deductible plan) that covers you alongside your HDHP. Dual coverage generally disqualifies HSA contributions unless the second plan is a "permitted insurance" type.
- Your medical expenses are unlikely to exceed 7.5% of your Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) and you will take the standard deduction. In that case, the itemized medical deduction produces no additional tax benefit.
- You received Veterans Affairs (VA) medical benefits within the past three months. Receiving VA benefits for a condition other than a service-connected disability disqualifies you for that month.

## Key Terms

**Adjusted Gross Income (AGI):** Your total gross income minus specific above-the-line deductions (such as HSA contributions, self-employed health insurance premiums, and retirement contributions). AGI appears on Line 11 of Form 1040. It is the starting point for calculating both the medical deduction floor and HSA contribution limits that interact with your plan.

**Health Savings Account (HSA):** A tax-advantaged savings account you own that is paired with an HDHP. Contributions reduce your taxable income, money grows tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. After age 65, withdrawals for any purpose are taxed as ordinary income but carry no penalty.

**High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP):** A health insurance plan with a minimum annual deductible and a cap on annual out-of-pocket expenses that meet IRS thresholds. For 2026, the minimum deductible is $1,700 for self-only coverage or $3,400 for family coverage, and the maximum out-of-pocket limit is $8,500 (self-only) or $17,000 (family).

**Qualified Medical Expense:** A cost for the diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, as defined under Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 213(d). This includes doctor visits, prescriptions, dental, vision, mental health care, and most over-the-counter medications (OTC drugs became eligible without a prescription under the CARES Act in 2020). Health insurance premiums generally do NOT qualify for HSA withdrawals unless you are paying COBRA premiums, coverage while receiving unemployment compensation, or Medicare premiums after age 65.

**Itemized Deduction:** A specific expense you list on Schedule A (Form 1040) instead of claiming the flat standard deduction. You only benefit from itemizing when the total of your itemized deductions exceeds the standard deduction for your filing status.

**Standard Deduction (2026):** The flat deduction the IRS allows if you do not itemize. For tax year 2026: $16,100 (single or married filing separately), $32,200 (married filing jointly), $24,150 (head of household). Medical expenses on Schedule A only produce a tax benefit when your total itemized deductions exceed these amounts.

**Schedule A (Form 1040):** The IRS form used to report itemized deductions, including medical and dental expenses, mortgage interest, state and local taxes, and charitable gifts.

**Form 8889:** The IRS form you file with your tax return to report HSA contributions, employer contributions, and distributions. Required any year you contribute to or withdraw from an HSA.

**Form 5329:** The IRS form used to calculate and report the 6% excise tax on excess HSA contributions.

**Form 7206:** The IRS form used by self-employed individuals to calculate the deduction for health insurance premiums under IRC Section 162(l).

**Above-the-Line Deduction:** A deduction you can take before calculating AGI. HSA contributions made directly by you (not through an employer payroll deduction) are above-the-line deductions on Schedule 1 of Form 1040. This means you get the benefit even if you take the standard deduction.

**Catch-Up Contribution:** An additional HSA contribution allowed if you are age 55 or older at any point during the tax year and are not enrolled in Medicare. For 2026, the catch-up amount is $1,000.

**Last-Month Rule:** An IRS rule that allows you to contribute the full annual HSA limit for a year if you are enrolled in an HDHP on December 1 of that year, even if you were not enrolled for the entire year. Using this rule triggers a 12-month "testing period" (see Common Mistakes section).

**Excess Contribution:** Any HSA contribution that exceeds your annual limit. Excess contributions are subject to a 6% excise tax each year they remain in the account (reported on Form 5329).

**IRC Section 213(d):** The Internal Revenue Code section that defines what qualifies as a medical expense for both Schedule A deductions and HSA-qualified withdrawals.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before starting the worksheet:

1. Your 2026 health insurance card or Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC) confirming whether your plan is an HDHP and your coverage type (self-only or family).
2. Your estimated or actual 2026 Adjusted Gross Income (AGI). Find this on Line 11 of your most recent Form 1040 as a starting estimate if your income is stable.
3. All receipts or Explanation of Benefits (EOB) statements for out-of-pocket medical, dental, and vision expenses paid in 2026 that were not reimbursed by insurance or a prior HSA.
4. Any Form W-2 you receive for 2026 that shows HSA contributions made by your employer through payroll (Box 12, Code W). Employer contributions count toward your annual limit.
5. Your filing status (single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, head of household).
6. Confirmation of your age as of December 31, 2026 (relevant for the $1,000 catch-up contribution if you are 55 or older).
7. Your state of residence. Several states (Alabama, California, New Jersey, Wisconsin) do not conform to federal HSA tax treatment. Your advisor must evaluate state tax impact separately.
8. If self-employed: confirmation of how your business is structured (sole proprietor, S-corporation, partnership) and your net self-employment income for 2026.

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect and keep for at least three years after the filing deadline (longer if the return involves a substantial understatement):**

- HSA account statements from your HSA custodian showing all contributions and distributions for 2026.
- Receipts, invoices, or Explanation of Benefits (EOB) for every qualified medical expense paid from your HSA. The IRS may ask you to prove each distribution was for a qualified expense.
- Documentation of your HDHP coverage for 2026 (insurance card, policy declaration page, or employer benefits confirmation).
- Form W-2 (Box 12, Code W) if your employer contributed to your HSA.
- Form 1099-SA from your HSA custodian, showing total distributions taken in 2026.
- If claiming Schedule A medical deductions: sorted list of all unreimbursed medical expenses with dates, provider names, amounts paid, and nature of the expense.
- If self-employed and claiming a health insurance premium deduction: proof of premiums paid (insurance statements, canceled checks, or bank records) and your net profit figure from Schedule C, Schedule E (Part II), or Schedule K-1.

**Retention guidance:** Keep all HSA records until three years after the filing deadline for the year in which the distribution is taken (not just when the expense occurred), since you may pay an expense in one year but withdraw HSA funds in a later year.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Step 1: Determine HSA Eligibility

Use the decision tree on the attached worksheet. Answer each question in order:

**Question 1:** Are you covered under an HDHP as of the first day of any month in 2026?
- If no, you cannot contribute to an HSA for any month you lack HDHP coverage. Proceed to Step 5 to evaluate Schedule A medical deductions.
- If yes, proceed to Question 2.

**Question 2:** Do any of the disqualifying conditions apply? (See "When This Is Not A Fit" above: Medicare enrollment, claimed as a dependent, non-HDHP dual coverage, VA benefits in past 3 months.)
- If yes to any, you are not eligible for the affected months. Proceed to Step 5 for those months. Consult your advisor about partial-year proration.
- If no, proceed to Step 2.

### Step 2: Calculate Your Maximum HSA Contribution for 2026

Use the contribution limits below (from The Numbers section):

- Self-only HDHP coverage: $4,400
- Family HDHP coverage: $8,750
- If you are age 55 or older at any point in 2026 and not enrolled in Medicare: add $1,000

**Reduce your limit by** any employer contributions already made or committed for 2026 (shown on Form W-2, Box 12, Code W). You and your employer together cannot exceed the annual limit.

**If you were not enrolled in an HDHP for all 12 months** (and you are not using the last-month rule): multiply the applicable monthly limit by the number of months you had HDHP coverage on the first of each month. Your advisor can run the Line 3 Limitation Worksheet from Form 8889 instructions.

**If you were enrolled in your HDHP by December 1, 2026** and want to use the last-month rule: you may contribute the full annual limit regardless of how many months you had HDHP coverage. Important: you must then remain enrolled in an HDHP through December 31, 2027 (the testing period). Failure to do so causes the pro-rated excess to be added back to income plus a 10% penalty. Discuss this with your advisor before electing.

### Step 3: Make Your HSA Contributions

- Contributions can be made at any time during 2026 and also by the tax filing deadline of April 15, 2027 (even if you file an extension), for the 2026 tax year.
- Contribute directly to your HSA custodian (bank, credit union, or insurance company holding the account) or through your employer's payroll if offered.
- Payroll-deducted contributions avoid FICA taxes (Social Security and Medicare taxes, currently 7.65% employee share) in addition to income tax. Direct contributions to your HSA avoid income tax only.
- Do not exceed your calculated limit. Excess contributions trigger a 6% excise tax per year until corrected.

### Step 4: Use and Track HSA Distributions

- Pay qualified medical expenses (see Key Terms and The Numbers sections for what qualifies) directly from your HSA debit card or by reimbursing yourself from the account.
- Save every receipt. Your HSA custodian will issue Form 1099-SA at year-end showing total distributions, but the IRS can audit whether those distributions were for qualified expenses.
- Non-qualified distributions are added to your taxable income AND assessed a 20% additional tax (exceptions: distributions after age 65, disability, or death).
- You may keep HSA money invested and accumulate it for future medical expenses or retirement. There is no "use it or lose it" rule for HSAs (unlike Flexible Spending Accounts).

### Step 5: Determine Whether to Itemize Medical Deductions on Schedule A

This step is relevant whether or not you have an HSA. Complete this calculation:

a. Total all unreimbursed qualified medical expenses paid in 2026 (from your records). Do not include expenses paid with HSA funds, as they were already tax-free.
b. Calculate 7.5% of your 2026 AGI. Example: If your AGI is $120,000, the floor is $9,000.
c. Subtract the floor from total expenses. Only the amount above the floor is deductible. Example: $14,000 in expenses minus $9,000 floor equals $5,000 deductible.
d. Add this medical deduction to your other potential itemized deductions (mortgage interest, state and local taxes capped at $40,400 for 2026, charitable contributions, etc.).
e. Compare the total to your standard deduction for your filing status (see The Numbers section). If itemized deductions are higher, Schedule A saves you money. If not, take the standard deduction and skip the Schedule A medical deduction.

**Note for self-employed individuals:** Health insurance premiums you pay for yourself, your spouse, and your dependents are deductible 100% on Schedule 1 as an above-the-line deduction under IRC Section 162(l), reported on Form 7206. This deduction reduces your AGI. It is not reported on Schedule A. The Schedule A medical deduction covers out-of-pocket costs beyond premiums (copays, prescriptions, dental, etc.). These are separate deductions that can be used together.

### Step 6: File the Required Forms

- **Form 8889:** Required with your Form 1040 any year you contributed to or received a distribution from an HSA. Reports contributions, employer contributions, distributions, and calculates any tax or penalty.
- **Schedule A (Form 1040):** Required to claim the itemized medical deduction. Only file Schedule A if your total itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction.
- **Form 5329:** Required if you made excess HSA contributions that were not withdrawn by the filing deadline.
- **Form 7206:** Required if you are self-employed and deducting health insurance premiums.

- You may deduct only the portion of qualified medical expenses that exceeds **7.5% of your AGI**.
- This threshold is permanent law under the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021 (Public Law 116-260).
- Form: Schedule A (Form 1040), Line 4.

Source: IRS Publication 502 (2025 edition, applicable to 2026 reporting), IRS Topic No. 502.

| Filing Status | Standard Deduction |
|---|---|

| Married Filing Jointly | $32,200 |
| Married Filing Separately | $16,100 |
| Head of Household | $24,150 |

### Penalties

| Situation | Penalty |
|---|---|
| HSA distribution for non-qualified expense (before age 65, death, or disability) | Amount added to income PLUS 20% additional tax (Form 8889, Part II) |
| Excess HSA contribution not corrected by deadline | 6% excise tax per year the excess remains (Form 5329) |
| Last-month rule testing period failure | Pro-rated contributions added to income PLUS 10% additional tax |

Sources: IRS Publication 969; IRS Form 8889 Instructions (2025).

### Medical Mileage Rate (2026)

- **20.5 cents per mile** for travel to receive qualifying medical care. Keep a mileage log showing date, destination, and medical purpose.

Source: IRS Notice 2026-10.

### Health FSA Contribution Limit (2026) -- For those whose employer offers a Flexible Spending Account instead of or alongside an HSA.

- $3,400 per year in employee salary reductions.
- Note: A general-purpose Health FSA disqualifies you from contributing to an HSA. A "limited-purpose" FSA (covering only dental and vision) may be paired with an HSA.

Source: IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-61.

### New for 2026: Expanded HSA Eligibility

Effective January 1, 2026, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act:
- **Bronze and catastrophic plans** (ACA marketplace plans and similar plans) are treated as HSA-compatible regardless of whether they technically meet the standard HDHP deductible and out-of-pocket thresholds.
- **Direct Primary Care (DPC) arrangements** are permitted alongside HSA eligibility. You may use HSA funds to pay DPC monthly fees up to $150 per month (single) or $300 per month (family).
- **Telehealth:** You may use telehealth services before meeting your HDHP deductible and still remain HSA-eligible. This rule is permanent for plan years beginning on or after January 1, 2025.

Source: IRS Notice 2026-05; IRS.gov newsroom (Treasury/IRS guidance on OBBBA HSA provisions).

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure affects HOW you get the deduction:**

- **Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C filers):** You contribute to your HSA directly and deduct on Schedule 1 (above the line). You also deduct health insurance premiums via Form 7206. Both deductions reduce your AGI.
- **S-corporation shareholders (owning more than 2% of shares):** Your S-corporation should include your health insurance premiums in your W-2 Box 1 wages, and you then deduct them on Form 7206. HSA contributions work differently: your S-corporation can contribute to your HSA directly (and the contribution is deductible to the S-corp and excluded from FICA wages) but it must be included in Box 1 of your W-2 as income, and you then deduct it on Form 8889. If your S-corp does not contribute, you may contribute directly but you do NOT avoid FICA. Discuss with your advisor how to structure this correctly.
- **Partners in a partnership:** Similar to sole proprietors. Premiums flow through to the Schedule K-1 and are deductible on Form 7206. HSA contributions are made directly.
- **C-corporation owners (employees of your own C-corp):** Your C-corp may offer HSA-compatible group HDHP coverage. Employer HSA contributions are excludable from your wages entirely (best structure for tax savings).

**Payroll:** If you process payroll for yourself (S-corp), HSA contributions made through payroll deduction avoid both income tax and FICA taxes. Contributions made outside payroll avoid income tax only. This difference can amount to 15.3% on contributions in some situations.

**State conformity:** The following states do not recognize HSA tax benefits and may tax your HSA contributions or distributions as ordinary income:
- **California:** Does not conform. HSA contributions are not deductible; HSA earnings are taxed.
- **New Jersey:** Does not conform. HSA contributions are not deductible for state income tax purposes.
- **Alabama:** Does not conform.
- **Wisconsin:** Does not conform.

If you live in or have income sourced to any of these states, your advisor must calculate state-level impact separately. The federal playbook above does not account for state tax treatment.

**Current-year legislative note:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed in 2025, introduced HSA changes effective January 1, 2026 (see The Numbers section). If your health plan is a bronze or catastrophic ACA plan, confirm with your advisor and HSA custodian that your plan qualifies under the new rules before contributing.

**Medicare enrollment:** If you turn 65 in 2026 and enroll in Medicare mid-year, you must prorate your HSA contribution by the number of months before your Medicare start date. Your advisor must calculate this, as enrolling in Medicare Part A retroactively (which Medicare sometimes does automatically for Social Security recipients) can create an unexpected excess contribution.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Contributing to an HSA while enrolled in a general-purpose Health FSA.** A general-purpose FSA provides "other health coverage" that disqualifies HSA contributions. If your employer offers a Health FSA, confirm it is a "limited-purpose FSA" (dental and vision only) before contributing to an HSA.

**2. Using HSA funds for non-qualified expenses before age 65.** This triggers a 20% penalty plus income tax. Keep a documented list of every qualifying expense before withdrawing.

**3. Exceeding the annual contribution limit.** Both your own contributions and your employer's contributions count toward the limit. Over-contributing creates a 6% excise tax each year until corrected.

**4. Missing the April 15 contribution deadline.** HSA contributions for 2026 must be made by April 15, 2027. Unlike IRA contributions, filing an extension does NOT extend the HSA contribution deadline.

**5. Using the last-month rule without understanding the testing period.** If you enroll in an HDHP on or before December 1, 2026, contribute the full annual amount, and then switch to a non-HDHP plan before December 31, 2027, the difference between what you contributed and what you were entitled to based on actual months of coverage is added back to income with a 10% penalty.

**6. Trying to deduct medical expenses that were paid with HSA funds.** You cannot deduct on Schedule A any expense you already paid tax-free from your HSA. That is double-dipping, which the IRS disallows.

**7. Claiming the Schedule A medical deduction without comparing to the standard deduction.** Even with $20,000 in medical expenses, Schedule A may not help you if your total itemized deductions are below the standard deduction for your filing status.

**8. Ignoring state non-conformity.** Assuming your HSA is tax-free at the state level when you live in California, New Jersey, Alabama, or Wisconsin will result in state tax underpayment and potential penalties.

**9. Losing receipts for HSA distributions.** The IRS does not require you to file receipts with your return, but if audited, you must prove each distribution was for a qualified expense. Keep documentation for as long as you hold the account and for three years after the return is filed.

**10. Medicare retroactive enrollment creating an excess contribution.** Enrolling in Social Security benefits at or after age 65 can trigger retroactive Medicare Part A enrollment. If Medicare Part A begins mid-year unexpectedly, you may need to withdraw HSA contributions for the affected months. Flag this with your advisor before the year-end if you are approaching 65.

## Advisor Review Gates

Bring the following situations to your advisor BEFORE acting:

1. **You are considering the last-month rule.** The testing period risk requires your advisor to weigh whether the additional contribution savings justify the compliance exposure if your coverage changes.

2. **You own more than 2% of an S-corporation.** The correct structure for S-corp owner HSA contributions and health insurance premium deductions is nuanced and error-prone. Get written confirmation from your advisor on the payroll treatment before your first payroll run of the year.

3. **You receive or are about to apply for Medicare.** Retroactive Part A enrollment is common and may create an excess HSA contribution without warning. Do not contribute to your HSA in the months you are enrolled in Medicare.

4. **You live in or earn income from California, New Jersey, Alabama, or Wisconsin.** State non-conformity requires a separate state-level analysis.

5. **Your HDHP is a bronze or catastrophic ACA plan (new for 2026).** Confirm with your advisor that your specific plan qualifies under IRS Notice 2026-05 before contributing.

6. **Your total medical expenses are close to (but not clearly above) 7.5% of your AGI.** Your advisor can determine whether accelerating or deferring elective procedures and payments changes which year you clear the threshold.

7. **You have both a Health FSA and an HSA offered through your employer.** General-purpose FSAs disqualify HSA contributions. Verify the FSA type before contributing to either.

8. **You are married and each spouse is covered under a different HDHP.** Combined family contribution rules apply and the calculation is not straightforward.

9. **You changed coverage type (self-only to family, or vice versa) mid-year.** A proration calculation is required and the Form 8889 worksheet must be completed with monthly figures.
