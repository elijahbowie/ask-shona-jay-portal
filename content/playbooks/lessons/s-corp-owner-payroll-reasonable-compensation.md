# S-Corp Owner Payroll / Reasonable Compensation

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

If you own an S-corporation (a specific type of small business corporation that passes income through to your personal tax return) and you work in the business, federal law requires you to pay yourself a reasonable salary (a real, payroll-processed wage) before taking any profit distributions. Getting this salary right creates a legal split between taxable wages (subject to payroll taxes) and distributions (not subject to payroll taxes), which is the primary tax-saving mechanism of S-corp ownership. Paying too little in salary is one of the most common and most audited S-corp mistakes; paying too much eliminates the tax advantage you structured the S-corp to create. This playbook walks you through how to set, document, pay, and file your reasonable compensation correctly for 2026.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy applies to you if ALL of the following are true:

- You own stock in a corporation that has made a valid S-election with the IRS (meaning the IRS approved the business being treated as an S-corp for tax purposes).
- You perform services for the S-corp -- you are actively working in the business, not just a passive investor.
- You receive or intend to receive payments from the S-corp, whether labeled as salary, distributions, loans, or anything else.
- The S-corp has net income (profits) after deducting legitimate business expenses.

This playbook is most valuable for S-corp owners who are the primary driver of the business's revenue and who currently take all or most of their payments as distributions rather than wages, or who have set a salary but have not reviewed it against market benchmarks in more than a year.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Purely passive S-corp owners:** If you own stock but perform zero services for the corporation, you are not required to take a salary. However, if the IRS later determines you did provide services, the no-salary position can be reversed with penalties and interest. Confirm your role in writing with your advisor before taking no salary.
- **S-corps with no net income:** If the corporation has no profit from which to pay a salary, there is no tax advantage to split. You still have an obligation to pay yourself for services rendered, but the strategy's benefit only materializes when there is income to shelter. Work with your advisor on the startup-loss period.
- **Single-owner S-corps operating at a loss:** The payroll tax savings do not exist if there are no profits. However, you must still track all payments for classification purposes.
- **Non-resident alien shareholders:** S-corps cannot have non-resident alien shareholders. If this applies, the S-election may be invalid; consult your advisor immediately.
- **Businesses still deciding on entity structure:** This playbook assumes an S-election is already in place. If you are evaluating whether to elect S-corp status, discuss the decision (including reasonable compensation obligations) with your advisor before electing.
- **C-corporations:** A C-corporation (a corporation that pays its own income tax, separate from the owners) has different compensation rules. This playbook does not cover C-corps.

## Key Terms

**S-corporation (S-corp):** A domestic corporation that has filed Form 2553 with the IRS to be treated as a pass-through entity for federal income tax. This means the corporation itself does not pay federal income tax on its profits; instead, profits and losses pass through to the shareholders' personal tax returns in proportion to their ownership. Governed by Subchapter S of the Internal Revenue Code (IRC Sections 1361-1379).

**Shareholder-employee:** An S-corp owner who also performs services for the corporation. The IRS treats shareholder-employees as employees for payroll tax purposes. Per the IRS, "the definition of an employee for FICA, FUTA and federal income tax withholding under the Internal Revenue Code includes corporate officers."

**Reasonable compensation:** The wage amount the IRS expects an S-corp to pay a shareholder-employee for services actually performed, before any profit distributions are made. The IRS defines it as "the value that would ordinarily be paid for like services by like enterprises under like circumstances." There is no IRS formula or published safe-harbor dollar amount; it is a facts-and-circumstances determination based on your role, your industry, and comparable market wages.

**Distribution:** A payment from the S-corp to a shareholder that represents a share of the corporation's profits, separate from wages. Distributions are NOT subject to FICA (payroll) taxes. This is the mechanism that generates payroll tax savings -- but only after a reasonable salary has been paid. Distributions that the IRS believes should have been wages are subject to back taxes, interest, and penalties.

**FICA:** Federal Insurance Contributions Act taxes. FICA has two components: Social Security tax and Medicare tax. Both the employer (the S-corp) and the employee (you) pay FICA on each paycheck. For 2026, the combined employer-plus-employee FICA rate on wages up to $184,500 is 15.3% (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare). Above $184,500, only Medicare applies (2.9% combined). See The Numbers section for the full breakdown.

**Social Security wage base:** The maximum amount of wages subject to the 6.2% Social Security tax in a given year. For 2026, the Social Security wage base is $184,500. Wages above this threshold are not subject to the 6.2% Social Security tax, but remain subject to the 1.45% Medicare tax with no cap.

**Payroll taxes:** A broad term for employment taxes withheld from or paid on wages. For an S-corp owner-employee, payroll taxes include: (1) FICA (Social Security and Medicare), (2) federal income tax withholding, and (3) federal unemployment tax (FUTA).

**FUTA:** Federal Unemployment Tax Act. Employers (not employees) pay FUTA on wages paid to employees. The FUTA tax rate is 6.0% on the first $7,000 of wages paid to each employee per year, but employers who make timely state unemployment contributions receive a credit of up to 5.4%, reducing the net FUTA rate to 0.6% on the first $7,000 per employee.

**Form W-2:** The Wage and Tax Statement that the S-corp must issue to each employee (including you as owner-employee) after each calendar year, showing total wages paid and taxes withheld. W-2s for wages paid in 2025 are due to employees and to the SSA by February 2, 2026 (January 31, 2026 falls on a Saturday, so the deadline shifts to the next business day, Monday February 2). W-2s for wages paid in 2026 are due by February 1, 2027 (January 31, 2027 falls on a Sunday, so the deadline shifts to Monday February 1).

**Form 941:** Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return. Filed quarterly to report wages paid, federal income taxes withheld, and FICA taxes. Due April 30 (Q1), July 31 (Q2), October 31 (Q3), and January 31 (Q4).

**Form 940:** Employer's Annual Federal Unemployment (FUTA) Tax Return. Filed annually, due January 31 of the following year (February 10 if all FUTA taxes were deposited on time).

**Form 1120-S:** U.S. Income Tax Return for an S Corporation. The annual tax return the S-corp files to report income, deductions, and the allocation of those items to shareholders. For calendar-year S-corps, the 2025 Form 1120-S is due March 16, 2026 (March 15 falls on a Sunday). The 2026 Form 1120-S will be due March 16, 2027 [confirm the exact date with your advisor].

**Schedule K-1:** A form attached to Form 1120-S that shows each shareholder's share of the S-corp's income, deductions, and credits for the tax year. You use your K-1 to report S-corp pass-through income on your personal Form 1040.

**EFTPS:** Electronic Federal Tax Payment System. The IRS's free online portal (eftps.gov) that employers use to deposit payroll taxes. All employers must deposit federal payroll taxes through EFTPS or another electronic method. Enrollment can take up to five business days and must be completed before you can make deposits.

**Payroll deposit schedule:** The timing rule that determines when you must deposit payroll taxes collected. Your schedule (monthly or semi-weekly) is determined by your total payroll tax liability during a "lookback period" -- for 2026, that lookback period is July 1, 2024 through June 30, 2025. If your total payroll tax liability during that period was $50,000 or less, you are a monthly depositor (deposit by the 15th of the following month). If it was more than $50,000, you are a semi-weekly depositor. New employers with no lookback history start as monthly depositors. The $100,000 next-day rule applies to all depositors: if you accumulate $100,000 or more in a single day, deposit by the next business day.

**Section 199A QBI deduction:** The Qualified Business Income deduction, created by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 and made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025). It allows eligible S-corp shareholders to deduct up to 20% of their qualified business income (the pass-through profit on their K-1) from their taxable income. The salary you pay yourself as W-2 wages is NOT part of QBI; only the K-1 distribution income qualifies. Higher salaries reduce QBI and can reduce the 20% deduction, which is a tradeoff your advisor must factor into salary-setting. For higher-income taxpayers, the deduction phases in limitations; confirm your specific income level and any applicable W-2 wage limitation with your advisor.

**Additional Medicare Tax:** An extra 0.9% Medicare tax that applies to individual wages exceeding $200,000 in a calendar year (single filer threshold; $250,000 for married filing jointly). The S-corp must withhold the 0.9% additional tax from your wages once they exceed $200,000 in a calendar year. There is no employer match on the Additional Medicare Tax -- only the employee side is assessed.

**Recharacterization:** The IRS's authority to reclassify payments made to shareholder-employees from non-wage distributions (not subject to employment taxes) to wages (subject to employment taxes and related penalties). Recharacterization results in back FICA taxes, interest, and potentially a 100% trust fund recovery penalty for unpaid employment taxes.

**Lookback period:** The 12-month period the IRS uses to classify your deposit schedule for the upcoming year. For 2026, the lookback period is July 1, 2024 through June 30, 2025.

**Corporate resolution:** A formal, written record of a decision made by the S-corp's board of directors. Setting or adjusting officer compensation requires a corporate resolution. This document is key audit evidence.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before setting your salary:

1. **Industry and job title:** What industry is your business in, and what would your role be called if you were hiring someone else to do it? (Example: "healthcare staffing, operations director.")
2. **Geographic location:** What city and state does the business operate in? Compensation varies significantly by market.
3. **Hours worked:** How many hours per week do you actually work in the business?
4. **Specific duties:** List what you actually do for the business -- sales, operations, service delivery, management, finance, administration. The more duties you cover, the higher a market-rate salary tends to be.
5. **Business gross receipts and net income:** What are the S-corp's total revenues and profits (after expenses, before your salary adjustment)?
6. **Number of non-shareholder employees and their roles:** If the business generates revenue through other employees, a portion of income is attributable to them rather than to your personal services.
7. **Current salary (if any):** What are you paying yourself now? When was the last time this was reviewed?
8. **Your state(s) of operation:** State payroll taxes, state income tax withholding, and state unemployment taxes apply on top of federal requirements. The rules vary by state.
9. **EIN (Employer Identification Number):** Your S-corp's nine-digit federal tax ID, required for payroll filings.
10. **Payroll system in place:** Are you using a payroll service provider, payroll software, or processing manually? If none, you need to set one up before running your first paycheck.
11. **EFTPS enrollment status:** Have you enrolled the S-corp in EFTPS? If not, allow at least five business days.

## Required Documents And Records

**Records to collect before implementing:**

- Articles of incorporation and the IRS acceptance letter for Form 2553 (proof of S-election).
- Prior-year Form 1120-S and Schedule K-1s (shows historical compensation and distributions).
- Prior-year Form W-2 (if any salary was previously paid).
- Payroll tax account information (EFTPS login, state payroll tax accounts).
- Compensation benchmark report (see Step 2 below) -- at minimum a printed or saved copy of the data source used.

**Ongoing records to create and keep:**

- **Corporate resolution setting compensation:** A dated, signed board resolution stating the approved annual salary for each officer-shareholder, the effective date, and how the amount was determined. Update this each time salary changes.
- **Compensation benchmark documentation:** A written record of the data sources you used (BLS Occupational Employment Statistics, industry salary surveys, or a professional benchmark tool), the comparable roles you examined, and how you landed on your number. Include the date prepared. Retain this permanently -- it is your audit defense.
- **Payroll records:** Retain all payroll registers, pay stubs, and direct deposit confirmations showing each paycheck date and amount. Retain for at least four years after the tax due date (IRS standard for employment tax records).
- **Form 941 copies:** Keep copies of all filed quarterly returns and confirmation of electronic filing. Retain four years.
- **Form 940 copies:** Keep copies of all annual FUTA returns. Retain four years.
- **Form W-2 copies:** Keep copies of all W-2s issued and filed. Retain four years.
- **EFTPS deposit confirmations:** Keep confirmation numbers for every tax deposit. Retain four years.
- **State payroll records:** Retain state withholding and unemployment returns per your state's record-retention rules, which may exceed the federal four-year minimum.

**Retention note:** The IRS generally has three years from the filing date to audit a return, but can extend to six years if income is understated by more than 25%. Retain payroll records for at least six years to cover the extended period; indefinitely for the corporate resolution and compensation rationale memo.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Step 1: Confirm S-election is active

Pull your IRS acceptance letter for Form 2553 from your records. If you cannot locate it, request a confirmation letter from the IRS by calling the Business Tax Line at 1-800-829-4933, or work with your advisor to request a copy. Do not proceed until you can confirm the S-election is in place and the corporation is in good standing.

**Output:** Written confirmation that the S-election is valid.

### Step 2: Determine your reasonable compensation amount

This is the most judgment-intensive step. The IRS does not publish a formula, but it will compare your salary to what a similar business would pay an unrelated person to do the same job. Use at least one of these market-data sources:

- **Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics (OEWS):** Free at bls.gov. Search by occupation and geographic area for the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile annual wages. This is the most commonly cited free source.
- **Professional salary benchmarking tools:** Services such as RCReports (rcreports.com) produce a written, defensible reasonable compensation report that is recognized in Tax Court and is widely used by CPAs. Your advisor may have access to this or similar tools.
- **Industry association surveys:** Many industry trade associations publish annual compensation reports for common roles.

Apply the IRS's eight factors to your situation (see Common Mistakes for a walkthrough): training and experience, duties and responsibilities, time and effort devoted to the business, dividend payment history, compensation paid to non-shareholder employees, timing of bonus payments, comparable business compensation, and compensation agreements and the use of a formula to determine compensation. Document how each factor applies to you.

Set a salary in a range you can defend with the data. The salary should reflect what you would pay an arm's-length hire to perform the same work. If your business generates revenue primarily through your own personal services, the salary should be a significant portion of net income -- not a token amount.

**Output:** A written compensation analysis (even a one-page memo) citing data sources, comparable roles, and your concluded salary figure. Keep this on file permanently.

### Step 3: Pass a corporate resolution

Hold a formal (or written-consent) board of directors meeting and pass a resolution that:

- Names the officer-shareholder receiving compensation.
- States the approved annual salary (e.g., $85,000 per year).
- States the effective date.
- References the compensation analysis supporting the amount.
- Is signed by all directors or all shareholders (in a single-owner S-corp, signed by you).

**Output:** A signed, dated corporate resolution in your corporate minute book.

### Step 4: Set up payroll if not already running

If the S-corp does not already run payroll:

1. Obtain an Employer Identification Number (EIN) if you do not have one (apply free at irs.gov/ein).
2. Enroll in EFTPS at eftps.gov (allow five business days for enrollment to process). You will receive a PIN by mail.
3. Register with your state's payroll tax agencies (state withholding account and state unemployment account). Requirements vary by state; your advisor or a payroll service provider can identify the specific agencies.
4. Choose a payroll method: a payroll service provider (such as Gusto, ADP, Paychex, or QuickBooks Payroll), payroll software, or manual calculation with your bookkeeper. For owner-payroll, a payroll service provider is strongly recommended to ensure correct tax withholding and timely deposits.
5. Add yourself as an employee in the payroll system with your full legal name, Social Security number, address, and salary rate.
6. Complete Form W-4 (Employee's Withholding Certificate) for yourself as the employee. This tells the payroll system how much federal income tax to withhold.

**Output:** Payroll system is active; EFTPS enrollment is confirmed; state accounts are open.

### Step 5: Run payroll on a consistent schedule

- Choose a pay frequency: weekly, bi-weekly (every two weeks), semi-monthly (twice a month), or monthly. Monthly or semi-monthly is common for S-corp owner-employees.
- Issue yourself actual paychecks (or direct deposits) on that schedule. The payments must actually move from the corporate bank account to your personal account. Entries in a ledger with no actual transfers do not count.
- Each paycheck must have payroll taxes withheld: federal income tax (per your W-4), employee Social Security (6.2% up to $184,500 in wages), and employee Medicare (1.45% on all wages; an additional 0.9% once wages exceed $200,000).
- The S-corp must simultaneously pay the employer share: employer Social Security (6.2% up to $184,500 in wages) and employer Medicare (1.45% on all wages).

**Output:** Regular paychecks issued and documented on your payroll records.

### Step 6: Deposit payroll taxes on schedule

Do not wait until you file Form 941 to pay the taxes. Payroll taxes must be deposited separately, on a deposit schedule determined by your lookback period:

- **Monthly depositors:** Deposit all payroll taxes for wages paid in a given month by the 15th day of the following month. Deposit using EFTPS.
- **Semi-weekly depositors:** Deposit payroll taxes within two to three days of each payroll run (see Key Terms for the exact schedule).
- **$100,000 rule:** If accumulated payroll tax liability in a single day reaches $100,000, deposit by the next business day regardless of your schedule.

New S-corps running payroll for the first time are monthly depositors by default.

**Output:** EFTPS deposit confirmation for each payroll period's tax deposit. Save every confirmation number.

### Step 7: File Form 941 quarterly

After each calendar quarter, file Form 941 (Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return) to reconcile wages paid and taxes deposited:

- **Q1 (January-March):** File by April 30, 2026.
- **Q2 (April-June):** File by July 31, 2026.
- **Q3 (July-September):** File by October 31, 2026.
- **Q4 (October-December):** File by January 31, 2027.

If all payroll taxes were deposited on time during the quarter, you have 10 additional calendar days after each deadline to file.

Form 941 can be filed electronically through your payroll software or provider, or mailed to the IRS address listed in the Form 941 instructions. Electronic filing is strongly recommended.

**Output:** Accepted Form 941 for each quarter, with confirmation number saved.

### Step 8: Pay and track FUTA taxes throughout the year

The S-corp owes FUTA (federal unemployment tax) on the first $7,000 of wages paid to each employee. The FUTA rate is 6.0%, but you receive a credit of up to 5.4% for timely state unemployment contributions, making the effective net rate typically 0.6% on the first $7,000 per employee (maximum $42 per employee per year at the 0.6% net rate).

If your total FUTA liability during a quarter exceeds $500, you must deposit it by the end of the month following that quarter. If your cumulative FUTA liability remains under $500, carry it forward until it exceeds $500 or until year-end (whichever comes first) and then deposit.

File Form 940 (Employer's Annual Federal Unemployment Tax Return) by January 31 of the following year (February 10 if all FUTA taxes were deposited timely).

**Output:** FUTA deposits made when required; Form 940 filed by deadline.

### Step 9: Issue Form W-2 after year end

After the calendar year ends, generate Form W-2 for yourself showing:

- Box 1: Total wages subject to federal income tax.
- Box 3 and Box 5: Wages subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes.
- Box 4 and Box 6: Social Security and Medicare taxes withheld.
- Box 2: Federal income tax withheld.
- State boxes: State wages and state income tax withheld (requirements vary by state).

Deadlines for W-2s covering wages paid in calendar year 2025: furnish to yourself and file with the SSA by February 2, 2026 (January 31, 2026 falls on a Saturday, so the deadline shifts to the next business day, Monday February 2). For wages paid in calendar year 2026: furnish to yourself and file with the SSA by February 1, 2027 (January 31, 2027 falls on a Sunday, so the deadline shifts to Monday February 1).

File Copy A of each W-2 along with Form W-3 (Transmittal of Wage and Tax Statements) with the Social Security Administration (SSA). Employers filing 10 or more information returns must file electronically through the SSA's Business Services Online (BSO) portal. If filing fewer than 10, paper filing is permitted but electronic is recommended.

**Output:** W-2s issued to yourself; W-2 and W-3 filed with SSA by deadline.

### Step 10: File Form 1120-S and attach Schedule K-1

The S-corp files Form 1120-S annually. For calendar-year S-corps:

- 2025 Form 1120-S is due March 16, 2026 (March 15 is a Sunday).
- A 6-month extension may be requested on Form 7004, extending the deadline to September 15, 2026.

Line 7 of Form 1120-S asks for compensation of officers. Your W-2 wages go here. The S-corp's remaining net income flows through to your Schedule K-1 as pass-through income. Your K-1 income (the distributions-eligible income, not your W-2 wages) may qualify for the Section 199A QBI deduction. Confirm with your advisor how to optimize the salary/distribution split taking the QBI deduction into account for your income level.

**Output:** Form 1120-S filed on time; K-1 received and used to file your personal Form 1040.

### Step 11: Review compensation annually

Set a calendar reminder in Q4 of each year to:

- Pull updated salary benchmark data for your role and location.
- Compare your current salary to the updated market data.
- Review your S-corp's net income for the year.
- Consider whether significant changes in your role, revenue, or hours require a salary adjustment.
- Pass a new corporate resolution if you adjust the salary.
- Document the review in writing even if the salary does not change.

**Output:** Annual compensation review memo on file; updated corporate resolution if salary changed.

**Key illustration -- the payroll tax savings math:**

Assume your S-corp has $200,000 in net income, and you set a $100,000 salary with $100,000 in distributions.

- FICA on salary: $100,000 x 15.3% = $15,300 (split equally between you and the S-corp).
- FICA on distributions: $0.
- If you had taken the full $200,000 as salary: $184,500 x 15.3% + $15,500 x 2.9% = $28,228.50 + $449.50 = $28,678 in FICA.
- By splitting salary and distributions, you save approximately $13,378 in FICA taxes (before considering QBI deduction tradeoffs).

This illustration is for education only. Your actual savings depend on your specific salary, income level, filing status, state taxes, and QBI deduction eligibility. Run your numbers with your advisor.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity caveats:**

- **S-election must be valid.** If the S-corp has more than 100 shareholders, a non-resident alien shareholder, an ineligible shareholder entity, or a second class of stock, the S-election may be terminated. If terminated, back distributions may be treated as C-corp dividends subject to double taxation. Verify your S-election status annually with your advisor.
- **New S-elections in 2026:** If you recently converted from an LLC or C-corp and elected S status, confirm the effective date of the election with the IRS before running payroll under the S-corp rules. The election is not retroactive unless specifically requested within the allowable window.
- **Built-in gains tax:** If your S-corp was previously a C-corp, a 5-year recognition period may apply to certain built-in gains. This does not affect payroll directly but affects overall tax strategy. Ask your advisor.

**Payroll caveats:**

- **You cannot pay yourself as an independent contractor.** S-corp officer-shareholders who perform services must be W-2 employees. Issuing a 1099-NEC to yourself to avoid payroll taxes is specifically flagged by the IRS as a misclassification. Employment taxes will be assessed on 1099 payments to shareholder-employees.
- **Shareholder loans are not wages.** Characterizing distributions as "loans" from the S-corp to the shareholder does not avoid employment tax if the IRS determines the payments are really compensation. Loans require documented terms, interest at or above the applicable federal rate, and actual repayment.
- **Zero-salary S-corps are a major IRS audit trigger.** The IRS actively targets S-corps that show officer compensation of $0 on Form 1120-S while the shareholder has received distributions. Even a low salary is better than none if you are working in the business.
- **Payroll must match Form 1120-S.** The officer compensation on Line 7 of Form 1120-S must match the W-2 wages you report for yourself. Discrepancies trigger notices.

**State caveats:**

- **State income tax withholding:** Most states with an income tax require employers to withhold state income tax from employee wages. Enroll in your state's withholding account before the first paycheck.
- **State unemployment tax (SUTA):** Every state has a state unemployment insurance program. As an employer, the S-corp must register, pay SUTA contributions quarterly, and file state unemployment returns. SUTA rates vary widely by state and by employer experience rating.
- **State payroll tax returns:** Many states require separate quarterly payroll tax filings on state-specific forms. Do not assume that filing Form 941 federally satisfies state obligations.
- **Multi-state operations:** If you work in more than one state, you may have payroll tax obligations in multiple states. This requires separate state account registration and can require state-specific payroll tax apportionment. Work with your advisor if you operate across state lines.
- **States with no income tax:** If your S-corp operates in a state with no personal income tax (currently Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming), there is no state income tax to withhold, but SUTA still applies. Confirm current state tax law with your advisor.

**Current-year caveats:**

- **Section 199A is now permanent** under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025), but the phase-in ranges and limitations were modified. Your salary-setting decision must account for your specific QBI deduction eligibility. The interaction between salary level and the W-2 wage limitation within the QBI deduction is particularly important for high-income S-corp owners. Confirm your situation with your advisor.
- **Social Security wage base adjusts annually.** The $184,500 figure is for 2026. It will likely change for 2027. Update your payroll calculations each January.
- **Deposit schedule classification resets annually.** Your lookback period resets each year. Confirm your deposit schedule classification (monthly vs. semi-weekly) at the start of each calendar year.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Setting the salary too low (most audited mistake).**
Paying yourself $1 in salary and $200,000 in distributions is a textbook IRS audit trigger. Courts have upheld IRS recharacterization of distributions as wages in cases such as David E. Watson, PC v. U.S. (668 F.3d 1008, 8th Cir. 2012). A salary that is obviously disproportionate to the revenue you generate exposes you to back FICA taxes plus a 100% trust fund recovery penalty for unpaid employment taxes.

**2. No written documentation of how you set the salary.**
The burden of proof is on you, not the IRS. If audited, you need to show data -- comparable salary surveys, your duties, your hours, your analysis. A verbal decision with no paper trail is not a defense.

**3. Failing to run actual payroll.**
Journal entries that record "deemed wages" without actual checks or direct deposits clearing the bank are not valid payroll. The IRS requires actual payments. Run the payroll system; move the money.

**4. Paying quarterly or annually instead of regularly.**
Issuing a single large paycheck in December to cover the whole year is a red flag. Pay yourself on a consistent, regular schedule (at least monthly) that mirrors how the business would pay an arm's-length employee.

**5. Missing payroll tax deposit deadlines.**
The IRS charges tiered penalties for late deposits: 2% if 1-5 days late, 5% if 6-15 days late, 10% if more than 15 days late, and 15% if amounts remain unpaid 10 days after an IRS notice. These add up quickly. Set calendar reminders and automate deposits through your payroll provider.

**6. Forgetting state payroll obligations.**
Many owners set up EFTPS and file Form 941 but overlook state withholding, SUTA, and state payroll tax returns. State penalties and interest are separate from federal penalties.

**7. Ignoring the QBI deduction tradeoff.**
A salary that is too high relative to net income reduces the K-1 pass-through income available for the 20% Section 199A deduction. If your income is in the phase-out range, the salary amount directly affects the size of your QBI deduction. Optimize both together with your advisor.

**8. Never updating the salary.**
Setting a salary once and never revisiting it is problematic. If your revenue grows significantly and your salary stays flat, the ratio becomes harder to defend. Review annually.

**9. Classifying yourself as a 1099 contractor.**
This is not a gray area. The IRS explicitly includes corporate officers in the definition of "employee" for FICA and FUTA. Attempting to pay yourself via 1099 to avoid payroll taxes is treated as misclassification, not as a legitimate alternative.

**10. Confusing officer compensation with health insurance premiums.**
S-corp owners who pay health insurance premiums may be able to deduct those premiums as self-employed health insurance (they are reported on the W-2 in Box 1 but not in Boxes 3 and 5, and then deducted on the owner's personal Form 1040). This is a separate topic with its own rules. Do not include health insurance premiums in the "reasonable compensation" salary analysis or net them against the salary. Discuss health insurance treatment with your advisor.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and confirm with your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

1. **You have never paid yourself a salary from the S-corp.** Before running your first paycheck, have your advisor confirm the correct salary level, payroll setup, and state registrations.
2. **Your current salary is less than 40% of your S-corp's net income.** This ratio is not an IRS safe harbor, but it is a common audit focus area. Have your advisor review your compensation analysis before the next payroll run.
3. **Your S-corp's net income increased by more than 25% compared to last year** and you have not adjusted your salary. A frozen salary while income grows is a warning sign; have your advisor review the ratio.
4. **You are operating in more than one state.** Multi-state payroll requires state-specific registrations, withholding rules, and SUTA accounts. Do not guess; have your advisor or a multi-state payroll specialist set this up.
5. **You have received an IRS notice referencing officer compensation, reasonable salary, or CP261** (the S-election acceptance notice -- not directly a compensation notice, but signals IRS awareness of the S-corp). Engage your advisor before responding to any IRS notice.
6. **You intend to pay yourself via 1099, shareholder loan, or any method other than W-2 payroll.** This requires advisor review before implementation.
7. **Your S-corp has more than one shareholder.** Multi-owner S-corps must ensure salary decisions are consistent with buy-sell agreements, shareholder agreements, and equity percentages. Do not change compensation without all shareholders' awareness and appropriate agreements in place.
8. **You are considering changing your salary because of the Section 199A deduction.** The salary-to-QBI optimization is specific to your income level, filing status, and business type. Run the numbers with your advisor before adjusting.
9. **You receive income from a Specified Service Trade or Business (SSTB).** SSTBs (such as law, accounting, health, consulting, and financial services) face additional Section 199A limitations at higher income levels. The compensation-to-distribution optimization may work differently. Confirm with your advisor.

**Stop here and confirm the following with your advisor before you run your first paycheck, change your salary, or file any payroll tax return:**

- Your S-election is valid and current.
