# Mixed-Purpose Travel Decision Framework

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

A mixed-purpose trip is a trip that combines business activity with personal activity -- for example, attending a client conference and then staying an extra three days for sightseeing, or booking a destination vacation and scheduling a few client calls while you are there. The tax treatment of a mixed-purpose trip turns almost entirely on one question: what was the primary purpose of the trip? Get that question right before you book, document it carefully, and you can deduct the transportation cost and all business-day expenses. Get it wrong, and you may deduct nothing. This playbook gives you a step-by-step decision process -- covering domestic and international trips separately -- so you can plan and document each mixed trip correctly and claim the deduction you have actually earned.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is valuable for:

- **Self-employed business owners, sole proprietors, and single-member LLCs taxed as sole proprietorships:** You deduct qualifying travel on Schedule C (Form 1040), reducing both income tax and self-employment tax (the 15.3% tax that covers Social Security and Medicare for the self-employed).
- **S-corporation and C-corporation owner-employees:** The entity deducts the business portion of travel either by paying directly or reimbursing you under an accountable plan (a formal employer reimbursement arrangement that meets IRS rules -- see Key Terms).
- **Partners in a partnership or multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership:** The partnership may deduct business travel directly, or you may deduct unreimbursed partnership expenses (business costs you pay personally that the partnership agreement does not reimburse) on Schedule E.
- **Business owners who travel frequently and mix personal and business days on the same trip:** The decision framework in this playbook is specifically designed to help you plan, count, and document each day correctly before and during the trip.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Trips that are primarily personal with a token business activity tacked on:** If the true reason you took the trip is personal -- a vacation, a family visit, leisure travel -- the fact that you scheduled a single business call or checked email does not make it a business trip. The IRS looks at the overall purpose of the journey, not isolated business moments.
- **W-2 employees who do not fall into a narrow qualifying exception:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed into law on July 4, 2025, permanently eliminated the miscellaneous itemized deduction for unreimbursed employee business expenses for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. For tax year 2026, a regular W-2 employee cannot deduct unreimbursed mixed-purpose travel at the federal level. The only exceptions are Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, and fee-basis state or local government officials, who may still use Form 2106.
- **Day trips that do not require overnight sleep or rest:** The "away from home" requirement demands that you need to sleep or rest while away because of the demands of your work. A same-day round trip -- even a long one -- that does not require you to spend the night does not satisfy the test. If there is no overnight stay, there is no lodging deduction and no per diem for meals.
- **Trips to your regular place of business:** Commuting from your home to your regular office is never deductible. This rule applies even if your commute is unusually long or the trip required overnight travel.
- **Situations with no contemporaneous records:** Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 274(d) requires written, timely documentation of every travel expense. "Contemporaneous" means created at or near the time of travel, not reconstructed from memory months later. If you cannot substantiate a trip with records made at the time, the deduction is disallowed even if the travel was genuinely for business.

## Key Terms

**Mixed-purpose trip:** Any trip that serves both a business purpose and a personal purpose -- combining a conference with a vacation extension, scheduling client meetings during a personal trip, or attending training in a city where you also visit family.

**Primary purpose test:** The IRS rule for determining whether the transportation costs of a mixed-purpose trip are deductible. If the trip's primary purpose is business, the round-trip transportation is fully deductible. If the primary purpose is personal, none of the transportation is deductible. For domestic trips, primary purpose is generally determined by comparing business days to personal days. For international trips, the rules are more detailed (see Step-By-Step Implementation below).

**Tax home:** Your main place of business or post of duty, regardless of where your family lives. If you have multiple business locations, the IRS uses three factors to identify your principal tax home: total time spent at each location, level of business activity at each, and amount of income earned at each. If you have no regular place of business, your tax home may be where you regularly live. Your tax home matters because you must be "away from" it for travel expenses to be deductible.

**Away from home:** You are traveling away from home when your business duties require you to be away from the general area of your tax home for a period substantially longer than an ordinary day's work, AND you need to sleep or rest to meet the demands of your work while away. Both conditions must be met. The rest requirement is not satisfied by napping in a car.

**Business day:** For the purpose of counting days on a mixed-purpose trip, a business day is: (1) a day you spend traveling to or from a business destination (a transportation day); (2) a day when your presence at a location is required for a specific business purpose (a presence-required day); (3) a day when your principal activity during working hours is business-related; or (4) a weekend or holiday that falls between two business days (a sandwiched non-work day). A weekend that falls after your last business day, during which you stay at the destination for personal reasons, does not count as a business day.

**Personal day:** Any day that is not a business day under the definition above. Days spent on vacation, sightseeing, personal errands, visiting family, or attending to personal matters are personal days.

**Contemporaneous record:** A log, diary, receipt, or written note created at or near the time of each expense or activity -- not reconstructed from memory. The IRS gives much stronger weight to records made during or immediately after the trip than to records assembled at tax time.

**IRC Section 274(d) substantiation:** The mandatory record-keeping rule that applies to travel, meals, vehicle use, and gifts. You must document four elements for every expense: (1) the amount of the expense, (2) the time and date, (3) the place or description, and (4) the business purpose. Without all four elements, the expense is disallowed.

**50% meal limitation:** Under IRC Section 274(n), only 50% of the cost of business meals is deductible. This applies whether you use actual meal costs or a standard daily meal allowance (per diem). The 50% limit applies to meals on business days only; personal-day meals are not deductible at all.

**Per diem:** A fixed daily rate used instead of tracking every meal receipt. The IRS publishes two sets of rates: (1) the GSA (General Services Administration) standard CONUS (continental United States) per diem rates, which vary by city, and (2) the IRS high-low per diem method, which uses only two rates. Self-employed individuals may use the standard per diem for meals only; they must use actual receipts for lodging. Employees reimbursed by their employer may use either method.

**M&IE:** Meals and incidental expenses. The "incidental expenses" portion covers fees and tips for porters, hotel staff, and similar service workers. M&IE is the meals-plus-incidentals component of a per diem rate.

**CONUS:** The continental United States -- the 48 contiguous states plus the District of Columbia. This excludes Alaska, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, and other U.S. territories, which have their own per diem tables (called OCONUS rates).

**The 7-day rule (international travel):** If your trip outside the United States lasts seven days or fewer -- not counting the day you depart -- the entire trip is treated as entirely for business for transportation purposes, regardless of how many personal days were included. You may deduct the full round-trip transportation cost.

**The 25% personal-time rule (international travel):** If your international trip lasts more than seven days, but fewer than 25% of the total days you were outside the U.S. were personal days, the entire trip is treated as entirely for business for transportation purposes. You may deduct the full round-trip transportation cost.

**Transportation allocation (international travel):** When neither the 7-day rule nor the 25% rule applies to an international trip, you must prorate the transportation cost: the deductible portion equals total transportation cost multiplied by (business days outside the U.S. divided by total days outside the U.S.).

**Accountable plan:** An employer reimbursement arrangement that meets three IRS requirements: (1) the expenses have a business connection, (2) the employee substantiates the expenses with documentation within a reasonable time, and (3) the employee returns any excess advance within a reasonable time. Reimbursements under a valid accountable plan are excluded from the employee's W-2 income and not subject to payroll tax.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The tax form sole proprietors and single-member LLC owners use to report business income and expenses. Travel deductions for the self-employed appear on Schedule C, Part II, Line 24a (travel, including transportation and lodging) and Line 24b (deductible meals, after applying the 50% reduction).

**Self-employment tax:** The tax self-employed individuals pay to cover Social Security and Medicare. Because Schedule C deductions reduce net self-employment income, a travel deduction saves both income tax and self-employment tax for sole proprietors and single-member LLCs -- making the effective value of each deductible dollar higher than for an employee.

**IRC Section 162:** The general tax code provision that allows a deduction for all ordinary and necessary expenses paid or incurred during the taxable year in carrying on a trade or business. Travel deductions flow through this section.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before applying the framework to any trip:

1. **Trip purpose statement:** In one to two sentences, state the specific business reason for the trip. What meeting, conference, client visit, contract, or business activity required you to travel? Be specific -- "attend the annual tax strategy conference for CPAs in Dallas" is acceptable; "explore business opportunities" is not.
2. **Trip dates and destination:** Departure date, return date, and primary destination city.
3. **Business-day list:** A specific list of each day of the trip and what business activity occurred on that day (or why the day qualifies as a business day under the sandwiched-day rule). Assemble this before you depart or during the trip.
4. **Personal-day list:** A specific list of each day that is a personal day, and what personal activity occurred.
5. **Transportation costs:** The total cost of getting to and from the destination (airfare, train fare, rental car, miles driven, tolls). You will need this number to apply the allocation formulas.
6. **Lodging costs:** Actual hotel or lodging receipts for each night. Self-employed individuals must use actual lodging costs; per diem does not substitute.
7. **Meal costs:** Either actual receipts for each meal or your election to use the applicable per diem M&IE rate. The 50% limit applies to whichever method you use.
8. **Other qualifying costs:** Baggage fees, laundry, business calls, tips for service workers, business materials shipped to the destination.
9. **International travel details (if applicable):** Total number of days outside the U.S. (not counting the departure day), number of those days that were business days, number that were personal days. This is required to apply the 7-day and 25% rules.
10. **Entity type and reimbursement status:** Are you deducting as a sole proprietor (Schedule C), an S-corp or C-corp paying directly, or an owner being reimbursed under an accountable plan? Was any portion reimbursed?

## Required Documents And Records

Create and keep the following for every mixed-purpose trip. Do not wait until tax season -- assemble these records during and immediately after each trip.

**Trip-level records:**
- **Pre-trip business purpose memo:** A written statement (an email to yourself, a calendar event note, or a document) created before you depart, explaining why business required you to travel, what specific activities you will conduct, and who you will meet. Created before the trip, this is far more credible to an auditor than a statement written after the fact.
- **Day-by-day schedule or agenda:** Your planned itinerary showing which days are business days and which are personal. A printed conference agenda, meeting calendar, or client correspondence qualifies.
- **Post-trip log:** A contemporaneous record (written during or within a day or two of each travel day) documenting for each day: the date, destination, who you met or what business activity you performed, and the business purpose. Three sentences per day is enough. Phone notes or emails to yourself are acceptable.

**Expense records:**
- **Transportation receipts:** Airfare confirmations, train tickets, rideshare receipts, rental car invoices, toll and parking receipts for every transportation expense.
- **Lodging receipts:** Hotel folios showing dates, nightly rates, and total charges. Receipts are required for all lodging, regardless of dollar amount. Per diem cannot substitute for lodging if you are self-employed.
- **Meal receipts or per diem election documentation:** If using actual costs, keep itemized meal receipts. If using the standard M&IE per diem, document your election and note the applicable rate for each destination.
- **Other expense receipts:** Baggage fees, laundry, business communication charges, tips. For non-lodging expenses under $75, a receipt is not strictly required by regulation (Treasury Regulation 1.274-5(c)(2)(iii)) -- but bank records, credit card statements, or a written log entry for each expense are still good practice and required to document the amount, date, and purpose.

**Business-purpose support:**
- Emails, contracts, client correspondence, or letters confirming meetings or engagements at the destination.
- Conference or event registration confirmations.
- Meeting agendas or notes taken at the meetings.
- Any invoices or proposals discussed or signed during the trip.

**Mixed-purpose calculation worksheet:**
- The Mixed-Purpose Travel Decision Framework Implementation Worksheet (the attached download) documents your day count, the primary purpose conclusion, and the allocation calculation for each trip. Complete it for every mixed trip. Store it with the underlying records.

**Retention period:** Keep all travel records for at least three years from the date the tax return on which the deduction appears is filed. If you underreport income by more than 25% of your gross income reported on the return, the IRS has six years to assess additional tax. When in doubt, keep records for six years. Employment tax records (if your entity pays W-2 employees) must be kept for at least four years after the tax becomes due or is paid.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Work through these steps in order -- ideally before you book the trip, during travel, and then after you return.

### Phase 1: Before You Book

**Step 1: Establish that the base trip qualifies as business travel.**

Before considering the personal component, confirm four threshold facts:
- Your tax home is established and the destination is away from it.
- The trip will require overnight sleep or rest (an overnight stay is necessary for lodging and meal deductions).
- There is a specific, identifiable business purpose that could justify the trip on its own even if you removed the personal days.
- The trip is to a temporary location (not a location where you have been working for more than one year or expect to work for more than one year).

If any of these is uncertain, stop and consult your advisor before booking.

**Step 2: Classify each day of the trip as business or personal before you depart.**

Go through the trip calendar day by day and assign each day a classification using the definitions in Key Terms above:

- Day you depart: counts as a business day if your primary travel that day is to the business destination.
- Business meeting or conference days: business days.
- Days when your presence is required for a specific business purpose, even if personal activities also occur: business days.
- Weekends or holidays between two scheduled business days: business days (sandwiched non-work days).
- Weekends or holidays after your last scheduled business day, during which you stay for personal reasons: personal days.
- Day you return: counts as a business day if you are traveling home.
- Pure vacation or sightseeing days: personal days.

Write this list out. It is the foundation of the primary purpose test.

**Step 3: Apply the domestic primary purpose test (for trips within the United States).**

Count total business days and total personal days.

- If **business days outnumber personal days**: the trip's primary purpose is business. Transportation (round-trip airfare, train, rental car, or mileage) is 100% deductible. Proceed to Step 5.
- If **personal days equal or outnumber business days**: the trip's primary purpose is personal. Transportation is NOT deductible. Only lodging and meal costs on actual business days may be deductible -- and only if those individual days satisfy the business-activity requirement. Proceed to Step 4 before deciding whether to take any deduction at all.
- If the count is equal, the IRS treats the trip as personal in the absence of other evidence of business purpose. This is an inherently contested area -- consult your advisor before proceeding.

**Step 4 (Personal-primary domestic trips only): Decide whether any deduction is worth pursuing.**

If the primary purpose is personal, the only expenses that may still be deductible are the lodging and meals on actual business days -- not the transportation. This is usually a smaller deduction that requires the same full documentation burden. Assess whether the remaining deductible amount is meaningful enough to justify the documentation effort. In most cases, a trip that fails the primary purpose test for transportation also produces limited deductions overall.

**Step 5 (International trips only): Apply the international rules before booking.**

International travel -- any travel outside the United States -- has its own set of rules that can be more favorable than the domestic test if you plan around them.

Apply the tests in this order:

**Test A (the 7-day rule):** Count the total days outside the United States, NOT including the day of departure from the U.S. If the total is seven days or fewer, the entire trip is treated as entirely for business for transportation purposes -- regardless of how many personal days were in that window. The full round-trip transportation cost is deductible. Move directly to Step 6.

**Test B (the 25% rule):** If the trip is longer than seven days, calculate: personal days outside the U.S. divided by total days outside the U.S. If personal days are less than 25% of total days outside the U.S., the entire trip is treated as entirely for business for transportation. The full round-trip transportation cost is deductible. Move to Step 6.

**Test C (transportation allocation):** If neither Test A nor Test B applies (the trip is more than seven days AND personal days are 25% or more of total days outside the U.S.), you must prorate transportation:

> Deductible transportation = Total transportation cost x (Business days outside U.S. / Total days outside U.S.)

Use this formula to calculate the deductible portion of airfare or other transportation. The non-deductible personal share is a personal expense.

**Note on international day counting:** "Outside the United States" begins when you leave the United States and ends when you return. For the 7-day and 25% tests, count each full day outside the U.S. Do not count the day you depart from the U.S. (that day counts as a U.S. day). Do count the day you return to the U.S. as a day outside the U.S. if you were physically outside the U.S. at the start of that day -- this is a fact-specific question; confirm with your advisor if travel straddles midnight.

### Phase 2: During Travel

**Step 6: Write contemporaneous log entries every day.**

Each evening during the trip, open your notes app (or a paper travel log) and record:
- The date and destination.
- What business activity you conducted during the day (meeting name, client name, conference session, or other business purpose).
- Who you met with, if applicable (name and company or business relationship).
- One sentence stating why that activity relates to your trade or business.

For personal days: note that the day was personal and briefly what you did. This demonstrates you are distinguishing business from personal days honestly.

Do not summarize multiple days at once. Do not wait until you return home.

**Step 7: Photograph every receipt the same day you incur the expense.**

Take a photo of each receipt (transportation, lodging, meals, incidentals) and save it to a labeled folder. Do not wait until checkout to photograph hotel folios. Do not rely on credit card statements alone -- they show amounts but not the business purpose or expense category.

**Step 8: Track vehicle miles if you are driving at the destination.**

For every drive from your lodging to a business location, record: date, starting address, ending address, business purpose, and odometer start and end readings. A mileage tracking app is acceptable. Do not track miles for personal drives (those are personal expenses).

### Phase 3: After You Return

**Step 9: Complete the Mixed-Purpose Travel Decision Framework Implementation Worksheet.**

Within one week of returning, fill out the worksheet (see Using The Download(s) below). The worksheet guides you through:
- Recording the day-by-day classification (business vs. personal).
- Applying the correct primary purpose test (domestic or international).
- Calculating the deductible transportation amount (100% or allocated).
- Totaling deductible lodging (business days only) and deductible meals (business days only, before the 50% reduction).
- Calculating the 50% meal deduction.
- Calculating vehicle deduction if applicable (business miles times $0.725 per mile for 2026, or actual expenses).

**Step 10: Separate and total expenses by category and deductibility.**

Using the worksheet and your receipts, calculate:

| Expense Type | Deductible Amount |
|---|---|
| Transportation (domestic, business-primary) | 100% of actual cost |
| Transportation (domestic, personal-primary) | $0 |
| Transportation (international, Test A or B passed) | 100% of actual cost |
| Transportation (international, Test C applies) | Total cost x (Business days / Total days outside U.S.) |
| Lodging | 100% of actual cost for business days only |
| Meals (before 50% limit) | Actual cost or applicable M&IE per diem rate, for business days only |
| Meal deduction (after 50% limit) | 50% of the business-day meal total |
| Vehicle (if driving) | Business miles x $0.725 (2026 standard rate), or actual expenses x business-use % |
| Other qualifying costs (baggage, laundry, business calls, tips) | 100% of actual cost attributable to business days |

**Step 11: Report the deduction on the correct form.**

- **Sole proprietor / single-member LLC (Schedule C):** Transportation and lodging on Line 24a (travel); deductible meals (after the 50% reduction) on Line 24b. Vehicle costs on Part II, Line 9 (car and truck expenses), supported by Part IV or Form 4562.
- **S-corp or C-corp paying travel directly:** Deduct on the entity's corporate return (Form 1120S or Form 1120) as a business expense. No income or payroll tax effect on the owner.
- **S-corp or C-corp reimbursing owner under an accountable plan:** The entity deducts the reimbursement amount; the owner does not include it in income and it is not subject to payroll tax.
- **Partner deducting unreimbursed partnership expenses (UPE):** Deducted on Schedule E as a separate line below the partnership income. Confirm the partnership agreement supports this before filing.
- **Qualifying employees (reservists, performing artists, fee-basis government officials):** Use Form 2106 and carry the result to Schedule 1 (Form 1040) as an above-the-line deduction.

**Step 12: Archive the trip file.**

Store all of the following together, organized by tax year:
- Pre-trip business purpose memo.
- Day-by-day travel log.
- All receipts (transportation, lodging, meals, other).
- Business purpose support (emails, agendas, client correspondence).
- Completed Mixed-Purpose Travel Decision Framework Implementation Worksheet.

Keep this file for at least three years (or six years if there is any concern about the 25% underreporting threshold).

## Timing And Deadlines

- **Contemporaneous records are required and must be made at or near the time of travel.** There is no specific number of days specified in the regulation, but the IRS interprets "contemporaneous" strictly. Write your travel log entry the same evening or the following morning at the latest.
- **The 2026 standard business mileage rate of $0.725 per mile** applies to all business vehicle miles driven from January 1, 2026 through December 31, 2026, per IRS Notice 2026-10.
- **Per diem rates run on a federal fiscal year (October 1 through September 30), not the calendar year.** The rates in this playbook (from IRS Notice 2025-54 and GSA FY 2026 tables) apply to travel on or after October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026. Confirm the new rates for travel after September 30, 2026 when the FY 2027 rates are published (typically in mid-August each year).
- **Complete the worksheet within one week of returning from each trip.** Do not accumulate trips and complete worksheets in December.
- **Annual return deadlines for the travel deduction:**
  - Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C on Form 1040): April 15, 2027, with an automatic six-month extension to October 15, 2027 available via Form 4868. Note: the extension extends the filing deadline, not the payment deadline. Estimated tax payments for 2026 are due quarterly (April 15, June 16, September 15, and January 15, 2027).
  - S-corps (Form 1120S) and partnerships (Form 1065): March 15, 2027, with an automatic six-month extension to September 15, 2027.
- **Quarterly review rhythm:** Tally your mixed-trip deductions by category at the end of each quarter (March 31, June 30, September 30, December 31). Do not wait until year-end to review all trips at once.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure determines how the deduction is captured:**

- **Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs:** The deduction appears on Schedule C and reduces both income tax and self-employment tax. A sole proprietor paying travel expenses personally deducts them directly on the return -- no intermediary accountable plan is needed (though documentation requirements are identical).
- **S-corp owner-employees:** Travel should be paid by the corporation directly (on a corporate card) or reimbursed to you under a written accountable plan. If the S-corp pays directly, it is a corporate deduction with no income or payroll tax consequence to you. If you pay personally and the S-corp reimburses you under a valid accountable plan, the reimbursement is excluded from your W-2 and is not subject to payroll tax. If you pay personally and there is no accountable plan in place, the tax treatment is more complex -- consult your advisor before filing.
- **C-corps:** Business travel paid by the corporation is a corporate deduction. Owner-employees reimbursed under an accountable plan owe no income or payroll tax on the reimbursement.
- **Partners in a partnership:** A partner who pays qualifying partnership travel expenses personally may deduct those costs as unreimbursed partnership expenses (UPE) on Schedule E. The partnership agreement must support this treatment. Confirm the agreement language with your advisor before claiming UPE.

**Payroll implications:**

- Any travel reimbursement paid to an employee OUTSIDE a valid accountable plan is treated as additional wages. It is included in Box 1 of the employee's W-2 and subject to FICA withholding (Social Security and Medicare taxes). This creates both a tax cost and potential penalties. If your entity reimburses employees for travel without a written, compliant accountable plan, fix this immediately.

**State tax caveats:**

- Most states follow the federal rules on business travel deductibility, but not all. California, for example, has its own conformity rules that may differ from federal law. A handful of states have not yet conformed to OBBBA changes and may still allow employees to deduct unreimbursed business expenses on the state return even though the federal deduction is permanently eliminated.
- If you travel across state lines regularly, you may have income tax filing obligations in states where you conduct business. This is a facts-specific question -- raise it with your advisor if you travel to non-resident states for client meetings.

**OBBBA and employees in 2026:**

- For W-2 employees outside the narrow qualifying exceptions (Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, fee-basis state or local government officials), there is no federal deduction path for unreimbursed travel in 2026 and beyond. The permanent elimination applies to tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. If a client is a W-2 employee paying personal travel costs for a job, the correct planning response is to ask the employer to adopt an accountable plan -- not to claim a deduction that no longer exists.

**Companion travel:**

- You cannot deduct the travel costs of a spouse, family member, or other companion unless that person is your employee, has an independent business purpose for the trip, and actually performs substantial services -- not merely attending social events or being present. "My spouse came along" does not qualify. Companion costs are personal expenses.

**Conventions outside the North American area:**

- The "North American area" for tax purposes includes the United States, its possessions, Canada, Mexico, and certain other jurisdictions listed in the Internal Revenue Code. Conventions held outside this area are subject to additional deductibility tests: the meeting must be directly related to the active conduct of your trade or business, and it must be as reasonable to hold the meeting outside North America as within it. The IRS considers factors including the purpose of the meeting, the activities of the sponsoring organization, where the organization's members are located, and other relevant circumstances. These deductions are more scrutinized. Consult your advisor before attending and claiming a deduction for any convention outside the North American area.

**TCJA and QBI -- no direct effect here:**

- The business travel deduction is governed by IRC Section 162 and Section 274 and is not subject to the same phase-outs or sunset provisions as the qualified business income (QBI) deduction under IRC Section 199A. These are separate deductions.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Failing to establish the primary purpose before booking.** Many mixed-purpose trips are planned as vacations first, with business activities added later to try to make them deductible. The IRS looks at the overall character of the trip, and after-the-fact additions of business meetings rarely change a personal trip into a business trip. Plan business first; plan personal activities second.

2. **Counting any day with a business email or call as a business day.** Checking email or taking a quick call on a vacation day does not make it a business day. A business day requires that your principal activity during working hours is the pursuit of your trade or business, or that your presence at the location is required for a specific business purpose. Incidental contact with business during a personal day does not convert the day.

3. **Confusing sandwiched weekends with personal weekends.** A Saturday that falls between a Friday business day and a Monday business day counts as a business day -- it is a sandwiched non-work day. But a Saturday after your last scheduled business meeting, during which you stay for personal reasons, is a personal day. Apply the rules correctly day by day.

4. **Deducting transportation on a personal-primary domestic trip.** If personal days outnumber business days on a domestic trip, none of the transportation is deductible. This is one of the most common errors on mixed-trip returns and is easy to spot in an examination. The deduction will be disallowed in full.

5. **Failing to apply the international allocation when required.** On an international trip longer than seven days where personal time is 25% or more of total days outside the U.S., the transportation must be prorated. Deducting 100% of round-trip airfare without running the allocation formula is an error that will be flagged.

6. **Assuming the 7-day rule for international trips counts departure day.** It does not. The IRS instructs you to exclude the departure day from the count. A trip where you depart Monday and return Monday eight days later has seven days counted -- not eight. The rule applies if those seven counted days include mixed business and personal activity.

7. **Using a lodging per diem as a self-employed person.** The GSA per diem lodging rate cannot substitute for actual receipts if you are self-employed. You must have hotel folios or equivalent documentation for every night of lodging. Only the M&IE portion can be estimated using the standard daily rate (subject to the 50% meal limit).

8. **Forgetting to apply the 50% meal limit.** Every meal on a business day is subject to the 50% deduction limit. Meals on personal days are not deductible at all. Many owners deduct all trip meals at 100% and face disallowance on examination.

9. **Writing the travel log after the trip is over.** Reconstructed records are a significant weakness in any audit. The IRS explicitly treats contemporaneous records as more reliable. Write your log entry the same day or the following morning.

10. **Deducting companion travel without an independent business justification.** A spouse or family member who attends a conference but has no independent business purpose is a personal expense. The companion's airfare, lodging, and meals are not deductible.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before proceeding in any of the following situations:

- **Your tax home is unclear.** If you work in multiple locations, work from home without a confirmed qualifying home office, or have recently relocated, your tax home may not be what you assume. An incorrect tax home invalidates all travel deductions.
- **A domestic trip has an equal number of business and personal days.** The primary purpose test is not clearly met when the day count is tied. An equal split is treated as personal in the absence of other evidence -- get guidance before claiming transportation.
- **A domestic trip has more personal days than business days and you intend to deduct any portion.** The only deductible costs may be lodging and meals on actual business days. Confirm the approach and the documentation with your advisor before filing.
- **An international trip falls close to the 7-day or 25% thresholds.** The day count and the departure-day exclusion rule can shift which side of the line a trip lands on. Confirm the count is correct before filing.
- **An international trip requires transportation allocation (Test C).** The allocation formula must be applied correctly, and the business purpose for each business day must be fully documented. Have your advisor review the calculation.
- **You want to deduct a trip you have taken to an international convention.** The "as reasonable as holding in North America" test is fact-intensive and is a common audit area. Do not claim the deduction without advisor review of the specific facts.
- **You are an S-corp owner who paid business travel personally without an accountable plan.** The correct treatment of this situation is not straightforward. Discuss with your advisor before filing.
- **You are an employee and believe you qualify for one of the narrow exceptions (reservist, performing artist, fee-basis official).** The definitions are strict. Confirm your specific facts before filing Form 2106.
- **Any trip involves substantial personal days at a luxury or resort destination.** These trips attract heightened scrutiny. Confirm the documentation is airtight before claiming.
- **You have not yet confirmed your state's conformity to OBBBA or its treatment of employee travel expenses.** Do not assume state treatment mirrors federal treatment for 2026.

## Using The Download(s)

**Mixed-Purpose Travel Decision Framework Implementation Worksheet**

This worksheet is your primary documentation tool for every mixed-purpose trip. Use it as follows:

**Where it fits in the workflow:** Complete the worksheet during Phase 3 (After You Return), at Step 9. Fill it out within one week of returning from each trip. Do not wait until year-end to reconstruct the details.

**What the worksheet captures:** The worksheet walks you through the full decision framework for a single trip. It has sections for:

- **Trip identification:** Trip name or reference, destination, departure date, return date, entity/business name.
- **Purpose statement:** A one- to two-sentence description of the specific business purpose that required the trip. Write this in the box labeled "Business Reason for Trip" -- use concrete language (client name, conference name, meeting objective).
- **Day-by-day classification table:** One row per day of the trip. For each day, you enter the date, a brief description of the activity (business activity, travel, or personal activity), and a classification (B = business day, P = personal day). The table totals business days and personal days automatically if using the electronic version, or you total them manually.
- **Domestic primary purpose determination:** A section that displays the business-day count, the personal-day count, and the primary purpose conclusion (business-primary if business days exceed personal days; personal-primary otherwise).
- **International rules section (if applicable):** A section to enter total days outside the U.S. (excluding departure day), business days, personal days, and percentage of personal days. The worksheet applies the 7-day rule, the 25% rule, and the allocation formula automatically (electronic version) or provides the formula for manual completion.
- **Expense summary:** Fields for transportation cost, lodging cost (business days only), meal cost (business days, before 50% limit), meal deduction (50% of meal cost), other qualifying costs, and vehicle miles (if applicable).
- **Deductible totals by category:** The worksheet summarizes the deductible amount for each expense category based on the primary purpose conclusion and any required allocation.
- **Supporting record checklist:** A checkbox list confirming you have gathered the pre-trip business purpose memo, the daily travel log, receipts, and business purpose support documents.

**How to use it with your tax preparer:** At year-end, provide your tax preparer with: (1) the completed worksheets for all trips, (2) the underlying receipts and travel logs for each trip, and (3) your business purpose support documentation. The worksheets give your preparer a pre-organized summary that flows directly into the Schedule C lines (24a and 24b) or the equivalent entity return lines.

**The worksheet is a summary, not a substitute for underlying records.** If you are examined, the IRS will ask for the receipts and the daily log, not just the worksheet. Keep all underlying records with the completed worksheet in the same trip file.

## Completion Checklist

Work through this checklist for every mixed-purpose trip.

**Before you book:**

- [ ] I identified a specific, documentable business purpose for the trip that would justify travel on its own.
- [ ] I confirmed the destination is away from my tax home and the trip requires overnight sleep or rest.
- [ ] I confirmed the trip is not to a location where I have been working for more than one year.
- [ ] I planned the itinerary with business days placed first and personal days as a secondary extension.
- [ ] I wrote a pre-trip business purpose memo (to myself or in my calendar) before departure.

**During travel:**

- [ ] I wrote a contemporaneous log entry for each day, documenting the date, location, business activity, and persons met.
- [ ] I photographed every receipt (transportation, lodging, meals, incidentals) the same day I incurred the expense.
- [ ] I noted personal days honestly in my log.
- [ ] For any driving at the destination, I recorded odometer readings, destination, and business purpose for each business drive.
- [ ] For international travel, I tracked each day's classification (U.S. day or outside-U.S. day) and business vs. personal status.

**After you return:**

- [ ] I completed the Mixed-Purpose Travel Decision Framework Implementation Worksheet within one week of returning.
- [ ] I applied the correct primary purpose test (domestic count, or international 7-day rule / 25% rule / allocation formula).
- [ ] I totaled transportation, lodging (business days only), and meals (business days only, before the 50% reduction).
- [ ] I applied the 50% meal limit and noted the deductible meal amount.
- [ ] I calculated vehicle deduction if applicable: business miles x $0.725 (2026 rate) or actual expenses x business-use percentage.
- [ ] I identified the correct tax form lines for this deduction (Schedule C Lines 24a and 24b for sole proprietors; entity return for corporations; Schedule E for UPE; Form 2106 for qualifying employees).
- [ ] I stored all records -- the worksheet, the travel log, receipts, and business purpose documentation -- in a dated trip file.

**For international trips:**

- [ ] I confirmed total days outside the U.S. (not counting the departure day from the U.S.).
- [ ] I applied the 7-day rule first.
- [ ] If the trip was more than seven days, I calculated the personal-day percentage and applied the 25% rule.

## Before You Act

Before claiming any mixed-purpose travel deduction on a return, reimbursement request, or entity expense report, confirm the following with your advisor:

1. Your tax home is clearly established and the destination is away from it.
2. The trip satisfies the "away from home" test -- it required overnight sleep or rest.
3. You have applied the correct primary purpose test (domestic day-count rule, or the international 7-day rule, 25% rule, or allocation formula as applicable).
4. Business days outnumber personal days on any domestic trip for which you are deducting transportation, OR the applicable international exception applies.
5. Lodging and meals are limited to actual business days only, and the 50% meal limit has been applied.
6. For international trips with 25% or more personal time, you have applied the transportation allocation formula.
7. Your records are contemporaneous -- they were created during or immediately after travel, not reconstructed at tax time.
8. The completed worksheet and all underlying receipts, logs, and business purpose documents are organized and stored together.
9. Your entity structure supports the deduction path you are using.
10. Your state return treatment is confirmed -- your advisor has reviewed whether your state conforms to federal rules for 2026.

Do not file the return, submit the reimbursement, or claim the deduction until these facts are confirmed with your advisor.
