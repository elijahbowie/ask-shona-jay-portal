# Accountable Plan Monthly Reimbursement Kit

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

---

## Overview

An accountable plan is a written reimbursement policy your business uses to pay employees -- including owner-employees -- back for ordinary and necessary business expenses they paid with personal funds. When the plan meets three specific IRS tests, the reimbursement is completely excluded from the employee's wages: no income tax, no payroll tax (FICA -- Social Security and Medicare), no FUTA (federal unemployment tax), and no W-2 reporting. The business deducts the reimbursement as a business expense. Used consistently, an accountable plan can move hundreds or thousands of dollars per year out of the payroll-tax system and into the owner's pocket tax-free.

---

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is available to any business that has an employer-employee relationship and that operates under one of these structures:

- **S-corporation (S-corp):** The owner is both shareholder and W-2 employee. This is the most common setup for Beyond Freedom Financial clients and the one that produces the largest combined income-tax and payroll-tax savings.
- **C-corporation (C-corp):** The owner-employee relationship is straightforward; accountable plan reimbursements are excluded from wages under the same IRS rules.
- **Multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership with W-2 employees:** Any employee of the LLC can be reimbursed under an accountable plan. Note: partners and members who are not also employees have a different treatment (see Caveats below).
- **Any employer with W-2 employees:** The plan works for employees at any compensation level, not just owners.

You must already be running payroll and paying yourself (or your employees) wages on a W-2 before an accountable plan can benefit you. The reimbursement is separate from -- and does not replace -- wages.

---

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Sole proprietors (Schedule C filers):** A sole proprietor cannot have an employer-employee relationship with themselves. You deduct business expenses directly on Schedule C; no accountable plan is needed or possible.
- **Single-member LLC taxed as a disregarded entity (not elected S-corp status):** Same as sole proprietor -- no employer-employee relationship exists within the entity, so the plan cannot apply to the owner. Employees of the LLC (if any) can be covered.
- **Partners in a partnership:** Partners are not employees of their own partnership. Reimbursements to partners generally flow through guaranteed payments (reported on Schedule K-1), not under an accountable plan. Consult your advisor for the correct treatment.
- **Situations where the owner takes no salary:** An accountable plan reimbursement cannot substitute for reasonable compensation. If the IRS determines the owner is avoiding payroll taxes by replacing salary with reimbursements, the reimbursements can be reclassified as wages with back payroll taxes and penalties owed.
- **Expenses that are personal in nature:** The plan cannot reimburse clothing, personal meals, commuting costs, or any expense that lacks a genuine business purpose. Doing so converts the entire arrangement to a nonaccountable plan (all payments become taxable wages).
- **States with different treatment:** Some states do not follow federal accountable plan rules. California, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania, for example, have additional or differing rules. Verify with your advisor.

## Key Terms

**Accountable plan:** A written employer reimbursement arrangement that meets three IRS tests -- business connection, substantiation, and return of excess -- causing reimbursements to be excluded from the employee's wages. Defined under Treasury Regulation 1.62-2 and IRC Section 62(a)(2)(A).

**Nonaccountable plan:** Any reimbursement arrangement that fails one or more of the three IRS tests. Amounts paid under a nonaccountable plan are treated as taxable wages, subject to income tax withholding and payroll taxes (FICA, FUTA), and must be reported on Form W-2.

**Business connection:** The requirement that an expense must be an ordinary and necessary business expense, paid or incurred while performing services as an employee. "Ordinary" means common and accepted in your industry; "necessary" means helpful and appropriate for your business.

**Substantiation:** The requirement that the employee provide adequate records proving the amount, time, place, and business purpose of each expense within a reasonable time.

**Return of excess:** The requirement that the employee return any amount reimbursed that exceeds the substantiated expense within a reasonable time. If an excess is kept, that specific amount becomes taxable wages.

**FICA:** Federal Insurance Contributions Act taxes -- the combined 7.65% employee share and 7.65% employer share of Social Security (6.2% each, up to the wage base) and Medicare (1.45% each) taxes withheld from wages. Proper accountable plan reimbursements are exempt from both halves.

**FUTA:** Federal Unemployment Tax Act tax, paid by employers only (0.6% after credit, on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages). Accountable plan reimbursements are exempt.

**W-2:** The Wage and Tax Statement filed by the employer and given to each employee showing wages, tips, and taxes withheld. Accountable plan reimbursements are not reported on W-2 as wages.

**IRC Section 62(a)(2)(A):** The Internal Revenue Code section that allows employer reimbursements meeting the accountable plan rules to be excluded from gross income.

**Treasury Regulation 1.62-2:** The IRS regulation that spells out, in detail, the three requirements an arrangement must meet to qualify as an accountable plan and the timing safe harbors.

**Safe harbor:** An IRS-specified method that, if followed, automatically satisfies a requirement. The timing windows (60 days to substantiate, 120 days to return excess) are safe harbors under Reg. 1.62-2(g).

**Mixed-use expense:** An expense that has both personal and business components (for example, your monthly cell phone bill). Only the business-use percentage can be reimbursed. The percentage must be reasonable and documented.

**Home office business percentage:** The square footage of your dedicated home office space divided by the total square footage of your home. This percentage is applied to allocable home costs (rent or mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, repairs) to determine the reimbursable amount.

**Documentary evidence:** Receipts, paid bills, canceled checks, credit card statements, or similar records that prove an expense was incurred. Required for all lodging and for any other expense of $75 or more. Below $75 (except lodging), the IRS does not require a receipt but you should still keep a written log of the expense.

**Per diem:** A fixed daily allowance paid instead of reimbursing actual meal and incidental expenses. The IRS accepts per diem reimbursements up to the applicable federal rate as substantiated without requiring individual receipts.

**Standard mileage rate:** The IRS-set rate per mile for business driving, currently 72.5 cents per mile for 2026 (IRS Notice 2026-10). Reimbursements at or below this rate are automatically within the accountable plan safe harbor and non-taxable.

**Contemporaneous mileage log:** A record of business trips kept as the trips occur (not reconstructed later), including date, starting and ending location, destination, business purpose, and miles driven.

**OBBBA:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, which permanently eliminated miscellaneous itemized deductions (including unreimbursed employee business expenses) for most W-2 employees starting in 2026. This law makes accountable plans more important than ever: without a plan, owner-employees cannot deduct any of these costs.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before setting up or optimizing your accountable plan:

1. **Entity type and tax election:** Confirm whether you operate as an S-corp, C-corp, multi-member LLC taxed as S-corp, or other structure. Your advisor needs this to confirm the plan is available to you.
2. **Payroll confirmation:** Confirm you are running W-2 payroll and paying yourself a salary. The plan requires an employer-employee relationship.
3. **Home office details (if applicable):**
   - Total square footage of your home
   - Square footage of the dedicated office space (must be used regularly and exclusively for business)
4. **Vehicle information (if claiming mileage):**
   - Make, model, and year of personal vehicle used for business
   - Odometer reading at the start of each year
5. **Estimated monthly expenses by category:** List the recurring costs you incur personally that relate to business (examples: cell phone bill, internet bill, home utilities, home insurance, home mortgage interest or rent).
6. **Existing company policy:** Confirm whether a written accountable plan policy currently exists, and if so, retrieve a copy.
7. **Corporate records access:** For corporations, confirm whether your board (even if that is just you) has passed a resolution adopting the plan. If not, this must be done.

## Required Documents And Records

### Documents to establish and maintain the plan

| Document | What it is | When to create it |
|---|---|---|
| Written accountable plan policy | A signed document stating which expenses qualify, substantiation requirements, and return-of-excess rules | Before making any reimbursements; keep permanently |
| Corporate board resolution (corporations only) | Minutes or a signed resolution from the board of directors adopting the plan | At plan adoption; keep permanently |

### Records to collect and keep for each reimbursement

| Record type | Required for | Retention |
|---|---|---|
| Receipt or invoice | Any single expense of $75 or more; all lodging regardless of amount | Minimum 3 years from the return due date; 4 years for employment tax records |
| Credit card or bank statement | Can supplement or replace receipts for expenses under $75 | Minimum 3 years |
| Contemporaneous mileage log | Every business vehicle trip (date, start/end location, destination, business purpose, miles driven, odometer) | Minimum 3 years |
| Home office calculation worksheet | Annual square-footage calculation and expense allocation | Minimum 3 years; keep until property is sold if claiming depreciation |
| Completed reimbursement form | Every reimbursement request submitted (see "Using The Download(s)" section) | Minimum 3 years |
| Proof of payment (bank statement or canceled check) | Every reimbursement payment the business made to the employee | Minimum 4 years (employment tax records) |

**Retention guidance:** The IRS statute of limitations for assessing additional tax is generally 3 years from the filing date (IRC Section 6501(a)). It extends to 6 years if more than 25% of gross income was omitted, and there is no limit if a return is fraudulent. Employment tax records (payroll) must be kept at least 4 years. Keep all accountable plan records for a minimum of 4 years from the filing date of the return to cover both the general and employment tax statutes.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Set Up the Plan (one-time)

**Step 1 -- Draft and sign a written accountable plan policy.**
Create a document (your advisor can provide a template) that states:
- Which employees are covered
- What expense categories qualify
- The maximum advance window (no more than 30 days before the expense)
- The substantiation deadline (no more than 60 days after the expense is paid or incurred)
- The return-of-excess deadline (no more than 120 days after the expense is paid or incurred)
- The documentation requirements

Sign and date the document. This document is your proof the plan exists.

**Step 2 -- Pass a corporate resolution (S-corps and C-corps).**
If you are a corporation, hold a formal (even if brief, one-person) board of directors meeting, record it in your corporate minutes, and adopt a resolution stating the corporation has formally approved the accountable plan. Date and sign the minutes. Keep them in your corporate records book permanently.

**Step 3 -- Calculate your home office percentage (if applicable).**
Measure the square footage of the room you use regularly and exclusively for business. Divide that number by your home's total finished square footage. Round to two decimal places.

Example: 180 sq ft office in a 1,800 sq ft home = 10.0% business-use percentage.

This percentage applies to your allocable home costs each month.

**Step 4 -- Determine your recurring monthly reimbursable amounts.**
For each mixed-use expense category, calculate the monthly reimbursable portion:

- **Home office costs:** Apply your business-use percentage to monthly rent or the monthly interest portion of your mortgage payment, plus utilities, insurance, and repairs paid that month.
- **Cell phone:** Apply a reasonable business-use percentage (document how you determined it, for example, "I reviewed one month of call logs and estimated 60% of calls and data were business-related").
- **Internet:** Apply a reasonable business-use percentage.

Write down these estimates; they will feed into your monthly form.

**Step 5 -- Set up a vehicle mileage log.**
Start a mileage log (a small notebook or a dedicated app) on January 1 (or the date you adopt the plan). Record every business trip as it occurs.

---

### Phase 2: Monthly Reimbursement Cycle

Run this cycle every month, ideally within the first five business days of the following month.

**Step 6 -- Collect expense documentation for the month just ended.**
Pull together:
- Receipts or statements for all business expenses of $75 or more
- Your vehicle mileage log entries for the month
- Your home office cost figures for the month (utility bills, rent/mortgage statement)
- Cell phone and internet bills
- Any other business expenses paid personally

**Step 7 -- Complete the Accountable Plan Reimbursement Form (the attached download).**
Fill in each section of the form (see "Using The Download(s)" for field-by-field guidance). Calculate:
- Mileage reimbursement: total business miles for the month multiplied by 72.5 cents (the 2026 IRS rate, per IRS Notice 2026-10).
- Home office reimbursement: your home office percentage applied to each allocable cost.
- Other expense reimbursements: 100% of pure business expenses; your documented business-use percentage for mixed-use expenses.

Sign and date the form.

**Step 8 -- Attach all supporting documentation.**
Staple or attach (or upload to your digital records folder) every receipt, statement, and log page that supports the amounts on the form.

**Step 9 -- Submit the form to the business.**
As the owner-employee, you are submitting the form to your business entity. If you have other employees, each employee submits their own form. Keep a copy for your personal records.

**Step 10 -- Issue the reimbursement check or electronic transfer from the business account.**
Write a business check (or initiate an ACH transfer from the business bank account) to the employee for the exact substantiated amount on the form. Record it in your accounting software as a "reimbursement expense" -- not wages, not a distribution.

The reimbursement must be paid out of a business account (not the personal account). This establishes the business connection in your bank records.

**Step 11 -- File the completed form and backup in your records.**
Store the signed form plus all attachments for at least 4 years from the return due date. Use a physical folder labeled by month and year, or an organized digital folder (cloud storage works).

**Step 12 -- Confirm no excess was advanced.**
If you took any advance earlier in the month, confirm the advance did not exceed the substantiated amount. If there is any excess, return it to the business account within 120 days of the original expense date.

---

### Phase 3: Annual Tasks

**Step 13 -- Reconcile total reimbursements at year-end.**
At December 31, add up all monthly reimbursements made during the year. Confirm each one was paid from the business account and is recorded in your bookkeeping as a business expense (not compensation).

**Step 14 -- Confirm W-2 does not include reimbursements.**
When your payroll provider prepares your W-2, verify that accountable plan reimbursements are NOT included in Box 1 (wages). They should not appear on the W-2 at all. If your payroll provider accidentally included them, it must be corrected before you file.

**Step 15 -- Deduct on the corporate return.**
Your accountant will deduct the total reimbursements as an ordinary and necessary business expense on the corporate tax return (Form 1120S for S-corps, Form 1120 for C-corps). These expenses reduce the business's taxable income.

**Step 16 -- Reset the mileage log.**
On January 1, record the vehicle's odometer reading. This documents your starting mileage for the new year.

## Timing And Deadlines

| Requirement | Safe harbor window | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Advance to employee | No more than 30 days before the expense is paid or incurred | Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g) |
| Submit expense report and receipts (substantiation) | No more than 60 days after the expense is paid or incurred | Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g) |
| Return any excess advance | No more than 120 days after the expense is paid or incurred | Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g) |
| Alternative: periodic statement method | Employer issues quarterly statements; employee has 120 days from each statement to substantiate or return excess | Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g)(2) |

**Recommended cadence for most clients:** Submit your completed reimbursement form and backup documentation by the 5th of the following month (for example, the May form is submitted by June 5). This keeps you well inside the 60-day substantiation window for expenses incurred throughout May.

**Year-end deadline:** All December expenses must be substantiated by February 28 of the following year (60 days from December 31) and all excess returned by April 30 (120 days).

**Corporate resolution timing:** Adopt the plan before the first reimbursement is made. Retroactive plans are not recognized by the IRS.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

### Entity caveats

**S-corp owner-employees:** You are legally required to pay yourself reasonable compensation (wages comparable to what you would pay an arm's-length employee for the same work) before taking profit distributions. Accountable plan reimbursements are in addition to -- not a replacement for -- reasonable compensation. The IRS can reclassify distributions as wages if compensation is unreasonably low, and can recharacterize reimbursements as wages if the plan lacks genuine substantiation.

**S-corp greater-than-2% shareholders and health insurance:** If you own more than 2% of an S-corp, employer-paid health insurance premiums for you must be included in your W-2 Box 1 wages (but are excluded from Social Security and Medicare boxes). This is not part of the accountable plan. Reimbursing individual market premiums through an employer payment plan can trigger a $100-per-day-per-employee excise tax under IRC Section 4980D. Health benefit arrangements for greater-than-2% S-corp shareholders require separate, specialized planning. See your advisor before acting.

**C-corporation:** The accountable plan rules apply in full to C-corp owner-employees. Reimbursements are deducted on Form 1120. Unlike S-corp distributions, they do not pass through to the shareholder's personal return.

**Partnership members:** Partners are not employees of their partnership and cannot use an accountable plan for their own expenses. Expenses are addressed through guaranteed payments or reflected in the partnership return. However, non-partner employees of the partnership can use an accountable plan.

**Multi-member LLC taxed as partnership:** Same as partnership rules above for the members themselves. LLC employees can be covered.

**Single-member LLC (disregarded entity):** The owner is treated as a sole proprietor for federal tax purposes. No employer-employee relationship exists with yourself. Deduct business expenses directly on Schedule C or (if the LLC is owned by an S-corp) through the S-corp's accountable plan.

### Payroll caveats

- You must have active W-2 payroll in place before any reimbursement under the plan. If payroll lapses or you go off payroll, accountable plan reimbursements during that period may lose their tax-free status.
- Do not record reimbursements as owner draws or distributions. Record them as business expenses in your bookkeeping software.
- Your payroll provider must NOT include reimbursements in wages on Form W-2. If they do, it must be corrected with a Form W-2c.

### State caveats

- Most states follow federal accountable plan rules, but not all. Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor. States with notable differences include California, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania.
- Some states have their own mileage rates or require additional documentation. Check your state DOR website before using only federal standards.

### Current-year caveats (2026)

- The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed July 4, 2025, permanently eliminated the deduction for unreimbursed employee business expenses (previously a miscellaneous itemized deduction subject to the 2% AGI floor under TCJA) for most W-2 employees starting in 2026. Without an accountable plan, an employee who pays a business expense personally has NO tax deduction for it. This makes the accountable plan the only available mechanism for W-2 employees to capture these deductions.
- Limited exceptions to the OBBBA rule exist for eligible educators, Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, and fee-basis state officials. These are narrow categories; most business owners do not qualify.
- The 2026 standard mileage rate increased 2.5 cents from 2025 (from 70 cents to 72.5 cents). Update any fixed reimbursement rate schedules in your plan document if you adopted the plan in a prior year.

## Common Mistakes

**1. No written plan document.**
A verbal policy or informal practice does not qualify. The IRS and Tax Court have repeatedly disallowed reimbursements where the employer could not produce a signed written plan. Create the document before the first reimbursement.

**2. No corporate resolution.**
For corporations, the board (even a sole director) must formally adopt the plan by resolution. Absence of minutes or a resolution can cause the IRS to treat all reimbursements as wages.

**3. Submitting expense reports late.**
If more than 60 days pass between an expense and substantiation, that expense's reimbursement is reclassified as wages. Set a firm monthly deadline and stick to it.

**4. Missing receipts for amounts of $75 or more.**
No receipt, no exclusion. The $75 receipt threshold applies to individual transactions, not categories or totals. A $100 dinner with only a credit card statement but no itemized receipt fails the test.

**5. Claiming 100% of mixed-use costs.**
You cannot reimburse 100% of your cell phone bill or home costs if you use them personally. Only the documented business-use percentage is reimbursable. Overclaiming converts the excess to taxable wages and can invalidate the plan.

**6. Forgetting to return excess.**
If you received any advance or reimbursement for an expense that turned out to be less than the reimbursed amount, return the excess to the business account within 120 days. Unreturned excess becomes taxable wages.

**7. Replacing salary with reimbursements.**
Using the plan to minimize payroll by substituting reimbursements for what should be wages is an IRS red flag. Maintain reasonable compensation on your W-2 and treat the plan as supplemental.

**8. Reimbursing personal expenses.**
Any reimbursement for a personal expense (clothing, personal meals, commuting) that is included under the plan -- even inadvertently -- can cause the IRS to recharacterize the entire plan as nonaccountable for that period.

**9. Mileage log reconstructed after the fact.**
A mileage log created from memory months later is not "contemporaneous." Courts have rejected reconstructed logs. Log each trip on the day it occurs.

**10. Paying reimbursements from the personal account.**
The reimbursement check or transfer must come from a business bank account. Payments from your personal account are indistinguishable from personal transactions and lack the employer-employee paper trail required.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before acting if any of the following apply:

- [ ] You have never had a written accountable plan document and are setting one up for the first time. Your advisor must review the plan document and confirm it meets the legal requirements before you use it.
- [ ] You are an S-corp owner and are unsure whether your current W-2 salary is reasonable compensation. Reimbursements on top of an unreasonably low salary can be reclassified by the IRS.
- [ ] You own more than 2% of an S-corp and are considering reimbursing health insurance or setting up any health benefit arrangement. This area carries excise tax risk that requires separate analysis.
- [ ] You operate in California, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, or any other state that may differ from federal rules. Confirm state treatment before assuming federal rules apply in full.
- [ ] You are a partner or LLC member and want to use an accountable plan for your own expenses. The rules are different for non-employee owners.
- [ ] You want to reimburse home office costs that involve depreciation. Depreciation adds complexity, and recapture may apply when you sell the property.
- [ ] Your reimbursement amounts have increased significantly year over year without a corresponding change in business activity. This pattern can trigger scrutiny.
- [ ] You intend to use per diem rates for travel rather than actual expenses. Confirm which method -- federal rate, high-low, or actual -- is most appropriate for your situation.
- [ ] You are adopting the plan mid-year. Confirm with your advisor whether and how the plan applies to expenses already incurred before adoption.

## Using The Download(s)

### Accountable Plan Reimbursement Form

This is the monthly submission form the employee (including owner-employees) completes and signs to request reimbursement from the business.

**Where it fits in the workflow:** Use it in Step 7 of the monthly cycle (above), after you have collected all receipts and computed your reimbursable amounts but before you cut the reimbursement check.

**How to complete the form:**

| Section | What to enter |
|---|---|
| Employee name | Your legal name as it appears on your W-2 |
| Business name | The legal name of your corporation or entity |
| Reimbursement period | Month and year covered (for example, "May 2026") |
| Submission date | The date you are completing the form (must be within 60 days of the last expense date for that month) |
| Mileage section | Enter total business miles for the period; multiply by 72.5 cents (the 2026 rate); enter the reimbursable amount |
| Home office section | Enter your home office percentage; list each allocable expense (rent/mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, repairs); enter the reimbursable portion for each |
| Cell phone section | Enter the monthly bill amount; enter your business-use percentage; enter the reimbursable amount |
| Internet section | Same format as cell phone |
| Other expenses | List each expense individually: date, vendor, amount, business purpose |
| Total reimbursement | Sum of all line items |
| Employee signature | Sign and date; your signature certifies the expenses were business-related and that you have attached all required documentation |
| Attachments checklist | Check off each category of supporting documentation attached (receipts, mileage log pages, utility bills, etc.) |

**After completing:** Attach all supporting documentation, submit to the business (even if that means handing it to yourself as the owner), retain one copy for your personal files, and issue the reimbursement from the business account.

**Digital vs. paper:** Either format is acceptable. If you use digital forms, ensure each completed form is saved as a locked PDF or in a system that preserves the original submission date.
