# Roth Conversion and Backdoor Roth Review

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

A Roth conversion moves money from a pre-tax retirement account (such as a traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, or 401(k)) into a Roth IRA, triggering ordinary income tax now in exchange for tax-free growth and tax-free withdrawals forever after. The Backdoor Roth is a two-step version of this strategy designed for high-income earners who earn too much to contribute directly to a Roth IRA: you contribute after-tax dollars to a traditional IRA, then immediately convert that account to a Roth IRA. Done correctly, the conversion itself is tax-free (or nearly so). Both strategies aim to move wealth into an account that will never be taxed again on growth or qualified withdrawals.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook applies to you if one or more of the following are true:

- Your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI -- defined below) exceeds the Roth IRA direct contribution phase-out threshold for 2026 ($153,000 for single filers, $242,000 for married filing jointly), making the Backdoor Roth your only path to Roth contributions.
- You have traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, or 401(k) balances that you expect to be taxed at a lower marginal rate today than in retirement, making a partial or full conversion strategically attractive.
- You are in a temporary low-income year (business loss year, gap between jobs, early retirement before Social Security begins) and want to fill lower tax brackets with Roth conversions at a favorable rate.
- You want to reduce future Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs -- defined below) by converting pre-tax retirement balances before you reach the RMD starting age.
- You are doing pre-estate-planning transfers and want to leave heirs tax-free Roth assets rather than taxable pre-tax accounts.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not proceed with Roth conversions or the Backdoor Roth without your advisor's review if any of the following apply:

- **You need the converted dollars within five years and are under age 59.5.** Converted principal that is withdrawn within five years of the conversion is subject to a 10% early withdrawal penalty if you are under age 59.5 (see "5-Year Conversion Clock" below).
- **The conversion would push your income into a higher Medicare IRMAA surcharge bracket.** IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount -- a Medicare Part B and Part D surcharge based on your income from two years prior) kicks in for single filers at $109,000 MAGI and for married filers at $218,000 MAGI. A large conversion can add hundreds of dollars per month to Medicare premiums two years later.
- **You will owe state income tax on the conversion and that state has a high marginal rate.** Some states tax Roth conversions as ordinary income and have no special retirement income exclusion. This can substantially raise the effective cost of converting.
- **You carry other pre-tax IRA balances and have not accounted for the pro-rata rule.** If you have any existing pre-tax funds in traditional IRAs, SEP-IRAs, or SIMPLE IRAs, the pro-rata rule (defined below) will make part of your Backdoor Roth conversion taxable. This does not make the strategy wrong, but it changes the math.
- **Your SIMPLE IRA was opened less than two years ago.** Converting a SIMPLE IRA (a type of employer-sponsored IRA for small businesses) to a Roth IRA within the first two years of participation may trigger a 25% excise tax on the distribution rather than the standard 10%. Confirm the two-year anniversary date with your advisor before converting.
- **You cannot pay the income tax on the conversion from outside funds.** If you plan to withhold tax from the converted amount itself, you lose that principal permanently (and if you are under age 59.5, the withheld amount is also subject to the 10% penalty). Conversions are best executed when you can pay the resulting tax bill from non-IRA savings.
- **You are currently in the 32%, 35%, or 37% marginal bracket with no expectation of a lower rate in future years.** Converting at the top rates can cost more than the long-run benefit delivers, depending on your time horizon and projected withdrawal needs.

## Key Terms

**Traditional IRA (Individual Retirement Arrangement):** A retirement savings account where contributions may be tax-deductible (depending on income and workplace plan coverage), and growth is tax-deferred. You pay ordinary income tax when you withdraw the money in retirement.

**Roth IRA:** A retirement savings account funded with after-tax dollars. Growth and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. There are no Required Minimum Distributions during your lifetime.

**Roth Conversion:** The act of moving money from a traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, or eligible employer plan into a Roth IRA. The amount converted is added to your ordinary taxable income in the year of the conversion.

**Backdoor Roth IRA:** A two-step process: (1) make a nondeductible (after-tax) contribution to a traditional IRA, then (2) convert that traditional IRA to a Roth IRA. Used by high-income earners who cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA due to income limits. The strategy relies on the fact that there is no income limit on IRA-to-Roth conversions (that limit was permanently repealed for tax year 2010 and later under the Tax Increase Prevention and Reconciliation Act of 2005).

**MAGI (Modified Adjusted Gross Income):** For Roth IRA contribution purposes, MAGI is your Adjusted Gross Income from Form 1040 Line 11, with certain deductions added back (such as IRA deductions, student loan interest, and passive activity losses). Note: the taxable portion of a Roth conversion is excluded from MAGI when calculating your eligibility to make direct Roth contributions, per IRC Section 408A(c)(3)(B)(i). However, Roth conversion income does count toward MAGI for IRMAA, net investment income tax, and ACA premium tax credit calculations.

**Nondeductible Contribution:** A contribution made to a traditional IRA that you cannot deduct on your tax return (either because your income is too high or you are covered by a workplace plan). These after-tax dollars create "basis" in your IRA, meaning you will not pay tax on them again when you withdraw them.

**Basis:** The after-tax money inside your traditional IRA. Because you already paid tax on nondeductible contributions, the IRS does not tax that portion again when it is distributed or converted. You track basis using Form 8606.

**Pro-Rata Rule:** An IRS rule under IRC Section 408(d)(2) that treats all of your traditional IRAs, SEP-IRAs, and SIMPLE IRAs as one combined account for tax purposes. When you convert or withdraw money, the taxable and non-taxable portions are calculated proportionally based on the ratio of pre-tax to after-tax money across all those accounts -- not just the account you happen to be converting. Workplace plans (401(k), 403(b)) are excluded from the calculation. Example: if you have $90,000 of pre-tax IRA funds and $10,000 of after-tax (nondeductible) basis across all IRAs, 10% of any conversion or withdrawal is tax-free and 90% is taxable, regardless of which account the money physically came from.

**Aggregation Rule:** The same as the pro-rata rule -- the IRS aggregates (combines) all non-Roth, non-workplace IRAs for the purpose of calculating the taxable fraction of any distribution or conversion. The December 31 balance of the conversion year is what matters, not the balance on the conversion date.

**Form 8606:** The IRS form used to report: (a) nondeductible contributions to traditional IRAs, (b) conversions from traditional/SEP/SIMPLE IRAs to Roth IRAs, and (c) distributions from IRAs where you have after-tax basis. This form creates the paper trail that proves your basis and prevents you from being taxed twice. Filing it is required by law; failure carries a $50 penalty per missed year.

**5-Year Contribution Clock:** One of two separate five-year rules for Roth IRAs. This clock starts on January 1 of the first tax year for which you made any Roth IRA contribution (not a conversion). Once five tax years have passed and you are age 59.5 or older, all Roth IRA earnings can be withdrawn tax-free and penalty-free (a "qualified distribution").

**5-Year Conversion Clock:** The second five-year rule, applicable only to converted amounts. Each Roth conversion starts its own five-year clock on January 1 of the year the conversion occurs. If you withdraw converted principal before that five-year clock expires and you are under age 59.5, you owe a 10% early withdrawal penalty on the taxable (pre-tax) portion of the conversion. The clock resets to zero with each new conversion. Once you are age 59.5, the 10% penalty does not apply regardless of how long the funds have been in the Roth account.

**Recharacterization:** The act of reversing a contribution from one type of IRA to another (for example, switching a Roth contribution to a traditional IRA contribution). Important: recharacterization of a Roth conversion is permanently prohibited for conversions made after December 31, 2017 (TCJA, effective via IRC Section 408A(d)(6)(B)(iii)). You cannot undo a 2026 Roth conversion. Recharacterization of a regular annual contribution (not a conversion) is still allowed by the tax return due date including extensions.

**RMD (Required Minimum Distribution):** The minimum amount the IRS requires you to withdraw each year from traditional IRAs, SEP-IRAs, SIMPLE IRAs, and most employer plans once you reach the RMD starting age. For people born from 1951 through 1959, the RMD starting age is 73. For people born in 1960 or later, the starting age rises to 75, effective in 2033. Roth IRAs are permanently exempt from RMDs during the original owner's lifetime.

**IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount):** A Medicare surcharge added to your Part B and Part D premiums when your MAGI exceeds certain thresholds. Medicare looks at your MAGI from two years prior (so your 2026 income affects 2028 premiums). For 2026, the IRMAA surcharge begins at $109,000 MAGI for single filers and $218,000 for married filing jointly (based on 2024 income). A large Roth conversion in 2026 affects 2028 Medicare premiums, not 2026.

**SEP-IRA (Simplified Employee Pension IRA):** An employer-funded IRA often used by self-employed individuals and small business owners. SEP-IRA balances are pre-tax and subject to the pro-rata rule for Backdoor Roth purposes.

**SIMPLE IRA (Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees IRA):** An employer-sponsored IRA plan for small businesses. Subject to the pro-rata rule. Subject to the two-year rule: distributions within the first two years of participation may carry a 25% penalty instead of the standard 10%.

**Step Transaction Doctrine:** A tax law principle that allows the IRS to treat a series of individually lawful steps as a single transaction if they were undertaken as part of a pre-planned sequence to achieve an end result that would otherwise be restricted. Some practitioners express concern this could apply to Backdoor Roth conversions; however, the IRS has never invoked this doctrine against a Backdoor Roth conversion, and the strategy is widely accepted as legitimate.

**Mega Backdoor Roth:** A separate, more advanced strategy available only through certain employer 401(k) plans that permit after-tax (non-Roth) employee contributions and either in-plan Roth conversions or in-service withdrawals. Under IRS Notice 2014-54, these after-tax 401(k) contributions can be rolled directly to a Roth IRA, bypassing the $7,500 IRA contribution cap entirely. The maximum after-tax contribution room in 2026 equals the IRC Section 415(c) annual additions limit of $72,000 minus the employee's regular deferrals and employer contributions. The Mega Backdoor Roth is covered in this playbook as an advanced variation but requires a separate plan-document review by your advisor.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before starting any conversion or Backdoor Roth transaction:

1. **All IRA and employer plan year-end account balances (December 31, 2025):** You need the total pre-tax and after-tax balances across every traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, and SIMPLE IRA you own. This drives the pro-rata calculation.
2. **Existing IRA basis (cumulative nondeductible contributions):** Pull all previously filed Forms 8606. The cumulative basis shown on Line 14 of your most recently filed Form 8606 is your starting point.
3. **Projected 2026 taxable income:** An estimate of wages, business income, capital gains, and other income before any conversion. This determines which tax bracket you are in and how much conversion space you have before crossing into the next bracket or an IRMAA threshold.
4. **Roth IRA account details:** Whether you already have an open Roth IRA, and if so, what year it was first opened and funded (this determines your 5-year contribution clock start date).
5. **Age and anticipated cash needs within five years:** Whether you are under age 59.5, and whether you might need access to the converted dollars before the five-year conversion clock expires.
6. **Medicare status or eligibility timeline:** If you are age 63 or older (because IRMAA is based on income from two years earlier), confirm whether a conversion in 2026 would push your 2028 Medicare premiums into a higher surcharge tier.
7. **State of residence:** Determine whether your state taxes Roth conversions as ordinary income. Some states offer retirement income exclusions that may shelter conversion income; others do not.
8. **Employer 401(k) plan documents (for Mega Backdoor Roth only):** Confirm whether your plan allows after-tax (non-Roth) employee contributions and in-plan Roth conversions or in-service distributions.

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect before transacting:**
- Most recent year-end statements for all traditional IRAs, SEP-IRAs, SIMPLE IRAs, and employer plans showing account balances
- All previously filed Forms 8606 (every year you made a nondeductible contribution or took a distribution with basis)
- Prior-year federal tax return (to confirm MAGI and basis carried forward)

**Create and retain from the transaction year:**
- IRA custodian confirmation of the nondeductible contribution (for Backdoor Roth): the written acknowledgment of your contribution, the date, and the tax year designated
- Conversion confirmation statement from the IRA custodian showing the amount converted and date
- Form 1099-R: The custodian will issue this in January of the following year reporting the distribution from the traditional IRA. Box 7 distribution code will typically be "2" (early, exception applies) or "7" (normal, age 59.5+). Do not discard this form.
- Completed Form 8606 for the conversion year: Parts I and II must both be completed for a Backdoor Roth (Part I reports the nondeductible contribution; Part II reports the conversion)
- Bank or brokerage records showing the source of funds used to pay the income tax bill (to demonstrate tax was paid from outside funds, not from the conversion itself)

**Retention period:** Keep all IRA records, Forms 8606, and 1099-Rs permanently (or at minimum, until all Roth accounts are fully distributed and all related tax returns are closed). The IRS has no statute of limitations on basis tracking if Form 8606 was never filed.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Path A: Standard Roth Conversion (converting existing pre-tax IRA or 401(k) funds)

**Step 1 -- Determine your conversion budget.**
Using your projected 2026 taxable income, calculate how much you can convert before crossing into the next tax bracket, an IRMAA threshold, or another income-sensitive trigger. Use the 2026 tax brackets in "The Numbers" section below. Example: if you are married filing jointly with $150,000 in taxable income, the top of the 22% bracket is $211,400 (after your standard deduction of $32,200 is already applied), so you have approximately $61,400 of conversion room at the 22% rate. Bring this calculation to your advisor for sign-off before executing.

**Step 2 -- Verify there is no outstanding SIMPLE IRA two-year restriction.**
If any account you plan to convert is a SIMPLE IRA, confirm that at least two full years have elapsed since the date of your first contribution to that plan. If not, do not convert until the two-year period is complete, or accept that the distribution may carry a 25% excise tax.

**Step 3 -- Contact your IRA or plan custodian.**
Call or log in to your custodian's website and request a Roth conversion. You will need to specify: (a) the dollar amount to convert, (b) the source account, (c) the destination Roth IRA account number (open one first if you do not already have a Roth IRA), and (d) your tax withholding election. Choose zero withholding and plan to pay the tax bill from outside funds. Confirm that the conversion will be processed in calendar year 2026 (by December 31, 2026).

**Step 4 -- Receive and review the conversion confirmation.**
The custodian will provide a confirmation showing the conversion amount and date. Save this document.

**Step 5 -- Set aside money to pay the tax bill.**
Calculate the estimated additional federal and state income tax owed on the converted amount. Set that money aside in a separate account. Make an estimated tax payment using IRS Form 1040-ES if the conversion will cause you to owe more than $1,000 in additional federal tax and your total withholding and prior estimated payments are insufficient, to avoid an underpayment penalty. Consult "Timing And Deadlines" below for due dates.

**Step 6 -- File Form 8606 with your 2026 tax return.**
Complete Form 8606 Part II to report the conversion. If no nondeductible contributions were ever made, the entire converted amount is taxable. If you have prior nondeductible contributions (basis), Part I and Part II must both be completed, and the taxable fraction is determined by the pro-rata formula using your December 31, 2026 IRA balances.

### Path B: Backdoor Roth IRA (for high-income earners)

**Step 1 -- Open a traditional IRA if you do not already have one.**
Contact any brokerage or bank. Opening takes 10 to 30 minutes online. You do not need to hold this account long-term; it is a conduit.

**Step 2 -- Check your existing IRA balances for the pro-rata rule.**
Log in to every traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, and SIMPLE IRA you own. If the total pre-tax balance across all those accounts (not counting your Roth IRA) is zero, you will have a "clean" Backdoor Roth with no taxable income on conversion. If there are any pre-tax balances, calculate the taxable fraction using the pro-rata formula before proceeding: (pre-tax balance / total IRA balance) = taxable fraction. Review with your advisor to decide whether to roll pre-tax IRA funds into a 401(k) first to eliminate or reduce the taxable fraction.

**Step 3 -- Make a nondeductible contribution of up to $7,500 to the traditional IRA.**
The 2026 contribution limit is $7,500 ($8,600 if you are age 50 or older). Specify that this contribution is designated for tax year 2026. You may contribute any time between January 1, 2026 and the 2026 tax return due date of April 15, 2027 (not extended). Do not invest the funds yet -- leave them as cash or a money market fund to avoid taxable gains sitting in the traditional IRA at conversion time, which would add a small taxable amount to the conversion.

**Step 4 -- Request the conversion to your Roth IRA.**
After the traditional IRA contribution settles (typically one to three business days), request that your custodian convert the entire traditional IRA balance to your Roth IRA. You may do this immediately after contribution -- there is no legally required waiting period. The IRS has not challenged immediate conversions under the step transaction doctrine.

**Step 5 -- Invest the funds in your Roth IRA.**
Once the conversion is complete, invest the Roth IRA funds in your chosen investments. The money now grows tax-free.

**Step 6 -- Complete Form 8606 Parts I and II.**
- Part I: Report the nondeductible contribution and confirm your basis.
- Part II: Report the conversion and calculate the taxable amount using the pro-rata formula based on your December 31, 2026 IRA balances.
- Attach Form 8606 to your 2026 Form 1040. File by April 15, 2027. Extensions of the 1040 extend Form 8606's due date as well.

**Step 7 -- Repeat annually.**
The Backdoor Roth is an annual strategy. Repeat Steps 2 through 6 each year. Keep a record of your cumulative basis by saving every Form 8606 you file.

---

### Path C: Mega Backdoor Roth (employer 401(k) plan with after-tax contributions)

This path requires your advisor to first confirm your employer plan allows after-tax (non-Roth) employee contributions and either in-plan Roth conversions or in-service withdrawals.

**Step 1 -- Verify plan eligibility.**
Obtain a copy of your 401(k) Summary Plan Description (SPD) or ask your HR department in writing whether the plan allows: (a) after-tax (non-Roth) employee contributions, and (b) in-plan Roth conversions or in-service distributions.

**Step 2 -- Calculate your after-tax contribution room.**
The 2026 IRC Section 415(c) annual additions limit is $72,000 per participant (or $80,000 with the standard age 50+ catch-up). Your after-tax room equals $72,000 minus your regular pre-tax or Roth deferrals minus employer contributions. Example: if you defer $24,500 (the 2026 employee deferral limit) and your employer contributes $10,000, you have $72,000 - $24,500 - $10,000 = $37,500 of after-tax contribution room.

**Step 3 -- Make after-tax contributions to your 401(k).**
Set up after-tax contribution elections in your plan portal. These are separate from your regular pre-tax or Roth deferral elections.

**Step 4 -- Convert the after-tax balance in-plan or roll it out.**
- Option A (In-plan Roth conversion): If your plan allows it, elect an in-plan Roth conversion. The after-tax contributions move to a designated Roth account inside your plan. The contribution basis is tax-free; earnings accumulated since the contribution are taxable.
- Option B (In-service distribution and rollover): If the plan allows in-service distributions, direct the after-tax contribution dollars to a Roth IRA rollover under IRS Notice 2014-54. Instruct the plan to send pre-tax amounts to a traditional IRA and after-tax amounts directly to a Roth IRA. The after-tax principal is tax-free; any earnings on those contributions are sent to the traditional IRA and taxable later.

**Step 5 -- Report on Form 1099-R and your tax return.**
The plan will issue a Form 1099-R for the distribution. Work with your advisor or tax preparer to report the rollover correctly. Form 8606 may also be required if after-tax amounts are involved.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure:** If you operate through an S-corporation or partnership, your IRA contribution must still come from earned income (W-2 wages or self-employment net earnings), not from pass-through distributions. S-corp distributions do not count as compensation for IRA purposes. Verify your W-2 wages are sufficient to cover the contribution before transacting.

**SEP-IRA and the pro-rata problem:** Business owners who fund large SEP-IRAs each year often have very high pre-tax IRA balances, which makes the Backdoor Roth nearly fully taxable under the pro-rata rule. A common solution is to roll SEP-IRA balances into the business's 401(k) or Solo 401(k) plan before year-end of the conversion year, which removes those balances from the pro-rata calculation. Confirm plan acceptance rules with your advisor before rolling funds.

**Solo 401(k) and the Mega Backdoor Roth:** Self-employed individuals with a Solo 401(k) may be eligible for the Mega Backdoor Roth if their plan document was written to allow after-tax contributions. Many standard Solo 401(k) prototype documents do not include this feature. Your advisor must review your plan document.

**State tax treatment:** State income tax treatment of Roth conversions varies widely. Some states (such as Pennsylvania) do not tax retirement income at all; others tax the full conversion as ordinary income; others offer partial exemptions based on age or filing status. Do not assume your federal conversion strategy is optimal from a state perspective without state-specific advice.

**Current-year income changes:** If your 2026 income turns out significantly higher than projected (a large business sale, unexpected bonus, or capital gain), a Roth conversion that was planned to land in the 22% bracket could instead land in the 32% or higher bracket. Build in a buffer and reconcile your projection in November before year-end.

**ACA marketplace plans:** If you purchase health insurance through the ACA Marketplace, Roth conversion income increases your MAGI and can reduce or eliminate your Premium Tax Credit. This is particularly relevant for early retirees between age 55 and 65. Confirm the impact with your advisor before converting in any year you receive a Premium Tax Credit.

**Social Security provisional income:** If you are receiving Social Security benefits, Roth conversion income increases your "provisional income" (the formula used to determine how much of your Social Security benefit is taxable). Up to 85% of Social Security benefits may become taxable if your provisional income is high enough. Confirm the impact with your advisor.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** Roth conversion income is not itself subject to the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (which applies to passive investment income). However, the conversion increases your AGI, which can cause passive investment income (dividends, capital gains, rental income) to fall within the NIIT threshold of $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). This is an indirect effect to model.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Forgetting to file Form 8606.** This is the single most consequential clerical error in the Backdoor Roth strategy. Without Form 8606, the IRS has no record of your nondeductible basis, and you may be taxed twice on the same money. File it every single year you make a nondeductible contribution, even if no conversion occurs that year.

2. **Neglecting prior-year unfiled Forms 8606.** If you made nondeductible contributions in past years and never filed Form 8606 for those years, you are missing basis. You can file a standalone (non-amended) Form 8606 for past years to establish basis. The $50 penalty per year may apply but is often waived for reasonable cause.

3. **Withholding tax from the conversion itself.** If you instruct your custodian to withhold 20% or more for taxes from the conversion, that withheld amount never reaches the Roth IRA -- it is lost from the tax-advantaged space. If you are under age 59.5, the withheld amount is also treated as a taxable early distribution subject to the 10% penalty. Always elect zero withholding and pay the tax from separate savings.

4. **Ignoring the pro-rata rule.** High-income business owners frequently contribute to SEP-IRAs, accumulate large pre-tax balances, and then attempt a Backdoor Roth without accounting for the pro-rata rule. The result: a conversion that is 80% or 90% taxable rather than zero. Solve this by rolling pre-tax IRA funds into a 401(k) before year-end, but only after confirming the 401(k) plan accepts IRA rollovers.

5. **Assuming the conversion is reversible.** As of January 1, 2018, Roth conversions cannot be recharacterized (undone). If the market drops sharply after you convert and you are holding the converted amount in stocks inside your Roth IRA, you still owe tax on the pre-conversion value. There is no undo button. Do not convert volatile assets you might want to reverse.

6. **Missing the December 31 deadline for conversions.** Conversions must be completed by December 31 to count in a given tax year. Custodians get extremely busy in December. Initiate any year-end conversion by mid-December at the latest to avoid processing delays pushing the transaction into January of the following year.

7. **Doubling up contributions and conversions in the same tax year without tracking both on Form 8606.** If you made a 2025 contribution in early 2026 (between January 1 and April 15, 2026) AND a 2026 contribution later in 2026, both must be correctly reflected on the appropriate year's Form 8606. Mismatching the year of contribution with the year of the Form 8606 creates basis errors that compound over time.

8. **Not modeling the IRMAA cliff.** If you are age 63 or older, a conversion of even $1 above an IRMAA threshold can add more than $80 per month to your Medicare premiums for a full year -- a cost of nearly $1,000 per year. This is one of the most frequently overlooked conversion costs for clients near or in Medicare eligibility.

9. **Converting SIMPLE IRA funds within the first two years of plan participation.** This can trigger a 25% excise tax. Always confirm the SIMPLE IRA two-year anniversary before initiating a conversion.

10. **Failing to plan for the 5-year conversion clock when under age 59.5.** Each conversion creates its own five-year waiting period before you can access the converted principal penalty-free. If you are young and plan on needing access to converted funds before the clock expires, factor the potential 10% penalty into your analysis.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before acting if any of the following apply:

- [ ] Your total pre-tax IRA balance (across all traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs) is greater than zero and you plan to do a Backdoor Roth. The pro-rata calculation must be reviewed before you transact.
- [ ] You are considering rolling a SEP-IRA or traditional IRA into a 401(k) to clear the pro-rata rule. Confirm the 401(k) plan accepts IRA rollovers and the timing is coordinated with the conversion year.
- [ ] Your projected 2026 income plus the conversion amount would cross a tax bracket boundary, an IRMAA threshold, or the ACA Premium Tax Credit phase-out.
- [ ] You are age 63 or older, because the conversion will affect your 2028 Medicare premiums.
- [ ] You are under age 59.5 and may need access to converted funds within five years.
- [ ] You have a SIMPLE IRA and are within two years of your first contribution to that plan.
- [ ] You receive Social Security benefits and want to understand how the conversion affects the taxable portion of your benefit.
- [ ] You have not filed Form 8606 for prior years when nondeductible contributions were made. Your advisor can help you reconstruct and file retroactively.
- [ ] You are considering a Mega Backdoor Roth. Your plan document must first be confirmed as allowing after-tax contributions and in-plan conversions or in-service distributions.
- [ ] Your state has a high marginal income tax rate and you have not modeled the state tax cost of the conversion.
- [ ] You want to convert a large amount and want to know whether a Roth conversion ladder (spreading conversions over multiple years) produces a better after-tax outcome than a single large conversion.

## Before You Act

You must confirm your specific facts with your advisor before executing any Roth conversion or Backdoor Roth transaction. This page teaches the strategy and its mechanics in general terms; it does not account for your individual income, existing IRA balances, entity structure, state of residence, Medicare status, Social Security situation, or investment timeline.

Before you move any money:

1. Complete the Implementation Worksheet with your actual 2026 numbers.
2. Bring the completed worksheet to your advisor and get explicit sign-off on the conversion amount and timing.
3. Confirm the tax payment plan: how much will you owe, when is it due, and which account will fund it.
4. If you have any pre-tax IRA balances, confirm the pro-rata rule has been addressed (either by rolling those balances into a 401(k) or by accepting and planning for the taxable fraction).

**A conversion executed without this review can result in an unexpected tax bill, IRMAA surcharges, early withdrawal penalties, or double taxation of money you already paid tax on.** The strategy is sound; the execution details are what require personalized review.
