# Cost Segregation

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Cost segregation is a tax strategy that lets owners of commercial and investment real estate accelerate depreciation deductions by reclassifying parts of a building -- walls, wiring, plumbing fixtures, flooring, parking lots, and similar components -- from slow long-life categories (27.5 or 39 years) into faster short-life categories (5, 7, or 15 years). A qualified engineer or cost segregation specialist prepares a detailed study that identifies which portions of your property cost qualify for the shorter schedules. The result is a front-loaded stack of depreciation deductions in the early years you own the property, which reduces taxable income and defers taxes -- often generating five- or six-figure deductions in year one alone. Because the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025, components reclassified through a cost segregation study may be fully deducted in the year placed in service rather than spread over years at all.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is most valuable for:

- **Owners of commercial real property** -- office buildings, retail spaces, warehouses, industrial facilities, mixed-use buildings, hotels, and similar nonresidential property. The building's book value for tax purposes (cost basis minus land) is depreciated over 39 years under standard rules; cost segregation shortens that for qualifying components.
- **Owners of residential rental property** -- apartment buildings, multifamily housing, single-family rentals held as investment property. The structure depreciates over 27.5 years; personal property components (appliances, carpeting, certain fixtures) and land improvements can be reclassified to 5 or 15 years.
- **Taxpayers who recently purchased, built, or substantially renovated real property** -- the strategy is most powerful in the early years of ownership or immediately after a large improvement project, before depreciation on the long-life components has accumulated.
- **Taxpayers with tax appetite to absorb the deductions** -- a large accelerated depreciation deduction only helps if you have income to offset. This includes real estate professionals (defined below), high-income business owners, or investors who qualify for the passive activity exceptions described below.
- **Owners of properties with high personal property and land improvement content** -- restaurants, medical offices, car dealerships, grocery stores, and similar property types tend to have 20-40% of their cost eligible for reclassification, making the return on study cost compelling.
- **Taxpayers who have owned property for multiple years without a prior cost segregation study** -- a "lookback" or catch-up method change (filed on Form 3115) lets you claim all the missed accelerated depreciation in a single year without amending prior returns.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Land-only or bare-land holdings:** Land itself is never depreciable. Cost segregation only applies to improvements -- the building and built components. If land is the bulk of your purchase price, the study will have little to analyze.
- **Property you plan to sell very soon (within 1-2 years):** Accelerating depreciation today means a larger Section 1245 recapture at sale -- any prior depreciation on personal property is recaptured as ordinary income, not capital gain, when you sell. If the holding period is too short, recapture may offset the benefit. Model this with your advisor before proceeding.
- **Taxpayers who cannot use the losses due to passive activity rules and have no path to eligibility:** Rental real estate losses are generally passive (see Key Terms). If your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) exceeds $150,000, you cannot use the $25,000 active participation allowance; and if you are not a real estate professional with 750+ qualifying hours, the accelerated deductions will pile into a suspended loss account rather than shelter current income. Suspended losses are released at sale, so the benefit is deferred rather than lost -- but if cash-flow timing matters, confirm your passive activity position first.
- **Very low-cost properties:** Engineering-based cost segregation studies typically cost $5,000 to $15,000 or more [confirm with your advisor -- could not verify against a public source for a specific current price range from a canonical source]. For properties under $500,000 in depreciable basis, the study fee may exceed the present-value benefit. Your advisor can model whether the net savings justify the cost.
- **Properties where the owner has already been under IRS examination and has not resolved depreciation positions:** Adding a method change while under audit requires non-automatic Form 3115 treatment and IRS National Office consent. Confirm your examination status before filing.

## Key Terms

**Depreciation:** The tax deduction that lets you recover the cost of a business or income-producing asset over time. You spread the cost across years rather than deducting it all at once. Governed by Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 167 and the Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System (MACRS) rules in IRC Section 168.

**MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System):** The system required for most business property placed in service after 1986. MACRS assigns each asset to a property class with a fixed recovery period and depreciation method, and produces annual deduction percentages through IRS tables. All references in this playbook assume GDS (General Depreciation System), the faster of the two MACRS tracks; the slower track is called ADS (Alternative Depreciation System).

**Recovery period:** The number of years over which you depreciate an asset under MACRS. Key periods relevant to cost segregation:
  - **5-year property:** Includes appliances, carpets, and furniture in a residential rental property; certain machinery and equipment. Method: 200% declining balance switching to straight-line.
  - **7-year property:** Includes office furniture and equipment, and any property not assigned to another class. Method: 200% declining balance switching to straight-line.
  - **15-year property:** Includes land improvements such as parking lots, sidewalks, roads, fences, landscaping, and drainage. Also includes Qualified Improvement Property (see below). Method: 150% declining balance switching to straight-line.
  - **27.5-year property:** Residential rental buildings (apartment buildings, multifamily housing where 80% or more of gross rent is from dwelling units). Method: straight-line. Convention: mid-month.
  - **39-year property:** Nonresidential real property (commercial buildings, office, retail, industrial). Method: straight-line. Convention: mid-month.

**Section 1245 property:** Tangible personal property and certain other non-real property whose full depreciation is subject to "recapture" as ordinary income upon sale. Components reclassified into 5-year or 7-year categories through cost segregation are typically Section 1245 property. When you sell, any prior depreciation you took on these components is taxed at ordinary income rates, not capital gain rates, up to the amount of gain.

**Section 1250 property:** Real property (buildings and structural components). When you sell real property on which you took straight-line depreciation, the gain attributable to prior depreciation above the property's adjusted basis is taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25% ("unrecaptured Section 1250 gain"), still below the ordinary income rate that applies to Section 1245 property.

**Bonus depreciation (also called "additional first year depreciation" or "special depreciation allowance"):** A provision under IRC Section 168(k) that permits an immediate 100% deduction for the full cost of qualifying property in the year it is placed in service, rather than over the recovery period. As restored by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21), 100% bonus depreciation applies to qualified property acquired after January 19, 2025. Property acquired on or before January 19, 2025 but placed in service after December 31, 2024 and before January 1, 2026 was limited to 40% (60% for long-production-period property). Components reclassified in the 5-year, 7-year, and 15-year classes through cost segregation typically qualify for bonus depreciation.

**Qualified Improvement Property (QIP):** An improvement to the interior of a nonresidential building that is placed in service after the building was first placed in service, made by the taxpayer, and is Section 1250 property. QIP excludes enlargements, elevators/escalators, and internal structural framework. QIP has a 15-year MACRS recovery period (straight-line method) and qualifies for bonus depreciation.

**Section 179 deduction:** A separate provision in IRC Section 179 that lets you elect to immediately expense the cost of qualifying business property (generally tangible personal property and certain qualified real property improvements) up to the annual dollar limit, rather than depreciating it. For tax years beginning in 2026, the limit is $2,560,000, phasing out dollar-for-dollar once total Section 179 property placed in service exceeds $4,090,000. Section 179 cannot create a loss (it is limited to taxable income from active trade or business), while bonus depreciation can.

**Cost segregation study:** A detailed engineering analysis prepared by a cost segregation specialist that identifies and documents the cost attributable to each building component, classifies each component into the appropriate MACRS property class, and calculates the resulting depreciation deductions. The IRS Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide (Publication 5653) describes what a high-quality study must contain.

**Lookback study (catch-up study):** A cost segregation study applied to a property you have already owned for one or more years. Because you have been depreciating the property on the wrong (slower) schedule, you calculate the "missed" depreciation through a method change filed on Form 3115. The IRS allows this catch-up as a Section 481(a) adjustment without amending prior returns.

**Form 3115 -- Application for Change in Accounting Method:** The IRS form used to request permission to change how you account for depreciation. When a cost segregation study causes you to reclassify components and adopt a corrected (faster) depreciation method, you file Form 3115 under the automatic consent procedures in Rev. Proc. 2025-23. No user fee is required for automatic changes. The change takes effect in the year of filing.

**Section 481(a) adjustment:** When you change an accounting method on Form 3115, the IRC 481(a) adjustment captures all the difference between depreciation actually claimed in prior years and depreciation that would have been allowable under the new (corrected) method. A negative adjustment (you are owed more deductions) is taken entirely in the year of change as a single large deduction. A positive adjustment (you over-deducted) is spread over 4 years. For a lookback cost segregation study, the 481(a) adjustment is almost always negative (favorable to the taxpayer) and is deducted all at once in the filing year.

**Passive activity:** An activity in which you do not materially participate, or, for purposes of rental real estate, an activity that is treated as passive by default under IRC Section 469. Losses from passive activities can generally only offset income from other passive activities, not wages or business income.

**Real estate professional (IRC Section 469(c)(7)):** A taxpayer who meets both of these tests: (1) more than 750 hours during the tax year are spent in real property trades or businesses in which the taxpayer materially participates, and (2) more than 50% of all personal services performed in all trades or businesses are performed in those qualifying real property trades or businesses. A real estate professional who also materially participates in each rental activity may treat rental losses as non-passive -- allowing accelerated depreciation deductions from cost segregation to offset wages, business income, or any other income.

**Active participation (Section 469(i)):** A lower standard than material participation. If you actively participate in your rental real estate activity (make management decisions such as approving tenants and repairs) and your MAGI is $100,000 or less, you may deduct up to $25,000 of rental passive losses against ordinary income each year. This allowance phases out at a rate of $0.50 per dollar of MAGI above $100,000 and is fully eliminated when MAGI reaches $150,000. (For married filing separately with spouses who lived apart all year, the allowance is $12,500, phasing out from $50,000 to $75,000 MAGI.)

**Passive activity loss suspension:** Passive losses that exceed passive income in a year are not lost; they are "suspended" and carry forward indefinitely. They are released in full when you sell the property in a fully taxable transaction.

**Form 4562 -- Depreciation and Amortization:** The form attached to your tax return each year to report all depreciation deductions, bonus depreciation claims, and Section 179 elections.

**Form 4797 -- Sales of Business Property:** The form used to report the sale of depreciable business property. Section 1245 recapture is computed on Part III of Form 4797 and flows to ordinary income.

**Form 8582 -- Passive Activity Loss Limitations:** The form used to calculate and track suspended passive activity losses.

**Convention:** The assumed mid-point rule the IRS uses to determine when in the year property is placed in service. The half-year convention (used for 5-year, 7-year, and 15-year property) treats all such property as if it were placed in service at the mid-point of the tax year, regardless of actual date. The mid-month convention (used for 27.5-year and 39-year property) treats the property as placed in service at the mid-point of the month it is actually placed in service. If more than 40% of depreciable personal property is placed in service in the last quarter, the mid-quarter convention applies instead of the half-year convention.

**GDS vs. ADS:** GDS (General Depreciation System) uses shorter recovery periods and accelerated methods and is the default for most property. ADS (Alternative Depreciation System) uses longer lives and straight-line method; it is required for certain property (listed property not used predominantly for business, property used outside the U.S., property leased to tax-exempt entities) and is elected for other property. Cost segregation studies assume GDS unless ADS is required or elected.

**IRC Section 263A (UNICAP):** Uniform Capitalization rules that may require certain costs to be capitalized into property rather than deducted immediately. UNICAP applies primarily to resellers with more than $31 million in gross receipts and to producers of inventory. Most real estate investors are not subject to UNICAP, but confirm with your advisor if you have significant gross receipts.

**Depreciable basis:** The cost you use as the starting point for depreciation. For purchased property, this is generally the purchase price plus closing costs attributable to the property, minus the value allocated to land (land is never depreciable). For constructed property, it is the total construction cost. Proper allocation between land and improvements at purchase is essential.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before engaging a cost segregation specialist:

1. **Property address and type:** Commercial (nonresidential), residential rental (multifamily, single-family), or mixed-use. This determines the applicable base recovery period (39-year or 27.5-year).
2. **Date placed in service (or acquired):** The year, month, and day the property was first available for its intended use. This establishes the convention that applies and determines bonus depreciation eligibility.
3. **Total purchase price (or construction cost)** and how it was allocated on your closing statement or construction contract between land, building, and any personal property.
4. **Depreciable basis in the building (cost minus land allocation):** Your advisor or the settlement statement should show how the purchase price was split. Land is not depreciable.
5. **Whether a prior cost segregation study exists** for this property, and if so, a copy. Duplicate studies for the same property waste money and create inconsistencies.
6. **Whether you have already filed depreciation on this property** (i.e., the property was placed in service in a prior tax year). If yes, your current depreciation schedule or Form 4562 from your most recent return is needed so the specialist can compute the Section 481(a) adjustment.
7. **Copies of construction documents, blueprints, or architectural drawings** (for new construction or significant renovations). The more detailed the records, the more defensible the study.
8. **Your tax entity type and filing status:** Individual (Schedule E), partnership (Form 1065), S-corporation (Form 1120-S), or C-corporation (Form 1120). This affects how the deductions flow through to you.
9. **Your passive activity position:** Are you a real estate professional? Do you actively participate? What is your estimated MAGI? This determines whether accelerated deductions offset current-year income or pile into suspended losses.
10. **Whether you are under IRS examination** for any open tax years. If yes, tell your advisor before filing Form 3115; examination status changes the filing rules.
11. **Whether the property was acquired after January 19, 2025** (for bonus depreciation eligibility at 100%).

## Required Documents And Records

**Gather at engagement:**
- Settlement statement (HUD-1 or Closing Disclosure) showing purchase price allocation
- Prior depreciation schedules (Form 4562 and detail) for the property if owned more than one year
- Construction contracts, pay applications, or cost certifications (for new builds or renovations)
- Architectural blueprints, floor plans, or as-built drawings
- Cost breakdown by trade (electrical, plumbing, HVAC, framing, finishes) if available from the general contractor
- Prior cost segregation study (if one was done) and any related Form 3115

**Keep indefinitely (for as long as you own the property plus at least 7 years after sale):**
- The completed cost segregation study report (the engineering document itself)
- Form 3115 and the acknowledgment copy (if a lookback study)
- All Form 4562 attachments showing the reclassified assets and their depreciation schedules
- Documents supporting the depreciable basis: settlement statement, appraisal allocating land vs. improvements, construction invoices
- Any IRS correspondence related to the depreciation positions taken

**Why long retention matters:** The IRS can challenge depreciation positions for any open tax year. Because the reclassified assets remain on your books until sold or retired, and because the basis and prior depreciation affect gain or recapture calculations at sale, these records must survive through the entire ownership period and beyond.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Step 1: Confirm your passive activity position (before spending money on a study)

Work with your advisor to determine whether you can currently use accelerated depreciation deductions against ordinary income, or whether they will be suspended as passive losses. Answer these questions:

- Are you a real estate professional under IRC Section 469(c)(7) (more than 750 hours in qualifying real property trades or businesses, and that is more than 50% of all your working hours)?
- If not, does your MAGI fall below $150,000 so the $25,000 active participation allowance applies in full or partially?
- Do you have other passive income from other rental activities or passive business interests against which suspended losses could be used?

If deductions will be suspended indefinitely and you have no near-term plan to sell the property, the cash-flow timing benefit may be minimal. Still discuss with your advisor, because suspended losses offset taxable gain at sale.

**Output of Step 1:** A clear statement from your advisor of how much of the projected cost segregation deduction will reduce your current-year tax vs. be suspended.

### Step 2: Estimate the feasibility and ROI of a study

Ask your advisor to run a quick feasibility estimate before hiring a cost segregation specialist. A rough rule of thumb: 20-40% of the depreciable building cost for a commercial property, or 10-30% for a standard multifamily building, may be eligible for reclassification to shorter lives [confirm this range with your advisor, as it varies significantly by property type -- no IRS source publishes a universal percentage]. Multiply your estimated reclassifiable percentage by your marginal tax rate and apply a present-value discount to estimate the tax benefit. Compare to the estimated cost of the study.

**Output of Step 2:** Decision to proceed or not proceed, documented in writing with your advisor.

### Step 3: Select a qualified cost segregation specialist

The IRS Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide (IRS Publication 5653) describes what a quality study looks like. Look for a firm or engineer who:

- Uses an engineering-based methodology (physical inspection of the property combined with analysis of construction documents)
- Has experience with your property type (commercial, multifamily, medical, hospitality)
- Can provide a written report that itemizes and supports each component classification
- Is willing to defend the study in an IRS examination

Do not use a study that merely applies a statistical or residual method without an engineering analysis unless the study is for a low-cost property where an engineering study is not economically justified. The IRS scrutinizes low-quality studies.

**Use the Cost Segregation Resource Guide download** at this step to evaluate potential specialists and understand what the report must contain.

**Output of Step 3:** Engagement letter with a qualified cost segregation specialist.

### Step 4: Provide documents and coordinate the site visit

Deliver to the specialist: the settlement statement, prior depreciation schedules, construction documents, blueprints, and contractor cost breakdowns gathered in the Required Documents section above. The specialist will typically conduct a physical inspection of the property. Coordinate property access.

**Output of Step 4:** Site visit completed; all source documents delivered to specialist.

### Step 5: Review the draft cost segregation study report

When the specialist delivers the draft report, review it with your advisor before finalizing. Confirm:

- The total depreciable basis in the report matches the depreciable basis on your tax records (purchase price minus land allocation, plus capitalized improvements, minus any amounts already disposed of).
- Each reclassified component is described in enough detail to be identified and defended (not vague descriptions like "electrical -- 5 year").
- The asset list distinguishes structural components (stay at 39 or 27.5 years) from personal property components (move to 5 or 7 years) and land improvements (move to 15 years), with the reasoning documented.
- The study methodology section describes the engineering approach, documents inspected, and computations used.

**Output of Step 5:** Signed-off, finalized cost segregation study report.

### Step 6: Determine whether this is a current-year study or a lookback study

- **Current-year study (property acquired or placed in service in the current tax year):** The reclassified assets are simply entered on Form 4562 at the correct (shorter) MACRS lives from day one. No Form 3115 is required. Apply bonus depreciation to eligible components if you choose not to elect out.
- **Lookback study (property placed in service in a prior tax year):** You have been depreciating the property on the wrong schedule. To correct it, you must file Form 3115 (Application for Change in Accounting Method) to formally change the accounting method to the reclassified lives. The Section 481(a) adjustment (the cumulative "missed" depreciation from prior years) is deducted entirely in the year of change as a single lump sum -- you do not amend prior returns.

**Output of Step 6:** Decision on filing path (current-year vs. Form 3115 lookback).

### Step 7: Determine your bonus depreciation position for reclassified components

For property acquired after January 19, 2025, 100% bonus depreciation is available under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21) for qualified property (5-year, 7-year, and 15-year MACRS components that are not the building structure itself). For property acquired on or before January 19, 2025 and placed in service after December 31, 2024 but before January 1, 2026, the allowance was 40% (60% for long-production-period property).

Discuss with your advisor whether to:
- **Claim 100% bonus** on all eligible reclassified components (maximizes the immediate deduction, accelerates tax benefit).
- **Elect out of bonus** on some or all property classes (spreads deductions over the normal MACRS recovery period, which may be preferable if you expect higher tax rates in the future or if the current-year deduction would exceed your usable income).

The election to use 40% instead of 100% for the first tax year ending after January 19, 2025 is available (see Notice 2026-11).

Bonus depreciation is NOT limited by taxable income (unlike Section 179). It can create or increase a net operating loss (NOL).

**Output of Step 7:** Decision, documented with your advisor, on whether to claim bonus depreciation and on which asset classes.

### Step 8: Prepare and file Form 4562

Your tax preparer enters all reclassified assets onto Form 4562 (Depreciation and Amortization) at their corrected MACRS class lives. If bonus depreciation is elected, those amounts are shown in Part II of Form 4562. Section 179 elections, if any, are shown in Part I. The form is attached to your timely filed tax return (or extension).

**Output of Step 8:** Completed Form 4562 included in your tax return.

### Step 9: File Form 3115 for lookback studies (if applicable)

If this is a lookback study, your tax preparer prepares Form 3115 under the automatic consent procedures in Rev. Proc. 2025-23:

- The original Form 3115 is attached to your timely filed (including extensions) federal income tax return for the year of change.
- A signed duplicate copy of Form 3115 is sent to the IRS in Ogden, UT, by the same deadline as the tax return.
- The Section 481(a) adjustment (the negative catch-up deduction) is reported on Schedule E (for individuals) or on the applicable income or deduction line of your entity return for the year of change.
- No user fee is required for automatic consent changes.

**Output of Step 9:** Form 3115 filed with original return and duplicate mailed to IRS Ogden, UT.

### Step 10: Update your depreciation schedule for future years

Once the reclassification is complete, your ongoing depreciation records must reflect the new asset list. Every year going forward, Form 4562 is filed showing the continuing depreciation on both (a) the reclassified shorter-life assets (until they are fully depreciated or disposed of) and (b) the remaining building components still on the 27.5-year or 39-year schedule.

**Output of Step 10:** Updated depreciation schedule in your tax records reflecting all asset classes.

### Step 11: Track passive activity loss carryforwards on Form 8582 (if applicable)

If any portion of the accelerated depreciation creates a passive loss that exceeds your current usable amount, your tax preparer tracks the suspended loss on Form 8582 each year. The suspended loss is released when you dispose of the property in a fully taxable transaction. Keep copies of all Form 8582 filings.

**Output of Step 11:** Form 8582 filed with annual return; suspended loss carryforward documented.

### Step 12: Plan for recapture at sale

Before you sell the property, work with your advisor to model the Section 1245 and Section 1250 recapture consequences. Any depreciation taken on 5-year and 7-year components is fully recaptured as ordinary income under Section 1245 upon sale (up to the amount of gain). The remaining building structure's unrecaptured depreciation is taxed at a maximum 25% federal rate (unrecaptured Section 1250 gain). Report both on Form 4797 in the year of sale.

**Output of Step 12:** Pre-sale tax model showing net after-tax benefit of cost segregation, accounting for recapture.

## Timing And Deadlines

**When to commission a study:**
- The optimal time is the year the property is placed in service. Deductions from the study are claimed on that year's return.
- For existing properties (lookback), the study can be done at any point. The Form 3115 method change takes effect for the tax year you file it.
- Studies can be done even after the property has been owned for 10, 15, or 20 years; all prior missed depreciation is still captured as a single 481(a) adjustment in the year of change.

**Tax return filing deadline for the year of change:**
- Individuals: April 15 of the year following the tax year (plus automatic extension to October 15 if Form 4868 is timely filed).
- Corporations: March 15 for S-corps (plus extension to September 15); April 15 for C-corps (plus extension to October 15).

**Form 3115 filing deadline (lookback studies):**
- The original Form 3115 must be attached to a timely filed return (including extensions) for the year of change. The duplicate copy must be mailed to the IRS Ogden, UT address by the same deadline.

**Bonus depreciation acquisition date cutoff:**
- Property acquired after January 19, 2025: eligible for 100% bonus depreciation.
- Property acquired on or before January 19, 2025 and placed in service in a tax year ending on or after January 20, 2025 but before January 1, 2026: limited to 40% bonus depreciation (as reflected in the phase-down rules before the One Big Beautiful Bill Act).

**Annual depreciation (ongoing):** Depreciation on all remaining assets is reported every year on Form 4562 attached to the tax return until each asset is fully depreciated or disposed of.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity type affects how deductions flow:**
- **Schedule E filers (individuals owning rental property directly or through a pass-through):** Depreciation deductions from cost segregation appear on Schedule E and flow to Form 1040. Passive activity rules apply at the individual level.
- **S-corporations:** Depreciation flows to shareholders on Schedule K-1. Shareholders must separately track their passive activity status and basis.
- **Partnerships and multi-member LLCs taxed as partnerships:** Depreciation is allocated to partners per the operating agreement. Each partner applies passive activity rules individually. Partners may need to elect to aggregate rental activities to qualify as a real estate professional.
- **C-corporations:** A C-corporation is not subject to the passive activity rules at the entity level. All depreciation deductions reduce corporate taxable income directly. However, C-corps cannot pass losses to shareholders.

**Aggregation election for real estate professionals (partnerships and LLCs):** If you own multiple rental properties and want to qualify as a real estate professional by aggregating hours across all of them, you must file an election statement with your return. Confirm timing of this election with your advisor; once made, it applies to all future years.

**State income tax:** Most states follow federal MACRS depreciation, but not all. Some states have decoupled from federal bonus depreciation (meaning they do not allow the immediate 100% deduction and require you to add back the federal bonus amount and depreciate at state rates). Check with your advisor for the specific states where your property is located and where you file returns. States that commonly decouple from federal bonus depreciation include California, New York, New Jersey, and Illinois [confirm current state conformity with your advisor -- state law changes frequently].

**Alternative minimum tax (AMT):** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act's changes to the corporate and individual AMT may affect planning. Confirm with your advisor whether AMT applies to your situation and whether any depreciation adjustment (difference between regular MACRS and ADS) is required.

**Net Operating Loss (NOL) limitations:** If the accelerated depreciation deduction creates or increases a net operating loss, the NOL is subject to the 80% taxable income limitation for carryforwards under current law. For certain pass-through businesses, excess business loss limitations under IRC Section 461(l) may also apply. Confirm with your advisor.

**At-risk rules (IRC Section 465):** Deductions from a rental activity are also subject to at-risk limitations. Generally you cannot deduct losses in excess of the amount you have "at risk" in the activity (essentially your equity investment plus recourse debt). Most real estate investors with equity in the property are at risk for at least that amount. Confirm if your financing structure involves non-recourse debt that is not qualified non-recourse financing (most commercial real estate non-recourse debt qualifies).

**IRC Section 163(j) interest limitation:** For businesses with average annual gross receipts over $31 million (the 2025 threshold; confirm the 2026 amount with your advisor), the deduction for business interest expense may be limited. Under the OBBBA, for tax years beginning in 2025, the calculation of adjusted taxable income now adds back depreciation, amortization, and depletion -- effectively a more favorable base for this calculation than applied from 2022 through 2024. This interacts with cost segregation because higher depreciation deductions reduce adjusted taxable income, which could in some structures reduce the allowed interest deduction. Confirm with your advisor if your business is above the gross receipts threshold.

**UNICAP (IRC Section 263A):** If your entity is subject to the uniform capitalization rules, some costs that would otherwise be deducted currently must be capitalized. Most individual investors in rental real estate are not subject to UNICAP, but confirm with your advisor.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Treating the building's structural components as personal property.** Walls, roof decking, load-bearing columns, and the building's internal structural framework are Section 1250 real property regardless of what a poorly done study may say. Over-aggressive reclassification is the primary audit risk in cost segregation. Use only qualified, engineering-based studies.

2. **Forgetting that bonus depreciation does not apply to the building structure itself.** A 39-year commercial building or 27.5-year residential rental building is Section 1250 property and does not qualify for bonus depreciation or Section 179. Only the reclassified shorter-life components get bonus.

3. **Not modeling recapture before deciding how aggressively to accelerate.** Section 1245 recapture at ordinary income rates upon sale can create an unexpected tax bill. Buyers of already-depreciated property inherit this risk. Model the net after-tax benefit over your expected holding period before committing.

4. **Missing the passive activity analysis.** Generating a $500,000 cost segregation deduction is pointless if $500,000 piles into suspended passive losses for a taxpayer who has no passive income and no path to becoming a real estate professional. Always confirm your passive activity position first.

5. **Failing to file Form 3115 for lookback studies.** Some taxpayers try to simply amend prior returns or claim the catch-up depreciation on the current return without Form 3115. This is not the correct procedure. A method change requires Form 3115, and filing without it invites IRS challenge.

6. **Applying the wrong convention.** If more than 40% of personal property is placed in service in the fourth quarter, the mid-quarter convention applies, which reduces the first-year deduction for property placed in service in the first three quarters. Confirm with your preparer which convention applies.

7. **Not keeping the study report permanently.** The IRS can challenge depreciation classifications for any open tax year. Because basis flows through to sale, depreciation records must survive the entire ownership period. Do not discard cost segregation studies after a few years.

8. **Treating all electrical and plumbing as personal property.** Electrical wiring and plumbing that serve general building functions (general lighting, restrooms) remain building components (Section 1250 property). Only the portions that serve specific process or equipment functions (dedicated equipment hookups, specialized drainage) qualify for reclassification. A qualified study distinguishes these.

9. **Confusing Section 179 and bonus depreciation.** Section 179 cannot exceed taxable income from active business; bonus depreciation can create a loss. Section 179 has dollar caps; bonus depreciation does not. For most real estate investors, bonus depreciation (not Section 179) is the relevant provision, because real estate rental is generally not an active trade or business for Section 179 purposes unless you meet specific requirements.

10. **Ignoring state decoupling.** Claiming full federal bonus depreciation when your state has decoupled means you will owe more state tax than expected. Confirm state conformity before relying on the combined federal-plus-state tax benefit in your ROI estimate.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and review the following with your advisor before taking action:

1. **Before commissioning a study:** Confirm your passive activity position and whether accelerated deductions will benefit you in the current year or be suspended. Confirm the depreciable basis and that the property type is eligible.

2. **Before claiming bonus depreciation on reclassified components:** Confirm acquisition date (before or after January 19, 2025), which property classes qualify, whether you want to elect out on any class, and how bonus depreciation interacts with any NOL limitations or excess business loss rules that apply to you.

3. **Before filing Form 3115 for a lookback study:** Confirm (a) you are not currently under IRS examination for the property's depreciation, (b) the correct revenue procedure (currently Rev. Proc. 2025-23) is the applicable one, (c) the Section 481(a) adjustment amount is correctly computed, and (d) the duplicate copy is mailed to the IRS Ogden address by the return deadline.

4. **Before selling a property that has been cost-segregated:** Model the full Section 1245 and Section 1250 recapture tax due, net of any suspended passive loss releases, to determine whether a 1031 exchange, installment sale, or other structure is appropriate.

5. **Whenever you acquire a new property** of any significant value: ask your advisor whether a cost segregation study is appropriate at that time, so you do not miss the opportunity to capture full bonus depreciation in year one.

6. **If you are considering claiming real estate professional status for the first time:** This requires documenting and substantiating hours, making the aggregation election in the correct year, and careful analysis of the 50%-of-all-services test. Review all requirements with your advisor before filing.

## Before You Act

Do not begin a cost segregation study, file Form 3115, claim bonus depreciation on reclassified components, change your depreciation schedules, or take any position on your return based on this playbook until you have confirmed your specific facts with your advisor. The deduction amounts involved are large enough that errors -- wrong property classification, an unqualified study, a missed passive activity limitation, or a state decoupling you did not account for -- can result in IRS audit adjustments, penalties, and interest that exceed the tax benefit. This page teaches the strategy and the implementation process; your advisor applies it to your specific property, entity structure, income level, and state filing obligations.
