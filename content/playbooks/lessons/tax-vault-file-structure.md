# Tax Vault File Structure

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

A Tax Vault is an organized digital (or physical) filing system that houses every document your business needs to support its tax return -- receipts, contracts, payroll records, bank statements, and more -- arranged so that you or your advisor can find any document in under two minutes. Building this system once and feeding it throughout the year transforms tax season from a scramble into a routine review. It also gives you a ready-made defense file if the IRS ever asks questions. This playbook shows you exactly how to build, label, and maintain that structure.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is for you if any of the following describes your situation:

- You own or operate a business in any form -- sole proprietorship, S corporation (a corporation that passes profits and losses through to individual shareholders to avoid double taxation at the corporate level), C corporation, partnership, or LLC (Limited Liability Company, a legal entity that can be taxed as a sole proprietor, partnership, S corp, or C corp depending on how it is set up).
- You claim business deductions and want documentation that can survive an IRS review.
- You currently store receipts and records in a disorganized way -- email folders, a shoebox, or multiple apps -- and want a single system.
- You work with an advisor and want to give them organized, complete records at year-end.
- You have been through an audit or received an IRS notice and realize your documentation needs improvement.

This playbook works whether your business is new or established, and whether you prefer a digital folder structure (recommended) or a physical binder system.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- You are a W-2 employee (someone who receives a paycheck from an employer who withholds taxes) with no business activity at all. Your personal document needs are simpler and are not covered here.
- You are already enrolled in an accounting platform (such as QuickBooks or Xero) that automatically ingests and categorizes receipts. You may still want this structure as a backup layer, but the platform itself handles much of the organization described here. Ask your advisor whether a standalone vault is still useful in your setup.
- You are looking for guidance on what deductions to claim. This playbook covers how to document and store records, not whether a specific expense qualifies. Deduction eligibility guidance is covered in the relevant strategy lessons (for example, the Home Office Reimbursement playbook or the Business Travel Deduction playbook).
- You have already received a Notice of Deficiency (a statutory IRS letter giving you 90 days to petition U.S. Tax Court) or are under a criminal tax investigation. Stop and contact your advisor immediately.

## Key Terms

**Tax Vault:** The organized filing system -- digital folders, a physical binder, or both -- that holds every document supporting your tax returns and business finances for the required retention period.

**Statute of Limitations (SOL):** The legal window during which the IRS can audit your return and assess additional tax. Knowing the SOL tells you how long to keep each document. See "The Numbers" section for the specific periods.

**Substantiation:** Proof that a deduction is legitimate. Substantiation means having a document (receipt, bank statement, log, or contract) that shows the amount, date, place, business purpose, and -- for meals -- who was present. Without substantiation, the IRS can disallow the deduction.

**Contemporaneous Records:** Records created at or very near the time of a transaction or expense. The IRS gives much more weight to contemporaneous records than to records reconstructed from memory weeks or months later. This is especially important for vehicle mileage logs, meal receipts, and travel records.

**IRC Section 274(d):** The section of the Internal Revenue Code that imposes strict substantiation rules for travel, meals, vehicle, and gift expenses. If these records are missing, the IRS can disallow the deduction entirely, even if the expense clearly happened. Normal judicial leniency (the "Cohan Rule") does not apply.

**IRC Section 6501:** The Internal Revenue Code section that establishes the IRS's statute of limitations for assessing additional tax -- generally 3 years from the filing date, with longer periods under certain conditions.

**Form 1120-S:** The U.S. Income Tax Return for an S Corporation, filed annually. S corps pass their income and losses to shareholders, who report them on their personal returns.

**Form 1065:** The U.S. Return of Partnership Income, filed annually by partnerships and multi-member LLCs taxed as partnerships.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The form sole proprietors and single-member LLCs taxed as disregarded entities attach to their personal Form 1040 to report business profit or loss.

**Form 941:** The Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return, which reports wages paid, income taxes withheld, and Social Security and Medicare (FICA) taxes for each quarter.

**Form 940:** The Employer's Annual Federal Unemployment (FUTA) Tax Return, filed once per year.

**Form W-2:** The Wage and Tax Statement an employer sends each employee by January 31 of the following year, showing total wages paid and taxes withheld.

**Form W-4:** The Employee's Withholding Certificate that each employee fills out so the employer knows how much federal income tax to withhold.

**Form 1099-NEC:** The form used to report payments of $2,000 or more made to non-employee contractors during a calendar year (for payments made on or after January 1, 2026, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act; threshold is inflation-adjusted after 2026). All contractor income remains taxable regardless of whether a 1099 is required.

**Form 4562:** Depreciation and Amortization. Used to claim deductions for business assets (equipment, vehicles, furniture) that are expensed over time rather than all at once.

**Form 8829:** Expenses for Business Use of Your Home, used to calculate the home office deduction for sole proprietors filing Schedule C.

**Basis:** Your tax cost in an asset. Basis starts at what you paid for the asset and is adjusted upward for improvements and downward for depreciation. You need basis records to calculate taxable gain or loss when you sell the asset.

**Depreciation:** The process of deducting the cost of a business asset gradually over its IRS-defined useful life rather than all in the year of purchase. Common depreciation forms: Form 4562.

**Accountable Plan:** A formal written reimbursement arrangement between a business and its employees (including owner-employees) that meets IRS rules so that reimbursements are not treated as taxable wages. The three IRS requirements are: (1) expenses must have a business connection, (2) employees must substantiate expenses within a reasonable time (generally 60 days), and (3) employees must return any excess advances within 120 days.

**EFTPS:** The Electronic Federal Tax Payment System, the IRS's online portal for submitting payroll tax deposits and other federal tax payments. You need your EFTPS deposit confirmation numbers in your records.

**EIN (Employer Identification Number):** The 9-digit tax ID number assigned by the IRS to a business. Also called the Federal Tax ID. You need your EIN on every business return and payroll form.

**ASED (Assessment Statute Expiration Date):** The exact calendar date on which the IRS's window to assess additional tax for a specific return year closes.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before setting up your Tax Vault. You do not need all of these to start, but knowing what you have helps you design the right folder structure.

1. Your business's legal name, EIN, and state of formation.
2. Your entity type (sole prop / Schedule C, S corp, C corp, partnership / LLC) -- this determines which forms and folders you need.
3. The tax year(s) you need to organize (current year only, or several back years?).
4. Your current storage method: paper, email folders, an accounting app, or scattered across multiple places.
5. Whether you have employees (determines whether you need payroll subfolders).
6. Whether you own business assets subject to depreciation (vehicles, equipment, real estate).
7. Whether you have an accountable plan for expense reimbursements.
8. Whether you own or use a home office.
9. Whether you use a personal vehicle for business.
10. The name and contact information for your tax preparer or advisor, so you can coordinate the vault hand-off at year-end.

## Required Documents And Records

The table below lists every major document category, what to collect, and how long to keep it. Retention periods are based on the IRS statute of limitations under IRC Section 6501 and IRS guidance in Publication 583 (Starting a Business and Keeping Records, December 2024 edition).

### Retention Quick Reference

| Situation | How Long to Keep |
|-----------|-----------------|
| General rule (income, deductions, credits) | 3 years from the date you filed, or 3 years from the return due date if you filed early |
| Amended return or refund claim | The later of: 3 years from original filing, or 2 years from the date you paid the tax |
| Unreported income exceeding 25% of gross income shown on the return | 6 years |
| Bad debt deduction or loss from worthless securities | 7 years |
| No return filed, or fraudulent return filed | Keep indefinitely |
| Employment tax records (Forms 940, 941, W-2, W-4) | At least 4 years after the tax becomes due or is paid, whichever is later |
| Employee Retention Credit and qualified sick/family leave wages documentation | 6 years |
| Asset (property) records | Keep until the statute of limitations expires for the year you sell or dispose of the asset -- potentially many years after purchase |
| Filed copies of business tax returns | Keep permanently (they help prepare future returns and verify prior-year numbers) |

Source: IRS Publication 583 (December 2024); IRS, "How Long Should I Keep Records?" (irs.gov).

### Document Categories and What to Collect

**Income Records**
- Bank deposit slips and bank statements
- Payment processor statements (Stripe, PayPal, Square, etc.)
- Sales invoices and receipts issued to clients
- Forms 1099-NEC or 1099-K received from clients or platforms
- Cash register tapes or point-of-sale reports
- Contracts evidencing income terms

**Expense Records**
- Receipts for every business purchase. For receipts under $75 (except lodging), a receipt is still best practice even though IRS Publication 463 sets the formal documentary evidence threshold at $75 for travel and entertainment expenses. Retain all receipts.
- Credit card and bank statements (as a backup cross-reference, not a substitute for receipts)
- Canceled checks or ACH confirmation records
- Vendor invoices and contracts
- Petty cash log if you maintain a petty cash fund

**Vehicle Records (if you use a vehicle for business)**
- A contemporaneous mileage log for every business trip showing: date, destination, business purpose, odometer start, odometer end, and miles driven
- Total annual odometer reading (to support the percentage of business use)
- Receipts for gas, maintenance, insurance, registration (if using actual-expense method)
- Documentation of the vehicle's purchase price, date placed in service, and any improvements (for depreciation and basis records)

**Travel, Meals, and Entertainment Records**
- Receipts for lodging, airfare, and transportation (showing name/location, dates, and cost breakdown)
- Meal receipts showing: name and location of restaurant, date, amount, and names/titles of all people present and their business relationship to you
- A written record of the business purpose for each travel trip and each meal
- For meals over $75, a receipt is required under the adequate-records standard of IRC Section 274(d)

**Payroll Records (if you have employees)**
- Signed Forms W-4 for each employee
- Records of all wages, salaries, bonuses, and other compensation paid, with dates
- Copies of filed Forms 941 (quarterly) and Form 940 (annual)
- Copies of W-2s issued to employees and the Form W-3 transmittal
- EFTPS deposit confirmation numbers and dates
- Records of sick pay, leave pay, and any non-cash compensation
- Employment dates for each worker

**Contractor / 1099 Records**
- Signed Form W-9 from each contractor before their first payment
- Payment records showing amount and date of each payment
- Copies of Forms 1099-NEC issued

**Asset and Depreciation Records**
- Purchase receipts, invoices, or closing statements for every depreciable asset
- Date each asset was placed in service
- Cost and any capitalized improvements
- Depreciation schedules (often produced by your tax software or preparer)
- Copies of filed Form 4562 for each year depreciation is claimed
- Records of disposal (sale, trade-in, or retirement) including sale price and date

**Entity and Ownership Records**
- Articles of Incorporation or Organization
- Operating Agreement (LLC) or Bylaws (corporation)
- S Corporation Election Form 2553 (if applicable), with IRS acceptance letter
- EIN assignment letter (IRS Letter 147C or CP 575)
- Shareholder or partner agreements and any updates

**Accountable Plan Records (if you reimburse yourself or employees for expenses)**
- The written accountable plan document itself
- Expense reports submitted with supporting receipts
- Proof that reimbursements were returned for any amounts not substantiated within the required time period

**Home Office Records (if applicable)**
- Documentation of the square footage of your office space and total home square footage (to support the business-use percentage)
- Utility bills, mortgage statements (showing interest and taxes), homeowner's or renter's insurance, and repair/maintenance receipts
- Photographs or floor plan showing the dedicated office area
- Copies of filed Form 8829 (for sole proprietors on Schedule C)

**Tax Returns and IRS Correspondence**
- Signed copies of every filed business and personal return, permanently
- IRS notices, letters, and your responses, permanently
- Copies of any Extensions of Time to File (Form 7004 for business returns, Form 4868 for personal)
- Proof of filing (electronic acknowledgment or certified mail receipt)

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Work through these steps in order. Allow about 2 to 4 hours for the initial setup, then roughly 15 to 30 minutes per month to maintain the vault going forward.

**Step 1: Choose your storage medium.**

Decide whether your vault will be digital only, physical only, or a hybrid. Digital is strongly recommended as the primary system because files can be backed up, searched, and shared with your advisor without mailing anything. Physical binders work as a backup. Common digital options:

- A folder structure on your computer backed up to a cloud service (Google Drive, Dropbox, iCloud)
- A dedicated cloud folder shared with your advisor
- A document management app (some accounting platforms include document storage)

Whichever you choose, the folder names and logic below apply to both.

**Step 2: Create the top-level folder structure.**

Create one folder per tax year. Label it with the year the income and expenses occurred, not the year you file (you file your 2026 return in early 2027, so the folder is "2026").

```
Tax Vault/
  2026/
  2025/
  2024/
  Permanent/
```

The "Permanent" folder holds documents that do not expire: entity formation documents, your EIN letter, S corp election and IRS acceptance, and all filed tax returns.

**Step 3: Create the sub-folders inside each year's folder.**

Inside each year folder, create these eight sub-folders:

```
2026/
  01 - Income
  02 - Expenses
  03 - Vehicle
  04 - Travel and Meals
  05 - Payroll and Contractors
  06 - Assets and Depreciation
  07 - Home Office
  08 - Tax Returns and Correspondence
```

The number prefix forces the folders to sort in a logical order. Adjust or remove sub-folders that do not apply to your business (for example, skip "07 - Home Office" if you have a separate commercial office).

**Step 4: Add sub-folders inside "02 - Expenses" by category.**

Break expenses into logical sub-folders that mirror the categories on your tax return. Common categories:

```
02 - Expenses/
  Advertising and Marketing
  Bank and Merchant Fees
  Insurance (Business)
  Legal and Professional Fees
  Meals (Business)
  Office Supplies
  Rent (Business Property)
  Software and Subscriptions
  Telephone and Internet
  Utilities
  Other
```

You do not need a sub-folder for every line item on your return -- group minor categories into "Other" to keep the system manageable.

**Step 5: Establish a weekly receipt capture habit.**

Pick one day each week (many clients use Friday) to do the following:

1. Forward all email receipts from your inbox into the correct vault sub-folder, or save them as PDFs named with the date and vendor (for example: "2026-03-14 Adobe Subscription.pdf").
2. Photograph any paper receipts with your phone (free apps like Adobe Scan or your phone's built-in document scanner work well) and save the image into the correct sub-folder.
3. Update your mileage log if you drove for business that week.
4. Log any meals or travel if they occurred that week, noting the business purpose and who attended.

**Step 6: Reconcile monthly.**

At the end of each month, spend 15 to 30 minutes doing the following:

1. Download your bank and credit card statements for the month and save them to "01 - Income" and "02 - Expenses" as appropriate.
2. Compare your downloaded statements to your receipt folder. Every transaction on the statement should have a corresponding receipt or explanation.
3. Note any missing receipts. For amounts under $75, reconstruct from bank records if needed and add a brief written note of the business purpose. For amounts $75 or more, make a genuine effort to retrieve the receipt from the vendor.
4. Save your EFTPS payment confirmations (if you make payroll tax deposits) to "05 - Payroll and Contractors."

**Step 7: File payroll and contractor documents as they occur.**

- Each quarter, file your signed and filed Form 941 and proof of payroll tax deposits into "05 - Payroll and Contractors."
- Each January, file W-2s issued to employees and 1099-NEC forms issued to contractors into "05 - Payroll and Contractors" under a "Year-End" sub-folder.
- File each contractor's W-9 when you receive it, before their first payment.

**Step 8: Document every asset purchase at the time of purchase.**

When you buy a business asset (equipment, furniture, a vehicle, software with a multi-year license), immediately save the following to "06 - Assets and Depreciation":

- The invoice or purchase receipt
- Proof of payment
- A brief note identifying the date placed in service and the business purpose

If you already have assets in service from prior years, gather their original purchase documents and create entries in your vault for those years or in the "Permanent" folder if the statute has not yet run.

**Step 9: Complete the Tax Vault File Structure Implementation Worksheet (the attached download).**

The worksheet walks you through confirming each folder has been created, identifying which document categories apply to your business, and recording the name and location of your vault so anyone can access it in an emergency. Complete the worksheet after completing Steps 1 through 8. See "Using The Download(s)" below for details.

**Step 10: Hand off to your advisor at year-end.**

By January 15 of the following year (for the prior tax year), share your completed vault with your advisor. Options:

- Grant view access to your cloud folder
- Export the folder as a compressed archive and share via secure file transfer
- Provide a physical binder if your system is paper-based

Tell your advisor the folder structure you used so they can navigate it without explanation. The Tax Vault File Structure Implementation Worksheet documents this for them.

**Step 11: Archive the completed year and start the next one.**

Once your tax return is filed and you have a copy of the filed return saved to "08 - Tax Returns and Correspondence," the year is complete. Do not delete the folder. Archive it (compress or move to a "Completed Years" folder) and retain it according to the retention schedule in "Required Documents And Records" above.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity type changes the required folders and forms.**
An S corporation owner must keep payroll records for their own W-2 wages (reasonable compensation), in addition to all other employee payroll records. A sole proprietor uses Schedule C and Form 8829 (home office) rather than separate corporate returns. A partnership files Form 1065 and issues Schedule K-1 to each partner. Make sure your vault structure matches your entity type and that you are keeping the right forms.

**State requirements may be longer or stricter than federal.**
Several states have their own income tax audit statutes that run longer than the federal 3-year period, or have separate payroll tax retention requirements. This playbook covers only federal IRS requirements. Confirm your state's specific retention rules with your advisor.

**The statute of limitations does not start running until a return is filed.**
If you filed late, the 3-year (or 6-year or 7-year) clock starts from the actual filing date, not the due date. If you never filed a required return, the statute never expires and the IRS can assess tax at any time.

**Digital records must be accessible and reproducible.**
The IRS requires that electronic records be stored in a way that allows them to be retrieved and reproduced in a legible format on request. Revenue Procedure 98-25 and Revenue Procedure 97-22 set the IRS's standards for electronic recordkeeping systems. Storing records in a cloud drive that you control satisfies this requirement. Using only the "share" link in another person's account -- where you could lose access -- does not.

**Backup your digital vault.**
A single digital copy is not enough. Follow the 3-2-1 rule as a best practice: 3 copies, on 2 different media types, with 1 stored off-site (for example, your computer's hard drive plus a cloud backup plus an external drive kept at a separate location). Note: this is a general technology best practice, not an IRS requirement.

**QBI deduction is now permanent.**
The Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction under IRC Section 199A, which allows eligible pass-through business owners to deduct up to 20% of qualified business income, was made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025), effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. The deduction rate remains 20%. The Act also added a new $400 minimum deduction for taxpayers with at least $1,000 of QBI from a qualified trade or business in which the taxpayer materially participates, and raised the phase-in thresholds to $75,000 (single filers) and $150,000 (married filing jointly), both indexed for inflation after 2026. Your income, wage, and qualified-property records supporting the QBI calculation remain important for 2026 and all future years. Confirm the specifics of your calculation with your advisor.

**TCJA provisions and legislative changes.**
Several Tax Cuts and Jobs Act provisions were set to expire or change for 2026 and beyond. Keep your advisor informed of any major business changes during the year so they can advise on whether your record categories need to be updated.

**New or changing payroll rates each year.**
Social Security wage base limits, FUTA credit reduction rates, and state unemployment tax rates change annually. This playbook does not state specific payroll tax rates for 2026 because they are not vault-structure issues -- your payroll system should track them. Confirm current rates with your advisor or your payroll provider.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Waiting until December (or tax season) to organize the year.**
Reconstructing a year's worth of receipts from memory and bank statements is slow, error-prone, and produces records that carry less weight with the IRS than contemporaneous records. Spend 15 to 30 minutes per week to avoid this.

**2. Treating a bank statement as a substitute for a receipt.**
Bank and credit card statements prove that a payment was made, but they do not prove business purpose, who was there for a meal, or what a purchase was for. Keep the receipt and the statement.

**3. Using a single folder for all years.**
When the IRS audits a return, it asks for documents related to a specific year. A single undifferentiated folder forces you (and your advisor) to search through everything. One folder per year, clearly labeled, solves this.

**4. Keeping records only for the "3-year rule" when a longer period applies.**
Clients who underreport income (even accidentally) face a 6-year audit window. Clients with asset sales need records going back to the original purchase date, which could be 10 or 20 years. Follow the retention schedule in "Required Documents And Records" rather than defaulting to 3 years for everything.

**5. Failing to capture mileage logs contemporaneously.**
Vehicle mileage logs reconstructed at year-end from Google Maps history or calendar entries carry significant audit risk. The IRS expects logs that were created at the time of each trip. Start a mileage log app (or a paper log) and fill it in trip by trip.

**6. Missing W-9s before paying contractors.**
If you pay a contractor $2,000 or more in a year (the 2026 reportable-payment threshold under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act) and do not have a Form W-9 on file, you must withhold 24% of all payments as backup withholding and remit it to the IRS. Collecting the W-9 before the first payment eliminates this problem. Note that even payments below $2,000 are fully taxable to the contractor -- the threshold affects your reporting obligation, not the contractor's tax liability.

**7. Discarding documents too soon.**
Business owners often discard records after three years assuming the audit window is closed. But if income was underreported, or if the documents relate to property not yet sold, the statute may still be open. When in doubt, keep it.

**8. No backup copies of the vault.**
A hard drive failure or accidental deletion can destroy years of records. Cloud backup is not optional -- it is the minimum safety measure.

**9. Not documenting the business purpose of expenses.**
The IRS requires that the business purpose of each travel trip, meal, and significant expense be documented. "Dinner - business" is not sufficient. "Dinner with [client role] to discuss [project type] -- client relationship and project scope" is. Write the purpose at the time of the expense, not at tax time.

**10. Confusing personal and business accounts in the vault.**
Personal receipts and business receipts must be completely separate in your vault. Commingling makes it difficult to prove which expenses were legitimately business-related and can invite IRS scrutiny.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and contact your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

1. **You discover a gap year.** If you have years where tax returns were not filed, do not reconstruct records or attempt to file on your own. Talk to your advisor first about the best strategy for coming into compliance.

2. **You receive any IRS notice or letter.** Do not respond to an IRS notice without your advisor's review, even if it looks routine. Bring the notice and your vault files to your advisor within 10 days of receiving it.

3. **You are considering changing your entity type.** A change from sole prop to S corp, or S corp to C corp, changes every form in your vault structure and has significant payroll, recordkeeping, and tax consequences. Review with your advisor before making the change.

4. **You are buying or selling a significant business asset.** Asset sales require calculating your adjusted basis (original cost plus improvements minus depreciation already claimed), which requires records going back to the purchase date. Your advisor needs those records before the transaction closes.

5. **You are starting or ending an accountable plan.** The plan must be in writing before any reimbursements are made under it. Your advisor can help you document and implement the plan correctly.

6. **You have employees and are setting up payroll for the first time.** Federal and state payroll registration, withholding setup, and deposit schedules involve deadlines and penalties that your advisor or a payroll service provider should help you navigate.

7. **You are not sure which retention period applies to a specific document.** When a document category falls into a gray area -- for example, records related to an asset you sold that was originally purchased many years ago -- ask your advisor before discarding anything.

8. **The QBI deduction (IRC Section 199A) is permanent for 2026 and beyond.** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025) made the deduction permanent at the 20% rate with new phase-in thresholds ($75,000 single / $150,000 MFJ) and a $400 minimum deduction for taxpayers with at least $1,000 of QBI. Ask your advisor what records you need to support your specific QBI calculation (qualified business income, W-2 wages paid, and qualified property values), as the specifics vary by entity type and income level.

## Before You Act

Before you finalize your vault structure and begin using it as your primary recordkeeping system, confirm the following with your advisor:

- Your entity type and the specific tax forms you file each year, so your folder structure matches your actual filing requirements.
- Your state's record retention rules, which may be longer than the federal periods described in this playbook.
- Whether you are subject to any special industry-specific recordkeeping requirements (for example, healthcare businesses, government contractors, and financial services firms often have rules beyond the IRS's general requirements).
- What records you need to support your QBI deduction calculation under IRC Section 199A, which is permanent for 2026 and beyond (made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025). The deduction rate is 20% of qualified business income, subject to phase-in thresholds and entity-specific rules; the records required (qualified business income, W-2 wages paid, qualified property) vary by your situation. Confirm the specifics with your advisor.
- Whether your accountable plan is currently in writing and whether the plan document needs to be updated.

Do not discard any existing records until your advisor confirms the applicable retention period for those specific documents has passed.
