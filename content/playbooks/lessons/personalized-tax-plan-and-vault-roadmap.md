# Personalized Tax Plan and Vault Roadmap

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

A personalized tax plan is a written, strategy-by-strategy action list built around your specific business structure, income level, and family situation -- it is the difference between reacting to a tax bill and engineering a lower one in advance. A tax vault is the organized collection of documents, receipts, elections, agreements, and records that proves each strategy to the IRS if you are ever examined. This playbook walks you through how to build both: how to translate the general strategies you have learned into a written plan with dollar amounts attached to your facts, and how to create a vault that makes those strategies bulletproof. The download (Planning Roadmap and Vault Review Implementation Worksheet) is the fill-in tool you will complete during and after each step.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook applies to you if:

- You are a Beyond Freedom Financial client who has completed at least one strategy module (for example, the S-corp election, accountable plan, home office, Augusta Rule, or hiring your children) and want to pull all active strategies into a single coordinated plan.
- You own a pass-through business -- a sole proprietorship, single-member LLC, multi-member LLC, S-corporation, or partnership -- where business income flows to your personal Form 1040.
- Your expected 2026 federal income tax liability (before applying strategies) is high enough that proactive planning produces a measurable savings: generally, net business income above $75,000 per year.
- You have not yet organized your strategy documentation into a single file or folder (your vault) that you could hand to an auditor today.
- You want an annual rhythm -- not just a one-time fix -- for updating your plan as income changes.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **W-2 employees with no business income:** Your employer controls most of your tax levers. A personalized plan built around business strategies will not apply until you have self-employment or business income.
- **New businesses with no history:** If your business is in its first 90 days of operation, you likely do not yet have reliable income projections. Wait until you have at least one quarter of actual numbers, then return to this playbook.
- **Clients who have not yet implemented any strategy:** This playbook assumes you have at least one strategy in progress. If you are starting from zero, begin with the Business Owner Tax Mindset lesson, then return here.
- **Taxpayers whose only income is from a C-corporation salary or dividends:** C-corporation owners face a separate layer of entity-level tax that requires C-corp specific planning beyond the scope of this roadmap.
- **Situations involving back taxes, unfiled returns, or active IRS notices:** Resolve those issues first with your advisor before focusing on proactive planning; an installment agreement or offer in compromise affects how you prioritize cash flow.

## Key Terms

**Tax plan (for purposes of this playbook):** A written document, updated at least annually, that lists every legal strategy you are implementing, estimates the tax savings from each, sets the action deadline for each, and identifies the person responsible for completing each step. It is not a generic checklist -- it contains your income numbers, your entity structure, and your specific filing situation.

**Tax vault:** A physical folder or organized digital folder (or both) that holds the written documentation, signed agreements, receipts, elections, and records that support every strategy on your tax plan. The vault is what you would hand to an IRS examiner to prove each deduction or election is real. Without the vault, a strategy you implemented correctly may still be disallowed because you cannot prove it.

**Pass-through entity:** A business structure -- sole proprietorship, partnership, LLC, or S-corporation -- in which business income is not taxed at the entity level but instead "passes through" to the owner's personal Form 1040 and is taxed at the owner's individual rates.

**S-corporation (S-corp):** A corporation (or LLC that has elected to be treated as a corporation) that has made an election under IRC Section 1362 to be taxed as a pass-through entity. The owner-employee pays reasonable compensation (a W-2 salary) and takes additional profit as distributions, which are not subject to self-employment tax or the employer side of FICA.

**Reasonable compensation:** The wage that the IRS expects an S-corp owner to pay themselves for the services they perform for the corporation -- it must be comparable to what an unrelated employer would pay someone to do the same work. Set too low, it is a red flag that invites IRS reclassification of distributions as wages.

**Qualified Business Income Deduction (QBID):** A deduction under IRC Section 199A allowing eligible owners of pass-through businesses to deduct up to 20% of their qualified business income (QBI) from taxable income, thereby reducing the income tax rate on business profits without affecting self-employment tax. Made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, July 4, 2025). A minimum deduction of $400 is available if you have at least $1,000 in QBI and materially participate.

**Qualified Business Income (QBI):** The net amount of income, gain, deduction, and loss from a qualified trade or business conducted within the United States. W-2 wages you pay yourself, capital gains, and investment income are excluded from QBI. Calculated on Form 8995 (simple cases) or Form 8995-A (complex or over-threshold cases).

**Specified Service Trade or Business (SSTB):** A category of professional service businesses -- including law, health, consulting, financial services, athletics, brokerage, and performing arts -- whose QBID is limited or eliminated once income exceeds the phase-out range. Architecture and engineering are excluded from the SSTB category.

**Accountable plan:** A written employer reimbursement policy that meets IRS requirements under Treasury Regulation 1.62-2, allowing a business to reimburse an employee (including an owner-employee) for business expenses without those reimbursements being included in the employee's taxable wages. Requires a written plan, business purpose documentation, adequate accounting, and return of excess reimbursements.

**Augusta Rule (IRC Section 280A(g)):** A provision that allows a homeowner to rent their home for up to 14 days per year and exclude the rental income from gross income entirely. When a business entity rents the owner's home for legitimate business meetings, the business deducts the rent as an ordinary business expense and the owner receives tax-free income.

**Home office deduction:** A deduction under IRC Section 280A for a portion of home expenses (mortgage interest or rent, utilities, insurance, repairs, and depreciation) proportional to the square footage used exclusively and regularly for business. Available to self-employed persons and to S-corp owner-employees through an accountable plan reimbursement.

**Health Savings Account (HSA):** A tax-advantaged savings account available only to individuals enrolled in a High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP). Contributions are deductible (or pre-tax if made through payroll), investment growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free -- a triple tax benefit. Governed by IRC Section 223.

**High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP):** A health insurance plan that meets IRS-defined minimums for annual deductibles and maximums for out-of-pocket costs. For 2026, the minimum annual deductible is $1,700 for self-only coverage and $3,400 for family coverage; the maximum out-of-pocket limit is $8,500 for self-only and $17,000 for family.

**Self-employment (SE) tax:** The 15.3% tax that self-employed individuals pay on net earnings in place of the employee-and-employer FICA taxes. It consists of 12.4% Social Security (on earnings up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500) and 2.9% Medicare (on all earnings). S-corp profit distributions are not subject to SE tax; only the W-2 salary portion is subject to FICA. Note: SE tax is not computed on 100% of net earnings. Net self-employment earnings are first multiplied by 92.35% (= 100% minus the employer-equivalent deduction of 7.65%) before the 15.3% rate is applied. The practical effect is that the effective SE tax rate on gross net earnings is approximately 14.13%, not 15.3%. Your tax software will perform this calculation automatically; the 15.3% rate in this playbook and worksheet is a conservative approximation. For exact figures, use your actual Schedule SE or confirm with your advisor.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** An additional 3.8% tax on net investment income (interest, dividends, capital gains, passive rental income, and annuities) for taxpayers whose modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) exceeds $200,000 (single), $250,000 (married filing jointly), or $125,000 (married filing separately). These thresholds are not indexed for inflation.

**Section 199A phase-out range:** The income range above which the QBID begins to be reduced for SSTB owners (and above which the W-2 wage limitation begins to apply for non-SSTB owners). For 2026: $403,500 to $553,500 (married filing jointly); $201,750 to $276,750 (single and other filers). SSTB owners with income above the top of the range receive no QBID.

**Long-term capital gain:** The gain from selling a capital asset (stock, real estate, business interest) held for more than one year. Taxed at preferential rates (0%, 15%, or 20%) rather than ordinary income rates, depending on total taxable income. For 2026, the 0% rate applies to taxable income up to $49,450 (single), $98,900 (married filing jointly), or $66,200 (head of household); the 15% rate applies up to $545,500 (single), $613,700 (MFJ), or $579,600 (HOH); the 20% rate applies above those amounts. See "The Numbers" section for the full table.

**Estimated tax:** Quarterly prepayments of income and self-employment tax on income not subject to employer withholding. Required when you expect to owe $1,000 or more after withholding. Paid via IRS Direct Pay at directpay.irs.gov or through IRS Online Account at irs.gov/account.

**Form 1040:** The U.S. Individual Income Tax Return -- the primary federal tax return for individuals, through which all pass-through business income, deductions, and credits ultimately flow.

**Form 1120-S:** The U.S. Income Tax Return for an S corporation. Filed annually; generates Schedule K-1 forms that report each shareholder's share of income, deductions, and credits.

**Schedule K-1:** The informational return that flows each partner's or S-corp shareholder's share of income, loss, and deductions to their personal Form 1040. It is the bridge between the entity-level return and the owner's personal return.

**Form 8995 / Form 8995-A:** IRS forms used to calculate the QBID. Form 8995 is for simpler cases (income below the phase-in threshold). Form 8995-A is for taxpayers whose income exceeds the threshold or who own multiple businesses.

**SALT deduction:** The itemized deduction under IRC Section 164 for state and local income taxes (or sales taxes) and property taxes paid. For 2026, capped at $40,400 for most filers ($20,200 if married filing separately), subject to a phase-out for high-income taxpayers. Claimed on Schedule A.

**Depreciation:** The annual deduction for the cost of a tangible business asset spread over its IRS-defined useful life under MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System). Accelerated depreciation options include Section 179 expensing and bonus depreciation.

**Bonus depreciation:** An accelerated first-year depreciation deduction under IRC Section 168(k). The One Big Beautiful Bill Act restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying property placed in service after January 19, 2025. Qualifying property must have a recovery period of 20 years or less; real property does not qualify (with limited exceptions for qualified improvement property).

**Cost segregation:** An engineering study that reclassifies components of a real property building from 39-year (or 27.5-year residential) depreciation into shorter-life personal property categories (5, 7, or 15 years), allowing faster depreciation deductions. Often combined with bonus depreciation to accelerate large write-offs in the acquisition year.

**Basis:** Your investment in an asset for tax purposes. Selling or distributing an asset with a low basis triggers a large taxable gain; assets stepped up to fair market value at death pass to heirs with a new, higher basis that eliminates the embedded gain.

**Tax year:** The 12-month period over which your income and deductions are measured. For most individuals and businesses, the tax year is the calendar year (January 1 -- December 31).

## Required Client Inputs

Gather all of the following before you begin filling in the Planning Roadmap and Vault Review Implementation Worksheet:

1. **Most recent filed federal return (Form 1040 + all schedules):** From this you need: (a) total federal income tax liability (line 24), (b) adjusted gross income (line 11), (c) taxable income (line 15), (d) your filing status, and (e) whether you itemized or took the standard deduction.

2. **Most recent S-corp or partnership return (Form 1120-S or Form 1065 + Schedule K-1), if applicable:** From this you need: (a) total gross revenue, (b) officer compensation paid, (c) net ordinary business income, and (d) total distributions.

3. **Current-year income projection:** Your best estimate of 2026 gross revenue, anticipated expenses, and projected net profit. If you have actual figures through the most recent completed quarter, use those plus estimates for the remainder of the year.

4. **Payroll summary, if you are on payroll:** Year-to-date W-2 wages paid to yourself or to family employees, plus any employer payroll tax amounts.

5. **List of strategies currently in place:** For each strategy, note whether documentation is complete (signed agreement, receipts, written policy) or still in progress.

6. **List of strategies you intend to implement this year but have not yet started.**

7. **Personal filing information:** Filing status (single, married filing jointly, etc.), number of dependents, ages of any children employed in the business or covered by an HSA or educational plan.

8. **State of residence and any states where the business operates:** State income tax treatment of pass-through income varies significantly. Your advisor will confirm whether your state conforms to federal rules for each strategy.

9. **Bank statements or QuickBooks export showing year-to-date business expenses** (to verify that claimed deductions are supported by actual transactions).

10. **Copies of any IRS notices, prior audit findings, or open tax issues** so the plan can account for heightened scrutiny areas.

**General retention rule:** The IRS standard statute of limitations for assessment is 3 years from the return due date or filing date, whichever is later. The period extends to 6 years if you omit more than 25% of gross income. There is no statute of limitations for fraud or for a return that was never filed. The IRS recommends retaining records that support any item on a return until the period of limitations for that return expires.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Work through these steps in order. Use the Planning Roadmap and Vault Review Implementation Worksheet to record your entries and track completion.

### Phase 1: Take Stock of Your Current Position

**Step 1: Assemble last year's return and current projections.**
Pull your most recent filed Form 1040 and, if applicable, your Form 1120-S or Form 1065 with Schedule K-1. On the worksheet, record: (a) prior-year taxable income, (b) prior-year total tax liability, and (c) your estimated 2026 net business income. This gives you the baseline against which you will measure savings.

**Step 2: Identify your current effective and marginal tax rates.**
Your effective rate is total tax divided by total taxable income. Your marginal rate is the bracket your next dollar of income falls into (see "The Numbers" for 2026 brackets). Write both on the worksheet. Strategies that reduce your marginal-bracket income produce the highest dollar savings per dollar of deduction.

**Step 3: Confirm your entity structure is correct for your income level.**
If you are operating as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC and your net profit consistently exceeds $40,000 to $50,000, ask your advisor whether S-corp election makes sense. If you already have an S-corp, confirm that your reasonable compensation level is documented and defensible. Record the current structure on the worksheet.

**Step 4: Confirm whether you currently itemize or take the standard deduction.**
The 2026 standard deduction is $32,200 (married filing jointly), $16,100 (single), or $24,150 (head of household). If your itemized deductions (mortgage interest, SALT up to $40,400, charitable contributions, etc.) do not exceed the standard deduction, strategies that reduce above-the-line income (IRA contributions, HSA contributions, S-corp salary level) produce greater benefit than strategies that add below-the-line itemized deductions.

### Phase 2: Build Your Strategy Stack

**Step 5: List every strategy currently in place and rate each one's documentation as Green (complete), Yellow (partial), or Red (not yet documented).**
Use the worksheet table to record each strategy, its estimated annual savings, and its documentation status. Examples: S-corp election, accountable plan, home office, Augusta Rule, hiring children, HSA, retirement plan contributions. Any strategy rated Yellow or Red is a liability risk -- you are claiming the deduction without the proof to defend it.

**Step 6: Calculate the projected savings for each active strategy using your actual income numbers.**
Work through each strategy one at a time:

- **S-corp salary optimization:** Multiply the difference between your actual distributions and your W-2 salary by 15.3% (SE tax) as a conservative approximation. Because SE tax is technically computed on 92.35% of net earnings (not the full 100%), the precise savings are slightly lower than a simple multiplication implies -- but using 15.3% as a planning estimate is standard practice and errs on the side of caution. If your distributions are $120,000 and your salary is $80,000, the S-corp structure potentially saves approximately $6,120 in FICA/SE tax on the $40,000 distribution (15.3% x $40,000 = $6,120 as a conservative estimate). Your advisor will confirm the exact calculation based on your reasonable compensation analysis.
- **QBID (Section 199A deduction):** Multiply your net QBI by 20%. If your net QBI is $100,000, the maximum QBID is $20,000. At a 24% marginal rate, that represents $4,800 in tax savings. The calculation is more complex if your income is above the phase-in threshold -- use Form 8995-A and confirm with your advisor.
- **Accountable plan:** Add up all business expense reimbursements you expect the company will pay to you this year (home office, mileage, cell phone, equipment, professional development). Multiply by your combined federal marginal income tax rate plus, if you are still on Schedule C (sole proprietor), your SE tax rate. That is the gross annual savings from routing those expenses through the plan rather than claiming them as unreimbursed personal expenses.
- **Augusta Rule:** Multiply the number of rental days (up to 14) by your documented market daily rate. That is the business's deduction and your tax-free income. A $500/day rate for 10 days = $5,000 deduction for the business and $5,000 tax-free to you. The income does not appear on your return; you do not pay federal income tax on it.
- **Hiring your children:** Add up the wages paid to qualifying children. Wages paid to children under age 18 by a sole proprietorship (or a partnership where both partners are the child's parents) are exempt from Social Security and Medicare (FICA) taxes. Additionally, wages paid to children under age 21 by a sole proprietor or an all-parent partnership are exempt from FUTA (federal unemployment tax) under IRC Section 3306(c)(5) -- note that the FUTA exemption extends two years further than the FICA exemption. Children who are claimed as your dependents can earn up to $16,100 in 2026 before owing federal income tax: a dependent child's standard deduction equals the greater of $1,400 or their earned income, capped at the single standard deduction of $16,100 (per Rev. Proc. 2025-32 and IRC Section 63(c)(5)). Your business deducts the wages.
- **HSA contributions:** Multiply the annual HSA contribution amount by your marginal income tax rate to estimate the federal tax savings. If you contribute $4,400 (self-only) and your marginal rate is 24%, the federal savings is approximately $1,056, plus savings on any state income tax.
- **Retirement plan contributions:** Multiply total contributions (your elective deferral plus employer contributions) by your combined federal and state marginal rate to estimate the annual deferral savings. These are not permanent savings -- you pay tax when you withdraw -- but deferral at a high rate and withdrawal at a lower rate produces a real benefit.

Record each savings estimate on the worksheet.

**Step 7: Identify strategies you are eligible for but have not yet implemented.**
Review the following and note any that apply to your situation but are not yet active: solo 401(k), SEP-IRA, defined benefit plan, cost segregation on business real estate, S-corp election, health insurance deduction as self-employed person or S-corp shareholder, vehicle deductions (actual expense vs. standard mileage rate), meals deduction for business meals (50% deductible under IRC Section 274), and education or professional development deductions. For each one that looks applicable, mark it "to implement" on the worksheet and set a deadline.

**Step 8: Prioritize new strategies by deadline sensitivity and savings magnitude.**
Not all strategies can be implemented after December 31. Some have hard deadlines:
- S-corp election for an existing LLC: generally must be filed by March 15 of the tax year for which it is to be effective (or within 75 days of the date of the triggering event for a late election).
- SEP-IRA contributions: can be made through the extended due date of the return (October 15 for individuals who filed an extension).
- Solo 401(k): the plan must be established by December 31 of the tax year; contributions can be made through October 15.
- Augusta Rule meeting rentals: the 14 rental days must occur within the current calendar year.
- Accountable plan reimbursements: must be submitted and paid within a reasonable time (generally within 60 days of the expense being incurred, per Treasury Reg. 1.62-2(g)).

Rank each "to implement" strategy by: (1) estimated savings and (2) proximity of the deadline. Tackle deadline-sensitive items first.

### Phase 3: Build and Review Your Vault

**Step 9: Create your vault folder structure.**
Whether physical or digital (a password-protected cloud folder is fine), create one folder per active strategy. Inside each folder, place only what directly supports that strategy. Suggested structure:

```
TAX VAULT -- [Your Name] -- 2026
  /S-Corp Election
    - Form 2553 IRS acceptance letter
    - Reasonable compensation analysis
    - Shareholder meeting minutes
  /Accountable Plan
    - Signed accountable plan policy
    - Monthly reimbursement reports with receipts
  /Home Office
    - Square footage calculation worksheet
    - Floor plan diagram or photo
    - Annual home expense summary
  /Augusta Rule
    - Rental agreement (business to homeowner)
    - Meeting agendas and attendee lists
    - Market rate documentation
  /Payroll -- [Child's Name]
    - Employment agreement
    - Job description
    - Timesheets
    - Payroll records and W-2
  /HSA
    - HDHP enrollment confirmation
    - HSA trustee account statements
    - Medical expense receipts (optional -- you may keep these separately)
  /Retirement Plan
    - Plan documents (adoption agreement)
    - Annual contribution records
  /Tax Returns
    - Filed Form 1040 (all years, last 7)
    - Filed Form 1120-S or 1065 (all years, last 7)
    - Schedule K-1s
  /Estimated Tax Payments
    - Payment confirmations from IRS Direct Pay
```

**Step 10: Conduct a vault review for each strategy you have rated Yellow or Red.**
For each under-documented strategy, answer these four questions on the worksheet:

1. Is there a written agreement or written policy? (If not, create one now -- a retroactive agreement created after an audit is not effective.)
2. Is there a contemporaneous paper trail showing the transaction occurred? (Receipts, invoices, bank statements, calendar entries with business purpose noted.)
3. Does the amount claimed match the supporting documentation?
4. Would an unrelated third party reviewing this file understand what the expense was for and why it was a legitimate business expense?

Any "no" answer requires immediate corrective action before the tax return is filed.

**Step 11: Fix gaps before filing.**
Common gaps and their fixes:

- **No written accountable plan policy:** Your advisor or attorney can draft one retroactively for future use, but it cannot be backdated to cover reimbursements that have already been paid without a policy in place. Going forward, get it signed before the next reimbursement cycle.
- **Augusta Rule meetings not documented:** Agendas and attendee lists can often be reconstructed from calendar records and email threads if the meetings genuinely occurred. A reconstruction note acknowledging it is not a contemporaneous record is better than nothing, but contemporaneous documentation is far stronger.
- **No comparable salary analysis for S-corp reasonable compensation:** Commission a wage analysis (compensation surveys for your industry and geography are available from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, trade associations, and professional HR databases) and document it in writing.
- **No proof of HDHP enrollment:** Request plan documents and enrollment confirmation from your insurance carrier or employer's HR department.

**Step 12: Date-stamp and sign all corrections.**
Any document created or revised to fill a vault gap must be dated with the actual date of creation, not backdated to the date of the original transaction. A document with an accurate creation date and a note explaining that it reconstructs a prior event is legally defensible; a backdated document is not and may constitute fraud.

### Phase 4: Set Your Planning Cadence

**Step 13: Schedule four quarterly vault-and-plan check-ins.**
Each quarter, block 60 to 90 minutes with your advisor to review: (a) whether income projections are still on track, (b) whether estimated tax payments need to be adjusted, (c) whether any strategy needs documentation refreshed before year-end, and (d) whether any new opportunity has emerged (a large equipment purchase, a new hire, a rental opportunity). Recommended schedule for tax year 2026: mid-February (post-filing review), mid-May, mid-August, mid-November (year-end planning).

**Step 14: Set calendar reminders for every hard deadline.**
At minimum:
- January 31: W-2s and 1099-NECs due to recipients.
- March 15: S-corp and partnership return deadline (or extension request); also the deadline to make an S-corp election for a new entity to be effective for 2026.
- April 15: Individual return due (or extension request); Q1 estimated tax payment due.
- June 16: Q2 estimated tax payment due.
- September 15: Q3 estimated tax payment due; S-corp and partnership extended returns due.
- October 15: Individual extended return due; SEP-IRA contribution deadline (if extension filed).
- December 31: Solo 401(k) plan establishment deadline; Augusta Rule rental day cutoff; last day to complete year-end payroll for child employees.

**Step 15: Assign ownership for each action item.**
For each item on the worksheet, designate: (a) who will do it (you, your advisor, your bookkeeper, your payroll provider), (b) what the deliverable is, and (c) by what date. Tax planning fails more often from lack of follow-through than from lack of good ideas.

**Step 16: Complete the Planning Roadmap and Vault Review Implementation Worksheet and send a copy to your advisor.**
The worksheet is your implementation contract with yourself. Share it with your advisor at your next meeting so they can flag any items that require their direct involvement before you act.

## Timing And Deadlines

**Year-round strategies (no annual deadline, but must be in place before expenses are incurred):**
- Accountable plan policy (must be in writing before the first reimbursement)
- Home office exclusivity requirement (must be maintained all year)
- Child employment (timesheets must be kept contemporaneously)
- HDHP enrollment (must be in force on the first day of each month you want HSA eligibility)

**Calendar year hard deadlines (cannot be backdated or extended):**
- Augusta Rule rental days: must occur on or before December 31 of the tax year.
- Solo 401(k) plan establishment: by December 31 of the tax year (contributions may follow by October 15 of the following year if an extension is filed).
- S-corp reasonable compensation review: should be completed before the last payroll of the year and documented before filing Form 1120-S.

**Return and payment deadlines for tax year 2026:**
- January 31, 2027: W-2s to employees and 1099-NECs to contractors.
- March 15, 2027: Form 1120-S and Form 1065 due; S-corp and partnership extensions available (to September 15, 2027).
- April 15, 2027: Form 1040 due; Q1 2027 estimated tax payment due.
- October 15, 2027: Individual extended return due; deadline for SEP-IRA and traditional IRA contributions for 2026 (if extension filed for SEP-IRA; IRA contributions must be made by the original April 15 due date regardless of extension).

**Note on IRA contributions:** Traditional and Roth IRA contributions for tax year 2026 must be made by April 15, 2027, regardless of whether you file an extension for your return. The extension applies to the return, not to the IRA contribution deadline.

- **Phase-in range (W-2 wage limitation begins for non-SSTBs; SSTB deduction begins phasing out):** $403,500 to $553,500 (MFJ) / $201,750 to $276,750 (all others)
- **SSTB total exclusion above:** $553,500 (MFJ) / $276,750 (all others)
- **Forms:** Form 8995 (under threshold); Form 8995-A (at or above threshold)
- **Status:** Made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, sec. 70105, July 4, 2025); $400 minimum deduction established by the same act

### SALT Deduction Cap (2026)
- **Standard cap:** $40,400 for most filers ($20,200 for married filing separately)
- **Phase-out:** The cap is reduced if MAGI exceeds $505,000 ($252,500 MFS), but cannot be reduced below $10,000 ($5,000 MFS)
- **Sunset:** This elevated cap applies for tax years 2025 through 2029 per the One Big Beautiful Bill Act; it is scheduled to revert to $10,000 after 2029 absent further legislation
- **Form:** Schedule A (Form 1040)

### Self-Employment Tax and FICA (2026)
- **SE tax rate:** 15.3% (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare) applied to 92.35% of net self-employment earnings (not the full 100%); 2.9% Medicare continues above the Social Security wage base
- **Social Security wage base:** $184,500
- **How to compute:** Multiply your net self-employment income by 0.9235 to get "net SE earnings," then multiply by 15.3% (on the amount up to $184,500) and by 2.9% (on any excess). Tax software performs this automatically; estimates in this playbook use 15.3% of gross distributions as a conservative approximation.
- **Additional Medicare Tax:** 0.9% on wages, compensation, and SE income above $200,000 (single) / $250,000 (MFJ) / $125,000 (MFS)
- **SE tax deduction:** You may deduct 50% of SE tax paid as an above-the-line deduction on Form 1040, reducing your AGI

### Annual Gift Tax Exclusion (2026)
- **Per-recipient exclusion:** $19,000 (gifts up to this amount per recipient per year do not count against your lifetime exclusion and require no gift tax return)
- **Estate and gift tax lifetime exclusion:** $15,000,000 (per person; $30,000,000 for married couples using portability)
- **Gift to non-citizen spouse:** $194,000 annual exclusion

### New Individual Deductions -- One Big Beautiful Bill Act (2026-2028)
These deductions are available to both itemizers and non-itemizers for tax years 2025 through 2028:
- **Senior deduction (age 65+):** Additional $6,000 deduction; phases out above $75,000 MAGI ($150,000 for MFJ)
- **Qualified tip income exclusion:** Up to $25,000 in tips received in qualifying occupations; phases out above $150,000 MAGI ($300,000 MFJ)
- **Overtime pay deduction:** Up to $12,500 ($25,000 for MFJ) for qualified overtime compensation; phases out above $150,000 MAGI ($300,000 MFJ)
- **Vehicle loan interest deduction:** Up to $10,000 for interest on loans for qualifying U.S.-assembled passenger vehicles; phases out above $100,000 MAGI ($200,000 MFJ)

### Bonus Depreciation (2026)
- **Rate:** 100% for qualifying property placed in service after January 19, 2025
- **Qualifying property:** Property with a recovery period of 20 years or less (5-year, 7-year, 15-year, etc.) used in a business; does not include regular residential or commercial real property
- **Form:** Form 4562 (Depreciation and Amortization)

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure:**
- The QBID, accountable plan, Augusta Rule, and reasonable compensation rules apply differently depending on your entity type. An S-corp shareholder accesses accountable plan reimbursements through payroll; a Schedule C sole proprietor claims home office directly on Schedule C. Do not apply S-corp rules to a sole proprietorship and vice versa.
- If you are considering converting from a sole proprietorship or partnership to an S-corp, the election must generally be filed by March 15 of the year for which you want it to be effective. Late elections are possible but require IRS relief procedures.

**Payroll:**
- S-corp owners must run actual payroll (not just pay themselves transfers or draws) and file Form 941 quarterly, pay FUTA annually on Form 940, and issue a W-2 by January 31. Failure to run payroll invalidates the S-corp structure for FICA savings purposes.
- Wages paid to children employed by a sole proprietor or a partnership where all partners are parents carry two separate exemptions: (1) Social Security and Medicare (FICA) taxes do not apply to a child under age 18 (IRC Section 3121(b)(3)); and (2) federal unemployment tax (FUTA) does not apply to a child under age 21 (IRC Section 3306(c)(5)). These exemptions do NOT apply to S-corps or C-corps -- child wages paid by a corporate employer are fully subject to FICA and FUTA regardless of the child's age.
- Health insurance premiums paid by an S-corp for a greater-than-2% shareholder-employee must be included in the shareholder's W-2 wages (Box 1 and Box 14) and then deducted on the shareholder's Form 1040 as self-employed health insurance. Failing to include premiums in W-2 wages may disqualify the deduction.

**State tax caveats:**
- States do not uniformly conform to federal pass-through entity rules. Many states: (a) do not recognize the QBID, (b) have their own limitations on bonus depreciation, (c) impose a separate tax on S-corp income at the entity level, or (d) have different SALT workaround (pass-through entity tax, or PTET) rules that interact with the federal SALT cap.
- If you operate in multiple states, you may owe nonresident state income tax in each state where the business has economic nexus. Your advisor will analyze your specific state exposure.
- The Augusta Rule's tax-free rental income treatment is a federal rule. Some states tax this income even though the federal government does not. Confirm with your advisor.

**Current-year legislative changes:**
- The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) made several changes effective for 2025 and 2026: it made the QBID permanent, restored 100% bonus depreciation, raised the SALT cap to $40,400 (with the phase-out described above), and created the new individual deductions (tips, overtime, senior deduction, vehicle loan interest). These provisions may change if future legislation is enacted.
- The SALT cap increase is scheduled to sunset after 2029. Year-end planning during 2029 may involve accelerating state tax payments to maximize the deduction before the reversion.

**NIIT and high-income planning:**
- If your income exceeds the NIIT thresholds ($200,000 single / $250,000 MFJ), passive rental income, capital gains, interest, and dividends are subject to an additional 3.8% tax. Active trade or business income (including S-corp income if you materially participate) is generally not subject to NIIT. Structuring to ensure material participation is documented matters for taxpayers near this threshold.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Claiming strategies without documentation in place.** The deduction itself is not the problem -- the absence of proof is. Auditors do not disallow strategies because they are aggressive; they disallow them because the taxpayer cannot produce the required records. Build the vault before (or immediately after) each strategy is implemented.

2. **Missing year-end deadlines.** The most common lost savings occur because the taxpayer waited until February or March of the following year to think about the prior year. A solo 401(k) must be established by December 31; the Augusta Rule days must occur by December 31; year-end payroll must be run by December 31.

3. **Using the wrong accountable plan reimbursement form (or none at all).** Paying yourself for a home office expense as a simple bank transfer without a written reimbursement request and business-purpose documentation converts an excludable reimbursement into taxable wages -- and triggers payroll tax liability.

4. **Paying S-corp reasonable compensation below a defensible amount.** The IRS and courts look at compensation surveys, comparable industry wages, and the ratio of salary to distributions. A salary that is too low is the most frequently audited S-corp issue. If you do not have a documented comparable salary analysis, get one.

5. **Failing to update the plan when income changes.** A plan built on a $200,000 income projection becomes wrong when actual income comes in at $350,000. That changes your QBI phase-out status, your marginal rate, your estimated tax obligations, and the optimal retirement plan type. Review projections quarterly.

6. **Mixing personal and business expenses.** A deduction that would otherwise be valid can be lost if it runs through a personal bank account or credit card with no business-purpose documentation, because there is no clean audit trail. Use separate business accounts for all business transactions.

7. **Ignoring state conformity.** Implementing a strategy that works at the federal level without asking whether your state conforms can result in an unexpected state tax bill. For example, several states do not allow bonus depreciation, so 100% federal bonus depreciation creates a state addback.

8. **Treating the tax plan as a one-time event.** Tax planning is a recurring discipline, not a one-year project. Income changes, the law changes, your business changes. The plan and the vault require an annual review minimum and quarterly monitoring.

9. **Not coordinating IRA and 401(k) contributions with income limits.** Roth IRA contributions phase out beginning at $153,000 (single) or $242,000 (MFJ) in 2026. Making a direct Roth IRA contribution when income exceeds those thresholds results in an excess contribution penalty (6% per year until corrected). A backdoor Roth IRA conversion may be available as an alternative -- discuss with your advisor.

10. **Forgetting that HSA contributions require HDHP enrollment for the entire contribution month.** If you switch from an HDHP to a non-HDHP mid-year, you must pro-rate your HSA contribution limit to the months you were enrolled. Contributing the full-year limit when you were enrolled only part of the year creates an excess contribution.

## Advisor Review Gates

Before taking any of the following actions, confirm with your advisor:

- **Before making or changing your S-corp election:** Confirm timing, entity conversion mechanics, reasonable compensation amount, and state-level implications. An incorrect election or improperly timed election can be difficult or costly to unwind.
- **Before setting or changing your S-corp salary for the year:** Your compensation must be documented as reasonable before payroll runs, not after year-end.
- **Before making Augusta Rule rental payments:** Confirm that the meeting qualifies, that the rate is at fair market value, that the agreement is in writing and signed by both parties (you as individual and you as business officer), and that all 14 days have not already been used.
- **Before contributing to a new retirement plan:** Confirm that the plan type (SEP-IRA vs. solo 401(k) vs. SIMPLE vs. defined benefit) is optimal for your income level, employee count, and desire to contribute for employees. Some plan types cannot be combined.
- **Before claiming the QBID if your income is in or above the phase-out range:** The calculation at the phase-in range is complex and the SSTB exclusion is absolute above the top of the range. Have your advisor calculate the exact allowable deduction on Form 8995-A before filing.
- **Before making any deduction for business use of your vehicle:** Confirm whether actual expense or standard mileage rate is more beneficial, and confirm that mileage logs exist that document each business trip (date, destination, business purpose, miles).
- **Before implementing a cost segregation study:** Confirm that the building qualifies, understand the depreciation recapture rules upon sale, and verify that you do not have passive activity loss limitations that would defer the benefit.
- **Before filing the return, confirm:** That all vault documents are in place for every strategy being claimed; that estimated tax payments match what was actually paid; and that state returns are consistent with the federal return.

## Before You Act

**Stop here if you have not yet reviewed this checklist with your advisor.**

Every strategy in this playbook is legal and widely used. But the details that make a strategy work -- your entity type, your income level, your state of residence, your filing status, your specific expenses and receipts, the timing of elections, and the accuracy of the numbers -- are personal to you. A strategy that saves one business owner $8,000 may be unavailable to, or harmful for, another owner whose facts are different.

Before you make any election, submit any reimbursement, run any payroll, fund any retirement plan, execute any rental agreement, or file any return based on the strategies in this playbook:

1. Confirm with your advisor that the strategy fits your specific facts.
2. Confirm with your advisor that the documentation in your vault is adequate to support the deduction.
3. Confirm with your advisor that the action will be taken before the applicable deadline.
4. Ask your advisor whether any state income tax consequences differ from the federal treatment described here.

The Planning Roadmap and Vault Review Implementation Worksheet is the working tool. Your advisor is the checkpoint. The combination of a complete worksheet and a confirmed advisor review is what converts a good plan into an implemented, defensible plan.
