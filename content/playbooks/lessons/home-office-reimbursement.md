# Home Office Reimbursement

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

If you operate your business as an S-corporation, you cannot claim a personal home office deduction on your individual tax return the way a sole proprietor can. Instead, the IRS-approved path is for your S-corporation to reimburse you -- as an employee of your own company -- for the portion of your home costs that are attributable to your home office. When this reimbursement runs through a written accountable plan (a formal employer reimbursement policy that meets three IRS requirements), it is completely tax-free to you and fully deductible by the corporation. No payroll taxes apply to the reimbursement amount, and it does not appear as wages on your W-2. The result is a dollar-for-dollar conversion of a personal housing cost into a corporate business deduction.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is for:

- **S-corporation owner-employees** who use a dedicated area of their home regularly and exclusively for business, and who receive a W-2 salary from their S-corp. This is the most common use case.
- **C-corporation employees and owner-employees** who meet the same home office use test. The accountable plan rules apply equally to C-corps.
- **Employees of any corporation** (not just owners) whose employer is willing to adopt a written accountable plan and reimburse genuine home office costs.
- **Business owners who work primarily from home** and lack a separate fixed office location, or who use the home office to conduct administrative and management activities (billing, record-keeping, scheduling) even if they also meet clients elsewhere.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs taxed as disregarded entities:** You are not your own employee, so an accountable plan does not apply. You claim home office costs directly on Schedule C (Form 1040) using Form 8829 or the simplified method.
- **Partners in a partnership (for themselves):** Partners are not employees for this purpose. Home office expenses for partners follow different rules; confirm the correct treatment with your advisor.
- **Employees who work from a corporate office they do not own:** If the corporation already provides you a principal place of business, the home office must meet a separate qualifying use test before any reimbursement is appropriate.
- **Mixed-use spaces that cannot pass the exclusive use test:** If you use the room for personal activities (even occasionally), no deduction is allowed and no reimbursement is proper. There is no partial credit for rooms used partly for business and partly for personal purposes.
- **Businesses not willing to maintain documentation:** The plan only works if the corporation maintains a written plan, you submit monthly or quarterly expense reports with supporting documents, and records are kept for the required retention period. If the record-keeping will not happen in practice, do not implement this strategy.
- **Renters whose lease prohibits business use:** Check your lease first. Tax law permits the deduction but a lease restriction could create a separate legal issue.

## Key Terms

**Accountable plan:** A written employer reimbursement arrangement that satisfies three IRS requirements (business connection, substantiation, and return of excess) under Treasury Regulation 1.62-2 and IRC Section 62(a)(2)(A). When all three requirements are met, reimbursements are excluded from the employee's gross income and are not subject to income tax withholding or payroll taxes (FICA). They do not appear as wages on Form W-2.

**IRC Section 62(a)(2)(A):** The Internal Revenue Code provision that allows employees to exclude employer reimbursements from gross income when paid under an accountable plan. "IRC" stands for Internal Revenue Code, the federal tax statute.

**Treasury Regulation 1.62-2:** The Treasury Department's detailed rules implementing IRC Section 62 and defining the three requirements an accountable plan must satisfy.

**Business connection:** The reimbursed expense must be a deductible business expense under IRC Section 162 (ordinary and necessary business expenses) that you paid or incurred while performing services as an employee of the corporation.

**Substantiation:** Providing the corporation with documentary evidence of each expense: the amount, date, description, and business purpose. For home office costs, this means records showing the square footage of the office space, total home square footage, and the monthly amounts of each home expense category being reimbursed.

**Return of excess:** Any advance or reimbursement that exceeds your actual substantiated expenses must be returned to the corporation within a "reasonable period of time" (see Reasonable period safe harbors below).

**Reasonable period safe harbors:** Three IRS-approved timeframes that automatically qualify as "reasonable": (1) you receive an advance within 30 days of incurring the expense; (2) you submit substantiation within 60 days after paying or incurring the expense; (3) you return any excess reimbursement within 120 days after the expense was paid or incurred.

**Nonaccountable plan:** An arrangement that fails any one of the three requirements. All payments under a nonaccountable plan are treated as taxable wages, reported on Form W-2, and subject to FICA.

**FICA:** Federal Insurance Contributions Act taxes -- the combined Social Security tax and Medicare tax paid by both employer and employee on wages. In 2026, the Social Security portion is 6.2% each (employer and employee) on wages up to the Social Security wage base, and the Medicare portion is 1.45% each with no wage cap. Accountable plan reimbursements are exempt from FICA entirely.

**Regular and exclusive use:** The IRS requires that the area you claim as your home office be used (a) regularly -- meaning on an ongoing, not merely occasional or incidental, basis -- and (b) exclusively -- meaning only for business, with no personal use of the space whatsoever. Both requirements must be met simultaneously and continuously throughout the year.

**Principal place of business:** One of the qualifying tests under IRC Section 280A(c)(1). Your home office qualifies as your principal place of business if you use it for administrative or management activities (such as billing, record-keeping, ordering supplies, or scheduling) AND you have no other fixed location where you conduct substantial administrative or management activities of your business.

**IRC Section 280A:** The Internal Revenue Code section that governs home office deductions. It disallows home office expenses by default and then carves out exceptions, including the principal place of business exception at subsection (c)(1).

**Direct expenses:** Costs that benefit only the office space itself, such as painting or repairing only the office room. Direct expenses are reimbursable in full (100%) because they relate entirely to the business area.

**Indirect expenses:** Costs that benefit your entire home, such as mortgage interest, property taxes, utilities, homeowner's or renter's insurance, general repairs, and internet service. Only the business-use percentage of each indirect expense is reimbursable.

**Business-use percentage:** The fraction of your home devoted to the qualified home office, calculated by dividing the square footage of the office space by the total finished square footage of the home. Example: a 200-square-foot office in a 2,000-square-foot home equals a 10% business-use percentage.

**Corporate resolution:** A formal written record of a decision made by the corporation's board of directors. You must pass a corporate resolution authorizing and establishing the accountable plan before reimbursements begin.

**Form W-2:** IRS Form W-2, Wage and Tax Statement. Proper accountable plan reimbursements are NOT reported as wages in Box 1. They do not appear on Form W-2 at all when properly administered.

**Form 1120-S:** The U.S. Income Tax Return for an S Corporation. The corporation deducts accountable plan reimbursements as ordinary business expenses, typically on Line 18 (employee benefit programs) or Line 20 (other deductions), not as compensation.

**Schedule A (Form 1040):** The itemized deductions form on your personal return. Mortgage interest and property taxes you reimburse through the corporation are deducted by the corporation as business expenses. However, if you also itemize on Schedule A, you may need to reduce your personal Schedule A deductions for mortgage interest and real estate taxes by the business-use portion you reimbursed through the corporation to avoid double-deducting the same dollars. Your advisor should coordinate this.

**Depreciation (for home office actual expense method):** A deduction representing the gradual wear of the structure used for business. For the home office actual expense method, the business portion of your home is depreciated as nonresidential real property under MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System) using the straight-line method over 39 years. "Straight-line" means an equal amount is deducted each year. "39 years" is the IRS-assigned useful life for nonresidential real property. Note: when you later sell your home, any depreciation deductions taken are subject to "depreciation recapture" tax (currently at a maximum rate of 25% under Section 1250). This is a significant tax planning consideration discussed in the caveats section.

**MACRS:** Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System -- the IRS depreciation system for business property. For home offices, nonresidential real property under MACRS uses the straight-line method over 39 years.

**Depreciation recapture:** When you sell a home on which you claimed depreciation as a business deduction, the IRS "recaptures" that benefit by taxing the gain attributable to depreciation at up to 25% (rather than the lower long-term capital gains rate). This applies even if you exclude the rest of the gain under the Section 121 home sale exclusion.

**Section 121 home sale exclusion:** The provision that lets you exclude up to $250,000 ($500,000 for married filing jointly) of gain on the sale of your principal residence if you meet the ownership and use tests. Depreciation claimed on the home office portion is NOT covered by this exclusion and remains taxable.

**Simplified method:** An alternative IRS calculation method for home office deductions that uses a flat rate of $5 per square foot of qualified office space, up to a maximum of 300 square feet, for a maximum deduction of $1,500 per year. Under this method, no separate depreciation calculation or recapture applies. As a formal IRS election, the simplified method applies to sole proprietors filing Schedule C. S-corp owner-employees using an accountable plan must base reimbursements on actual documented expenses; the IRS has not issued guidance authorizing use of the $5/sq ft simplified figure as a substitute for actual expenses in an accountable plan context. Consult your advisor on how to calculate the reimbursable amount.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The tax form sole proprietors use to report business income and deductions, including home office expenses. S-corp owner-employees do NOT use Schedule C for their S-corp activities.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before beginning:

1. **Office square footage:** Measure the dedicated office space in square feet.
2. **Total home finished square footage:** The total finished area of your home (excluding garages, unfinished basements, etc. unless used for business).
3. **Ownership or rental status:** Whether you own or rent the home affects which expenses apply (owners have mortgage interest and property taxes; renters have rent payments).
4. **Monthly home expense amounts for the prior 12 months:** Gather amounts for each of the following as applicable: mortgage interest paid (from your lender's Form 1098), real estate (property) taxes paid, homeowner's or renter's insurance premiums, electricity, gas, water/sewer, trash collection, general home repairs and maintenance, internet service (if used for business).
5. **Date your home office was first used for business:** Needed to calculate depreciation if using the actual expense method.
6. **Home's original purchase price and date:** Needed for depreciation basis calculation (actual expense method only).
7. **Adjusted basis of the home:** The original cost plus improvements, less prior depreciation. Your advisor will help calculate this if it is not straightforward.
8. **Fair market value of the home when business use began:** The depreciable basis is the lower of adjusted basis or fair market value at the time business use started.
9. **Whether a written accountable plan and corporate resolution already exist:** If not, these must be created before any reimbursements are paid.
10. **Whether you have other fixed business locations** where you perform administrative and management activities (which could disqualify the home office as your principal place of business).

## Required Documents And Records

**Establish and keep permanently:**
- Written accountable plan document (the policy adopted by the corporation)
- Corporate board resolution authorizing the accountable plan, signed and dated before the first reimbursement
- Floor plan or diagram of your home showing the office area with square footage labeled

**Gather annually and retain for at least 3 years after filing:**
- Annual calculation worksheet showing: office sq ft, total home sq ft, business-use percentage, and the business-use percentage applied to each expense category
- Monthly or quarterly expense reimbursement request forms submitted to the corporation, with the amount of each home expense and the reimbursable portion
- Supporting receipts or statements for each expense category: mortgage statement (Form 1098 from lender), property tax bills and payment receipts, insurance premium statements, utility bills, repair invoices

**Retain property records until 3 years after the year you sell the home:**
- Depreciation schedule for the home office (if using actual expense method)
- Records of home improvements that affect the adjusted basis
- All prior home office depreciation deductions claimed

**Employment tax records:** Keep for at least 4 years after the tax becomes due or is paid. (Source: IRS guidance on record retention.)

**IRS general rule:** Keep records supporting income, deductions, and credits for as long as the IRS can assess additional tax -- generally 3 years from the filing date, but 6 years if you underreported gross income by more than 25%, and 7 years for certain loss claims. For home office records, the conservative practice is 7 years plus the property-holding period.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Qualify the Space (Do First, Before Any Money Moves)

**Step 1: Measure the office and apply the exclusive use test.**
Walk through the room you intend to claim. Ask: is this space used ONLY for business? If there is a guest bed, a TV watched for personal use, children's homework, or any personal storage in the room, it does not qualify under the IRS exclusive use rule. The entire room must be dedicated to business use. Measure the room's square footage.

**Step 2: Confirm the principal place of business test.**
Ask yourself: do you regularly use this space to conduct administrative or management activities of your business (billing clients, scheduling, record-keeping, ordering supplies, writing reports)? And is there no other fixed location where you conduct those same administrative activities substantially? If yes to both, the home office qualifies as your principal place of business under IRC Section 280A(c)(1).

**Step 3: Calculate your business-use percentage.**
Divide the office square footage by the total finished home square footage. Write this percentage down. Example: 250 sq ft office / 2,500 sq ft home = 10%.

### Phase 2: Set Up the Accountable Plan (One-Time Corporate Action)

**Step 4: Draft the written accountable plan document.**
The plan document must state, at minimum: (a) which employees are covered, (b) which expense categories qualify for reimbursement, (c) the requirement to submit substantiation within 60 days of incurring or paying an expense, and (d) the requirement to return any excess reimbursement within 120 days. There is no IRS-prescribed form. A one-to-two-page policy document is sufficient. Use the Home Office Quick Reference Guide (attached download) to confirm the required elements.

**Step 5: Pass a corporate board resolution.**
The board of directors of the S-corporation -- even if that is only you -- must formally adopt the accountable plan by written resolution. The resolution should: name the plan, state the effective date, authorize reimbursements under its terms, and be signed and dated. This document must be kept in the corporation's permanent records. The resolution must predate any reimbursement payment.

**Step 6: Set up a tracking system.**
Create a simple monthly or quarterly expense log. Each entry should capture: the date of the expense, the category (utilities, insurance, etc.), the total amount paid, the business-use percentage applied, and the reimbursable amount. Attach supporting receipts or statements. Software tools such as QuickBooks, Excel, or expense-management apps work well.

### Phase 3: Calculate the Monthly Reimbursable Amount

**Step 7: List each eligible indirect expense and calculate the business portion.**
For each month, list the actual amounts paid for mortgage interest, property taxes (prorated monthly), homeowner's insurance, utilities, internet (business-use portion), and repairs. Multiply each amount by your business-use percentage from Step 3. This is the reimbursable indirect expense amount.

**Step 8: Add any direct expenses.**
If you repainted the office, installed office-specific lighting, or made repairs solely in the office space, add 100% of those costs to the reimbursable total. Keep all receipts.

**Step 9: Calculate depreciation for the business portion (actual expense method only -- optional).**
If you choose the actual expense method and wish to claim depreciation, your advisor will calculate the depreciable basis (the lower of adjusted basis or fair market value when business use started, multiplied by the business-use percentage) and apply the straight-line MACRS rate over 39 years. Include this annual depreciation amount (divided by 12) in monthly reimbursements or claim it as an annual amount. Important: claiming depreciation creates future depreciation recapture risk at sale. Many business owners skip depreciation in the accountable plan reimbursement to avoid this complexity; confirm the approach with your advisor.

**Step 10: Total the reimbursable amount and prepare the reimbursement request form.**
Add indirect business expenses + direct expenses + depreciation (if applicable) = total monthly reimbursement amount. Complete a reimbursement request form and submit it to the corporation within 60 days of the end of each month (or the period covered).

### Phase 4: Pay, Record, and Verify

**Step 11: The corporation issues the reimbursement.**
The corporation writes a check (or initiates a transfer) to you as the employee. This payment must be labeled as an expense reimbursement -- not as a draw, distribution, or bonus. Keep a copy of the check or transfer record.

**Step 12: Record the expense on the corporate books.**
The corporation's bookkeeper or accounting software should record the reimbursement as a business expense deduction. It is typically categorized as "Home Office Expense," "Employee Business Expense Reimbursement," or under the appropriate expense category (utilities, rent, etc.) on the corporate return. It goes on Form 1120-S under "Other Deductions" (Line 20) or as a separately stated ordinary business expense. It does NOT go on Lines 7 or 8 (officer/employee compensation).

**Step 13: Confirm the reimbursement does NOT appear on your W-2.**
At year-end, review your W-2. The accountable plan reimbursements should NOT appear in Box 1 (Wages), Box 3 (Social Security Wages), or Box 5 (Medicare Wages). If they do appear, your payroll provider has made an error that must be corrected. Contact your advisor immediately.

**Step 14: Return any excess amounts within 120 days.**
If you received an advance that exceeded your actual expenses, return the difference to the corporation within 120 days of incurring the expense. Document the return.

**Step 15: Repeat monthly or quarterly throughout the year.**
The strategy requires ongoing documentation. Submit expense reports on a regular schedule (monthly or quarterly) rather than annually, so documentation stays current and the plan remains compliant.

### Phase 5: Year-End and Tax Filing

**Step 16: Reconcile annual totals and confirm the corporate deduction.**
At year-end, total all reimbursements made and confirm they match the amounts recorded in the corporation's books. Your tax preparer will deduct the total from the S-corp's income on Form 1120-S.

**Step 17: Coordinate Schedule A with your advisor.**
If you itemize deductions personally, review with your advisor whether the mortgage interest and real estate taxes reimbursed through the corporation need to be reduced on your personal Schedule A to avoid claiming the same dollars twice.

**Step 18: File and retain all records.**
File the corporate return, and retain all supporting documentation according to the retention schedule in the "Required Documents And Records" section above.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**S-corp entity structure required for this exact approach.** This reimbursement path depends on you having a true employer-employee relationship with your S-corporation. If your business is a sole proprietorship, single-member LLC taxed as a disregarded entity, or a partnership, the accountable plan approach for your own home office expenses does not apply.

**Reasonable compensation still required.** Using an accountable plan does not change the IRS requirement that S-corp owner-employees pay themselves a reasonable salary (the W-2 wage the IRS expects based on the services performed). Do not use large reimbursements as a substitute for reasonable compensation. Your advisor should confirm your salary level is defensible before adding reimbursements.

**2% shareholder nuance for health benefits vs. business expense reimbursements.** The "2% shareholder" rule (which applies to any person who owns more than 2% of the S-corp's stock) creates restrictions for health and welfare fringe benefits (such as employer-paid health insurance premiums). However, the 2% shareholder limitation does NOT eliminate the ability to receive tax-free business expense reimbursements under an accountable plan for ordinary business costs such as home office, mileage, cell phone, and travel. Confirm the specific treatment of any non-standard reimbursement category with your advisor.

**No double-deduction of mortgage interest and property taxes.** If the corporation reimburses you for the business portion of your mortgage interest and property taxes, and you also itemize deductions on Schedule A (Form 1040), you must reduce your Schedule A deductions for mortgage interest and real estate taxes by the amounts reimbursed. Deducting them twice -- once on the corporate return and once on Schedule A -- is not permitted. Your tax preparer should coordinate this at filing.

**Depreciation recapture on home sale.** If you include home depreciation in your reimbursed amounts (under the actual expense method), those depreciation deductions create taxable income when you sell the home. The gain attributable to prior depreciation is taxed at up to 25% and is NOT excluded by the Section 121 home sale exclusion. Consult your advisor before claiming depreciation in the reimbursement to make sure the annual tax benefit outweighs the future cost.

**State income tax treatment varies.** Most states follow federal accountable plan rules, but not all. A small number of states impose state income tax on reimbursements that are federally tax-free, or require separate documentation. Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor before assuming federal and state treatment are identical.

**One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025).** The TCJA already suspended miscellaneous itemized deductions (including unreimbursed employee business expenses) for all tax years 2018 through 2025 -- so these costs were already nondeductible on the individual return throughout that period. The OBBBA (effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025) makes that suspension permanent with no gap: there was no window in 2025 or any prior year when unreimbursed home office costs were deductible for S-corp employees. The accountable plan is the only effective path to a deduction for these costs.

**Home office must be your ONLY option for that room.** The exclusive use requirement is strictly enforced. If you are audited, the IRS may request a floor plan, photos, or a description of everything in the room. Shared-use spaces -- an office that doubles as a guest bedroom, a kitchen table where you also eat meals, a living room corner -- do not qualify. If you cannot demonstrate exclusive business use, the entire deduction and reimbursement are disallowed.

**No home office deduction on your personal return as an S-corp employee.** Unreimbursed employee home office expenses have been nondeductible since 2018 under the TCJA suspension, and the OBBBA now makes that nondeductibility permanent. You cannot deduct these costs on Schedule A as an employee for any year from 2018 onward. The accountable plan is the only effective path.

## Common Mistakes

**1. No written plan before reimbursements begin.** The most common audit failure: payments made before the corporate resolution and written plan are in place. These retroactively fail accountable plan status and become taxable wages with penalties and interest. The plan must precede the first payment.

**2. Using a room that is not exclusively for business.** Reimbursing expenses for a room that doubles as a personal space is not only wrong -- it is the kind of error that turns a tax audit into a fraud inquiry. Measure, document, and photograph the space.

**3. Submitting expense reports annually instead of monthly or quarterly.** The IRS looks at whether substantiation is provided within a "reasonable period of time." Annual submissions made at year-end (long after expenses were incurred) raise compliance questions, especially if expenses span more than 60 days from the relevant dates.

**4. Including home depreciation without understanding recapture.** Some business owners include depreciation in the reimbursed amount without realizing that the recapture tax at home sale can significantly erode the benefit. Calculate the long-term math with your advisor first.

**5. Failing to adjust personal Schedule A for reimbursed mortgage interest and property taxes.** If you itemize, deducting the same mortgage interest or property taxes on both the corporate return (via reimbursement) and Schedule A is a double-deduction error that the IRS routinely catches.

**6. Running reimbursements as draws or distributions.** The payment must be documented and characterized as a reimbursement, not an owner's draw or S-corp distribution. Draws and distributions do not carry the same accountable plan protections.

**7. Changing the business-use percentage mid-year without updated documentation.** If you add a room, move offices, or change how you use the space, recalculate and document the new percentage immediately. Using a stale number without updating records undermines the substantiation requirement.

**8. Reimbursing the full cost of utilities without limiting to the business-use percentage.** The reimbursable amount for indirect expenses (utilities, insurance, etc.) is ONLY the business-use percentage of the total cost. Reimbursing 100% of a utility bill for which the business-use percentage is 15% is an overstatement.

**9. Forgetting to return excess advances.** If the corporation advanced money and you did not spend all of it on qualified home office expenses, the unspent amount must be returned within 120 days. Failing to return it converts the excess into taxable wages.

## Advisor Review Gates

Pause and confirm with your advisor before proceeding at any of the following points:

1. **Before drafting the plan:** Confirm that your entity structure (S-corp) and employment arrangement support an accountable plan and that you do not already have a plan in place that conflicts.

2. **Before establishing the business-use percentage:** Have your advisor review your office layout, exclusive use analysis, and the principal place of business test. A mistake here invalidates the entire strategy.

3. **Before including depreciation in reimbursed amounts:** Your advisor should calculate the depreciation benefit net of future recapture risk, given your home's value, your marginal tax rate now, and the likely capital gains rate at sale.

4. **Before filing the corporate return:** Your advisor should confirm the reimbursements are recorded as a deduction on Form 1120-S (not as wages on Lines 7 or 8) and that the amount is correct.

5. **Before filing your personal return:** If you itemize deductions, confirm the Schedule A adjustment for mortgage interest and property taxes has been correctly made to avoid double-deducting.

6. **If you sell or refinance your home:** Notify your advisor. Prior home office depreciation may create recapture tax that must be reported on Form 4797 (Sales of Business Property).

7. **If you change your state of residence or add operations in a new state:** State tax treatment of accountable plan reimbursements varies. Get state-specific confirmation.

8. **If the IRS or a state agency contacts you:** Do not respond to any audit inquiry related to home office reimbursements without first contacting your advisor.

1. **IRS Publication 587 (2025), Business Use of Your Home** -- simplified method rate ($5/sq ft), 300 sq ft cap, $1,500 maximum, regular and exclusive use definition, direct vs. indirect expense categories, depreciation method (straight-line MACRS, 39 years), gross income limitation, principal place of business administrative management test.
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/publications/p587

2. **IRS Simplified Option for Home Office Deduction (updated March 2, 2026)** -- confirmed $5/sq ft rate and 300 sq ft maximum remain in effect; confirmed no carryover under simplified method.
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/simplified-option-for-home-office-deduction

3. **IRS FAQs -- Simplified Method for Home Office Deduction (last reviewed April 30, 2026)** -- confirmed $1,500 maximum annual deduction and depreciation treatment under simplified method.
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/faqs-simplified-method-for-home-office-deduction

4. **Treasury Regulation 1.62-2 (via Cornell LII eCFR)** -- three accountable plan requirements (business connection, substantiation, return of excess), reasonable period safe harbors (30/60/120 days), FICA and W-2 exclusion for qualifying reimbursements.
   URL: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.62-2

5. **IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses** -- accountable plan substantiation rules, reasonable period safe harbors.
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463

6. **IRS Topic No. 509, Business Use of Home (last reviewed April 22, 2026)** -- direct vs. indirect expense categories, both calculation methods, no 2026 rate changes announced.
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc509

7. **26 U.S.C. Section 280A (via Cornell LII)** -- general disallowance rule and exceptions (principal place of business, client meeting space, detached structure), S-corp shareholder substitution rule in subsection (f)(2).
   URL: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/280A

8. **IRS Instructions for Form 8829 (2025)** -- confirmed employees cannot use Form 8829 on personal return; Form 8829 is for Schedule C filers only; direct vs. indirect expense definitions.
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8829

9. **IRS How Long Should I Keep Records?** -- 3-year standard retention, 6-year rule for underreported income, 7-year rule for loss claims, 4-year employment tax record rule.
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/how-long-should-i-keep-records

10. **IRS Newsroom -- One, Big, Beautiful Bill Provisions (P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025)** -- confirmed permanent extension of TCJA suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025.
    URL: https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/one-big-beautiful-bill-provisions

11. **Congress.gov CRS Report R48550, Tax Provisions in H.R. 1, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act: House-Passed Version** -- additional detail on permanent suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions.
    URL: https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48550

12. **IRS S-Corp Compensation and Medical Insurance Issues** -- confirmed accountable plan reimbursements are excluded from wages when three requirements are met; confirmed 2% shareholder health benefit limitations (separate from business expense reimbursements).
    URL: https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporation-compensation-and-medical-insurance-issues

13. **IRS Instructions for Form 1120-S (2025)** -- confirmed Line 18 is employee benefit programs, Line 19 is the Energy Efficient Commercial Buildings Deduction, and Line 20 is Other Deductions.
    URL: https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1120s
