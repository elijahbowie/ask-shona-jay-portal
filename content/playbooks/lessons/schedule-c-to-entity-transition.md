# Schedule C to Entity Transition

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

A sole proprietor who files a Schedule C (the IRS form that reports self-employment profit and loss) pays self-employment tax on every dollar of net profit, has no legal separation between personal and business assets, and cannot split income between a salary and profit distributions. Transitioning to a formal entity -- most commonly a single-member LLC that then elects S corporation tax treatment -- creates three potential benefits: legal liability protection between the owner and the business, the ability to split income so that only the salary portion is subject to the 15.3% self-employment tax, and a business structure that supports long-term growth, hiring, and eventually a sale. This playbook walks a current Schedule C filer through every step of forming an LLC, filing the S corporation election if appropriate, setting up payroll, and maintaining the structure correctly going forward.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is most valuable for:

- **Active Schedule C filers whose net profit consistently exceeds $40,000 to $60,000 per year** and who currently pay self-employment tax on all of that profit.
- **Freelancers, consultants, coaches, independent contractors, and service-business owners** who operate under their own name or a DBA (doing business as name) without any formal entity.
- **Sole proprietors who want personal liability protection** to separate business obligations and lawsuits from personal assets such as a home or personal savings.
- **Business owners who are ready to commit to a minimum level of ongoing compliance** -- at minimum, annual state LLC fees and a separate business bank account, and, if pursuing S corporation status, regular payroll, quarterly federal filings, and an annual S corporation return.
- **Owners planning to operate for at least three to five more years** in the same or similar business, so the one-time setup cost and recurring compliance costs have time to pay off.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Net profit consistently below $40,000.** The compliance costs of an LLC with S corporation status -- payroll processing (roughly $500 to $1,500 per year), an annual S corporation return, Form 1120-S (roughly $1,500 to $3,000 per year), and state fees -- often erase the self-employment tax savings at lower income levels. An LLC alone (without the S corporation election) is still worth considering for liability protection even at lower income, but the tax savings analysis must include all compliance costs. Confirm the net benefit with your advisor for your specific state and situation.
- **Owners who cannot commit to running regular payroll.** An S corporation owner-employee must receive a regular, documented salary through a payroll system. Irregular or absent payroll is a primary IRS audit trigger.
- **Businesses with highly unpredictable or seasonal income** where the owner cannot reliably project enough profit to justify a salary and operating costs.
- **Owners planning to bring in non-U.S. investors, corporate investors, or more than 100 shareholders.** S corporation eligibility requires all shareholders to be U.S. citizens or resident individuals (or certain qualifying trusts or estates), with no corporations or partnerships as shareholders, and no more than 100 shareholders total. If your ownership structure may include any of these, the S corporation election is not an option; a different entity path is needed.
- **Owners planning to sell the business in the near term.** The S corporation election can affect how a sale is taxed and may complicate buyer due diligence. Discuss entity strategy with your advisor before electing if a sale is possible within two to three years.
- **Owners who want to retain significant earnings inside the business at a low tax rate.** S corporations are pass-through entities: all income passes to the owner's personal return each year, taxed at individual rates, even if cash is not distributed. A C corporation may be more appropriate if the business plan involves significant reinvestment of earnings at the current flat 21% corporate rate. Discuss with your advisor.

## Key Terms

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The tax form attached to a sole proprietor's personal Form 1040 that reports business income and expenses. Net profit on Schedule C is subject to both regular income tax and self-employment tax.

**Sole proprietor:** A person who owns and operates a business without forming a separate legal entity. There is no legal separation between the owner and the business; the owner is personally responsible for all business debts and legal claims.

**DBA (doing business as):** A trade name or assumed name under which a sole proprietor operates. A DBA is a name registration, not a legal entity. It does not provide any liability protection.

**LLC (limited liability company):** A business entity formed under state law. Owners of an LLC are called members. The LLC provides a legal wall between the member's personal assets and the debts or lawsuits of the business. By default, a single-member LLC is treated by the IRS as a disregarded entity -- meaning the IRS ignores the LLC as a separate taxpayer, and the owner still files a Schedule C exactly as if the LLC did not exist. The LLC's liability protection is a state-law benefit that exists independently of its federal tax treatment.

**Disregarded entity:** An LLC (or other eligible entity) that the IRS treats as non-existent for federal income tax purposes. A single-member LLC is a disregarded entity by default. The owner reports all income and expenses on Schedule C of their personal Form 1040 and pays self-employment tax on all net profit, just as a bare sole proprietor does.

**Self-employment tax (SE tax):** A tax paid by sole proprietors and single-member LLC owners in place of the payroll taxes that employers and employees normally share. The SE tax rate is 15.3%, consisting of 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. It is assessed on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings (the IRS allows you to multiply net profit by 0.9235 before calculating SE tax, reflecting the fact that employees do not pay SE tax on the employer's half of FICA). You deduct one-half of the SE tax as an above-the-line deduction on Schedule 1 of Form 1040.

**SE tax deduction (half of SE tax):** Sole proprietors deduct 50% of their calculated self-employment tax as an above-the-line adjustment to income on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 15. This deduction partially offsets the self-employment tax burden. When you transition to an S corporation, this deduction is replaced by the S corporation's deduction of the employer half of FICA on your salary.

**FICA (Federal Insurance Contributions Act):** The payroll taxes paid on employee wages. Both employer and employee each pay 6.2% Social Security tax (on wages up to the Social Security wage base) and 1.45% Medicare tax (no cap). The combined rate is 15.3%, shared equally at 7.65% per side.

**Social Security wage base:** The maximum amount of wages or self-employment income subject to the 12.4% Social Security portion of payroll or SE tax in a given year. For 2026, the Social Security wage base is $184,500. Wages and self-employment earnings above this amount are exempt from the Social Security portion but remain subject to Medicare tax.

**Additional Medicare Tax:** A 0.9% surtax on wages and self-employment income that exceeds $200,000 for single filers or $250,000 for married filing jointly. Employers are required to withhold this on wages above $200,000 per employee in a calendar year. There is no employer match for the Additional Medicare Tax.

**S corporation (S-corp):** Not a separate type of entity but a tax classification. A corporation or eligible LLC that has elected to be taxed under Subchapter S of the Internal Revenue Code. An S corporation is a pass-through entity: it files its own tax return (Form 1120-S) but pays no federal income tax itself. Profits and losses pass through to shareholders and are reported on their personal Form 1040. The key tax benefit over a Schedule C is that only the owner's salary -- not the entire profit -- is subject to FICA payroll taxes.

**S corporation election:** The formal choice to have an LLC or corporation treated as an S corporation for federal tax purposes. Made by filing Form 2553 with the IRS. For a single-member LLC, filing Form 2553 simultaneously elects corporate classification (what would normally require a separate Form 8832) and S corporation tax treatment in a single step. No separate Form 8832 is required when using Form 2553.

**Form 2553:** The IRS form titled "Election by a Small Business Corporation." Filing this form is the only required IRS filing to elect S corporation status for a new or existing LLC. The election must be signed by all shareholders (members).

**Form 8832:** The IRS form used to elect a different tax classification for an eligible entity (for example, having a single-member LLC taxed as a C corporation instead of a disregarded entity). When using Form 2553 to elect S corporation status, Form 8832 is deemed filed automatically -- you do not file both forms.

**Reasonable compensation:** The salary an S corporation must pay its owner-employees before any profit distributions are made. The IRS requires this salary to reflect what a similarly qualified person doing the same work would be paid in the open market in an arm's-length transaction. There is no fixed formula or minimum; the standard is facts-and-circumstances based. Paying too little is the most frequently audited aspect of S corporation taxation.

**Profit distribution:** A payment from the S corporation to a shareholder that is not wages. Distributions come from after-salary profits and are not subject to FICA payroll taxes or self-employment tax. Distributions cannot substitute for reasonable compensation.

**Pass-through taxation:** The system by which S corporation income, losses, deductions, and credits flow to shareholders' personal tax returns in proportion to their ownership, rather than being taxed at the entity level.

**Form 1120-S:** The annual U.S. Income Tax Return for an S Corporation. This is a stand-alone return filed by the S corporation itself. It does not impose tax at the entity level but reports income and allocates it to shareholders via Schedule K-1. Due by the 15th day of the third month after the tax year ends (March 15 for calendar-year S corporations).

**Schedule K-1 (Form 1120-S):** The statement the S corporation provides each shareholder showing that shareholder's share of income, deductions, and credits for the year. Shareholders use their K-1 to complete their personal Form 1040. S corporation income reported on a K-1 is subject to income tax but NOT self-employment tax.

**Form 941:** The Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return. S corporations with employees (including the owner-employee) file Form 941 each quarter to report wages paid and payroll taxes withheld and remitted.

**Form 940:** The Employer's Annual Federal Unemployment (FUTA) Tax Return. Filed annually by S corporations that pay any employee (including the owner-employee) wages of $1,500 or more in any quarter.

**Form W-2:** The wage statement the S corporation provides each employee (including the owner-employee) by January 31 each year, showing wages paid and payroll taxes withheld.

**EFTPS (Electronic Federal Tax Payment System):** The free IRS system for making federal payroll tax deposits electronically. S corporations are required to use EFTPS for all federal payroll tax deposits. Enrollment requires five to seven business days to receive a PIN by mail.

**EIN (Employer Identification Number):** A nine-digit number the IRS assigns to identify a business for tax purposes. An LLC applying for an EIN uses Form SS-4 (or the free online application at IRS.gov/EIN). The EIN is required before filing Form 2553, running payroll, or opening a business bank account in the entity's name.

**Articles of organization:** The formation document filed with the state to legally create an LLC. Once the state approves the articles and issues a formation certificate, the LLC legally exists. State filing fees and processing times vary.

**Operating agreement:** The LLC's internal governance document, similar to bylaws for a corporation. It defines ownership percentages, how decisions are made, what happens if a member leaves, and how profits and losses are allocated. Required in many states and a best practice in all states.

**QBI deduction (Section 199A):** A deduction on the owner's personal return equal to up to 20% of qualified business income (QBI). QBI is the net business income that passes through to the owner, minus the W-2 salary taken. Made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed into law July 4, 2025, for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. The deduction applies to both Schedule C income and S corporation pass-through income, with different wage-limitation rules above certain taxable income thresholds. See The Numbers section for 2026 thresholds.

**Accumulated adjustments account (AAA):** An account tracked on the S corporation's books (not filed separately) that records cumulative taxable income and deductions since S corporation status began. Distributions up to the AAA balance are generally tax-free to shareholders. Your accountant maintains this schedule each year.

**2% shareholder:** For S corporation fringe benefit purposes, any person who owns more than 2% of the corporation's outstanding stock. Most owner-operated S corporations have a single 100% owner who is a 2% shareholder. Health insurance premiums paid by the S corporation for a 2% shareholder must be included in the shareholder's W-2 as taxable wages for income tax purposes (though they are exempt from FICA), and the shareholder may then deduct the premiums on their personal return using Form 7206.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A 3.8% surtax on investment income (including certain business income) for taxpayers with modified adjusted gross income above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). S corporation distributions are generally exempt from NIIT for active owner-operators who materially participate in the business. Passive investors in an S corporation may be subject to NIIT on their share of S corporation income.

**Material participation:** A set of IRS tests that determine whether a taxpayer is actively involved enough in a business activity to deduct losses against other income and to avoid the Net Investment Income Tax on S corporation distributions. Most owner-operators who work in their business full-time pass the material participation tests easily; passive investors in an S corporation often do not.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before you start:

1. **Current filing status:** Confirm you currently file a Schedule C as a sole proprietor (or as a single-member LLC treated as a disregarded entity). Pull your most recent Form 1040 and Schedule C to verify.
2. **Net profit history:** Pull your net profit from Schedule C for the most recent two to three years. You need this to project self-employment tax savings and to set a reasonable compensation amount.
3. **Expected future net profit:** Estimate what the business will net in 2026 and in each of the next two years. The S corporation election makes most sense when profits are consistent and growing.
4. **Your primary role in the business:** What services do you personally perform? How many hours per week? The IRS ties reasonable compensation to the services you provide; you need to describe your role clearly to set a defensible salary.
5. **Market salary for your role:** Before you can set a reasonable salary, you need to know what the open market pays for someone with your skills, experience, and credentials performing your specific duties in your geographic area. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS.gov), industry trade association salary surveys, and comparable job postings are the IRS-recognized benchmarks.
6. **State where you plan to form the LLC:** LLC filing fees, annual fees, and S corporation recognition rules vary significantly by state. Identify the state where the business is primarily operated. If you operate in more than one state, list all of them.
7. **Social Security number and legal name** for use on formation documents and Form 2553.
8. **Whether any business assets are personally held:** If you own equipment, vehicles, intellectual property, or other assets personally that the business uses, discuss with your advisor whether and how to transfer them to the LLC before or after formation.
9. **Whether you have existing employees:** If you currently pay anyone as a W-2 employee, the payroll structure of the new entity must account for them from day one.
10. **Target effective date of the S corporation election:** The election can be retroactive to the start of the current tax year if filed on time. Decide the year you want S corporation treatment to begin.

## Required Documents And Records

**Documents to gather before starting:**

- Most recent two to three years of Form 1040 with Schedule C (to document profit history)
- Current EIN, if you already have one (found on any prior IRS correspondence, Form CP-575, or by calling 800-829-4933)
- List of any business assets and their current fair market value and adjusted basis (for asset transfer planning)
- Any existing contracts, leases, or accounts in the business name (to plan for name transfer to the LLC after formation)
- Your state's LLC formation requirements (Secretary of State website)

**Documents to create during formation and election:**

- Signed Articles of Organization (filed with the state)
- State-issued LLC formation certificate or stamped articles (keep permanently)
- EIN confirmation letter (IRS Form CP-575) for the new or existing entity
- LLC Operating Agreement (signed by all members; keep permanently in corporate records)
- Completed and signed Form 2553, with date-stamped proof of filing (certified mail receipt or fax confirmation)
- IRS acceptance letter for the S corporation election (keep permanently)
- Written reasonable compensation analysis: a dated memo in your corporate records documenting the role description, hours per week, market data sources, comparable salary figures, and the salary amount selected
- Corporate resolution or member consent authorizing payroll and establishing the officer's title and compensation
- State-level S corporation election form, if required in your state (varies by state)
- Form W-4 (Employee's Withholding Certificate) completed by you as an employee of your own S corporation
- New hire reporting form, if required by your state (most states require reporting a new employee, including yourself, within 20 days of the first day worked)

**Ongoing records to maintain (minimum retention: 7 years):**

- All payroll records: pay stubs, payroll register, EFTPS deposit confirmations, and payroll tax filings
- Quarterly Forms 941 and the corresponding tax deposit receipts
- Annual Form 940 and all W-2 forms for every employee
- Form 1120-S and Schedule K-1 for each tax year
- Minutes of any annual meetings or member resolutions
- Records of every distribution: amount, date, and shareholder allocation
- Bank statements showing salary payments and distributions as separate, identifiable transactions
- Documentation of health insurance premiums paid for 2% shareholders and how they were reported on each year's W-2
- Accumulated adjustments account (AAA) tracking schedule (maintained by your accountant each year)

## Step-By-Step Implementation

**Phase 1: Decide on entity structure and analyze the numbers**

1. Decide whether you want an LLC alone (for liability protection, still filing Schedule C) or an LLC with an S corporation election (for liability protection plus payroll tax savings). An LLC without the S corporation election adds no new tax compliance burden -- you still file Schedule C -- but adds annual state fees and the need for a separate bank account and operating agreement. An LLC with the S corporation election adds meaningful payroll and return-filing obligations. If your net profit is below $40,000, forming the LLC alone for liability protection is often the right starting point; the S corporation election can be added later when profits justify it.

2. Project your annual self-employment tax savings from the S corporation election:
   - Take your expected annual net profit for 2026.
   - Subtract a reasonable compensation amount (see Step 6 for how to set this).
   - The remainder is your projected "distribution" amount -- the portion that avoids FICA.
   - Multiply the distribution amount by 15.3% to estimate gross FICA savings.
   - Subtract estimated annual compliance costs: payroll service (roughly $500 to $1,500 per year [confirm with your advisor -- costs vary by provider and state]), Form 1120-S preparation by an accountant (roughly $1,500 to $3,000 per year [confirm with your advisor -- costs vary]), and your state's annual LLC or S corporation fees (varies by state).
   - If the net savings are positive and expected to continue for at least two to three years, the election is likely worth pursuing.

   *Example (for illustration only -- your numbers will differ):* Annual net profit $120,000. Reasonable salary $60,000. Distribution $60,000. Gross FICA savings: $60,000 x 15.3% = $9,180. Subtract $4,000 in compliance costs. Projected net annual savings: approximately $5,180. Note: the 15.3% rate applies in full only on the portion of the salary that falls below the 2026 Social Security wage base of $184,500 (confirmed by SSA.gov). For salary amounts above $184,500, only the 2.9% Medicare component applies (the 12.4% Social Security portion does not). At the $60,000 salary level in this example the salary is well below $184,500, so the full 15.3% applies; at higher salary levels the savings calculation must be adjusted accordingly.

3. Confirm you meet the S corporation eligibility requirements if you plan to elect S corporation status:
   - You are a U.S. citizen or resident alien individual.
   - You will be the only shareholder (or all shareholders meet the eligibility rules).
   - The entity will have only one class of economic interest (meaning all shares have identical rights to distributions and liquidation proceeds).
   - If unsure about any eligibility requirement, stop here and confirm with your advisor before proceeding.

**Phase 2: Form the LLC**

4. Choose your state of formation. For most single-owner businesses, the best state to form the LLC is the state where the business is primarily operated. Out-of-state formations (Delaware, Wyoming, Nevada) often require the LLC to also register as a "foreign" (out-of-state) entity in the home state, adding fees and complexity without a meaningful tax benefit for most small businesses. Discuss with your advisor if you have specific concerns.

5. File Articles of Organization with your state's Secretary of State (or equivalent agency). The filing process and fee vary by state. The articles typically require:
   - LLC name (must be distinguishable from other entities on file in the state)
   - Registered agent name and address (the person or company that receives legal documents on behalf of the LLC in that state; you can serve as your own registered agent in most states)
   - Member name(s) or manager name(s), depending on whether the LLC is member-managed or manager-managed
   - Effective date (usually the filing date or a date you specify)

   Once the state approves the articles, you will receive a stamped confirmation or formation certificate. Keep this permanently.

6. Obtain an EIN. If you do not already have one, apply for a free EIN at IRS.gov/EIN (the online application is immediate and free). Apply using Form SS-4 if you prefer mail or fax. You need the EIN before opening a business bank account or filing Form 2553. If you already have an EIN as a sole proprietor with no employees, ask your advisor whether that same EIN can be used for the new LLC; in most cases a new LLC should obtain its own EIN to keep records clean.

7. Draft and sign an Operating Agreement. An operating agreement is the LLC's internal governance document. Even for a single-member LLC, an operating agreement establishes that the business is a legitimate separate entity, which matters for liability protection. It should document: your ownership percentage (100% for a single-member LLC), how profits and losses are allocated, how decisions are made, and what happens to the LLC if you die or become incapacitated. Many state bar associations and legal document services offer templates; have your advisor or an attorney review the agreement.

8. Open a dedicated business bank account in the LLC's name using the EIN. Commingling personal and business funds is one of the most common ways owners inadvertently expose themselves to personal liability despite having an LLC. All business income must be deposited into and all business expenses paid from the business account from day one.

**Phase 3: Elect S corporation status (skip if LLC only)**

9. Determine your reasonable compensation before filing Form 2553. You need to know what salary you will pay yourself as an S corporation owner-employee before the election takes effect, because you will need to begin payroll promptly once the election is effective. Research what the open market pays for someone with your qualifications performing your specific duties in your location. The IRS looks at: your training and experience, the specific duties and responsibilities you perform for the S corporation, the time devoted to those duties, how much of the S corporation's gross receipts depend directly on your personal services, and compensation paid to comparable employees or independent contractors in the same role at similar businesses. Document your research in a dated written memo. Set your salary at or above a figure you can defend with this documentation.

10. Download and complete Form 2553 from IRS.gov/forms. The form has three main sections:
    - **Part I (General Information):** Entity name, EIN, address, tax year type (check "calendar year" for most businesses), the effective date of the election (January 1, 2026 if filing for 2026 tax year effect), and the name and title of a corporate officer the IRS can contact.
    - **Column K (Shareholder Consent Statement):** Every member must provide their printed name, address, Social Security number, ownership percentage, the date they acquired their interest, and their signature. For a single-member LLC you are the only signer.
    - **Part II (Selection of Fiscal Tax Year):** For most businesses, check the calendar year (January 1 to December 31). A fiscal year other than the calendar year requires special IRS approval and is not typical for most service businesses.

11. File Form 2553 by the deadline:
    - To have S corporation status effective January 1, 2026: file by **March 15, 2026** (the deadline is 2 months and 15 days after the start of the tax year). Alternatively, file any time during 2025 (the preceding tax year) to take effect January 1, 2026.
    - To have S corporation status effective January 1, 2027: file by March 15, 2027, or any time during 2026.
    - Mail to the correct IRS Service Center for your state (see The Numbers section for addresses and fax numbers), or fax using the number listed for your state. Use certified mail with return receipt or a confirmed fax transmission sheet as proof of timely filing.
    - Keep a date-stamped copy of Form 2553 permanently.

12. Wait for IRS acceptance. The IRS will mail a written acceptance letter confirming the S corporation election and its effective date. This typically takes four to eight weeks but may be longer. You can confirm receipt by calling the IRS Business Tax Line at 800-829-4933. Keep the acceptance letter permanently. Do not attempt to treat distributions as S corporation distributions before the election is accepted and effective.

13. File any required state-level election. Some states (including New York and New Jersey) require a separate state S corporation election form filed within a specific deadline; the federal Form 2553 is not sufficient in those states. Confirm your state's requirements with your advisor or your state's Department of Revenue before assuming the federal election covers everything.

**Phase 4: Set up payroll**

14. Enroll in EFTPS. Go to EFTPS.gov and register your business to make federal payroll tax deposits electronically. The IRS requires all federal payroll tax deposits to be made through EFTPS or an equivalent electronic funds transfer. Allow five to seven business days to receive your PIN by mail before running the first payroll.

15. Select a payroll service. Choose a payroll provider -- examples include Gusto, QuickBooks Payroll, ADP, Paychex, or a payroll module integrated with your bookkeeping software. A full-service payroll provider will calculate federal and state income tax withholding, Social Security and Medicare taxes, generate pay stubs, remit deposits through EFTPS, file Form 941 quarterly, and generate your W-2 by January 31. Having a payroll provider handle deposits and filings significantly reduces the risk of errors and missed deadlines.

16. Complete your W-4. As an employee of your own S corporation, complete IRS Form W-4 (Employee's Withholding Certificate) to tell the payroll system how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. Your payroll provider will use this form to set up your employee profile.

17. Set your pay schedule and run payroll regularly. Begin payroll on a consistent, predictable schedule (weekly, biweekly, or monthly) and maintain that schedule. Irregular or single annual payroll is a significant audit red flag. Your salary must be the reasonable compensation amount you documented in Step 9. Do not adjust the salary informally; formal changes require a written corporate resolution.

18. If you have employees other than yourself, ensure they are onboarded with Form W-4, Form I-9 (Employment Eligibility Verification), and any required new hire state reporting. All employees must be set up in the payroll system and their wages must run through payroll alongside yours.

**Phase 5: Run the S corporation and take distributions**

19. After paying yourself a salary through payroll each pay period, any remaining profit in the corporation may be distributed to you as a profit distribution. Distributions must be:
    - Supported by sufficient cash in the business bank account after all expenses, payroll taxes, and estimated income taxes are accounted for.
    - Documented with a written distribution resolution or a dated note in the corporate records noting the amount, date, and the fact that it is a distribution, not compensation.
    - Separate from payroll on the bank statement and in the bookkeeping system.
    - Never used to replace or supplement a salary that would otherwise be inadequate.

20. Track your accumulated adjustments account (AAA). Your accountant will compute the AAA each year on your Form 1120-S. Distributions up to the AAA balance are tax-free to you. Distributions in excess of your stock basis in the S corporation may be taxable as capital gains. Your accountant must track both the AAA and your personal stock basis in the S corporation each year using Form 7203.

**Phase 6: Annual compliance**

21. File Form 1120-S each year by March 15 (for calendar-year S corporations). The 1120-S is an information return. It does not result in corporate-level tax under normal circumstances, but it is still required every year the S corporation status is in effect. Attach a Schedule K-1 for each shareholder.

22. Report your K-1 income on your personal Form 1040. Your share of S corporation income shown on Schedule K-1 flows to your personal return and is taxed at your individual income tax rates. It is NOT subject to self-employment tax.

23. File Form 941 quarterly and Form 940 annually. See the Timing and Deadlines section for all due dates.

24. Issue W-2 forms to all employees (including yourself) and file with the Social Security Administration by January 31 each year.

25. Review and update your reasonable compensation at the start of each calendar year or whenever revenue changes materially. Revenue growth that is not matched by a salary increase is an IRS audit signal. Update your written compensation documentation annually.

## Timing And Deadlines

**For S corporation status effective January 1, 2026:**
- File Form 2553 by **March 15, 2026**, OR
- File Form 2553 at any time during 2025 (the preceding tax year).

**For S corporation status effective January 1, 2027:**
- File Form 2553 by March 15, 2027, OR
- File Form 2553 at any time during 2026.

**Late election relief:**
If you miss the deadline, you may request relief by filing Form 2553 within **3 years and 75 days** of the intended effective date, accompanied by a statement showing reasonable cause for the late filing and evidence that you have reported income consistent with S corporation status for all affected years. This relief is governed by Revenue Procedure 2013-30. If you do not qualify under that procedure, a private letter ruling (which is more expensive and time-consuming) is the only remaining path.

**Annual S corporation return (Form 1120-S):**
Due the 15th day of the 3rd month after the tax year ends. For calendar-year S corporations: **March 15** each year. An automatic 6-month extension is available by filing Form 7004 by the original due date.

**Quarterly payroll returns (Form 941):**
- Q1 (January through March): due **April 30**
- Q2 (April through June): due **July 31**
- Q3 (July through September): due **October 31**
- Q4 (October through December): due **January 31** of the following year

**Payroll tax deposits:**
New employers (in their first year) are monthly depositors: deposit the prior month's accumulated payroll taxes by the **15th of the following month** using EFTPS. After your first full year, the IRS assigns you a deposit schedule based on your lookback period. If your lookback period total employment taxes exceeded $50,000, you become a semi-weekly depositor. If any single day's accumulated payroll tax liability reaches $100,000, you must deposit by the next business day regardless of your regular schedule.

**Annual unemployment return (Form 940):**
Due **January 31** each year.

**W-2 forms:**
Must be provided to all employees (including you) and filed with the Social Security Administration by **January 31** each year.

**Annual compensation review:**
Conduct a written review and update of your reasonable compensation at the beginning of each calendar year, or whenever annual revenue changes by more than 20%. Update the corporate resolution if you change the salary amount.

**Estimated tax payments (Form 1040-ES):**
As an S corporation owner-employee, your salary will have income tax withheld through payroll, but your S corporation pass-through income on Schedule K-1 will not have withholding. You will likely need to make quarterly estimated income tax payments to cover the income tax due on your K-1 income. Estimated payment due dates for 2026: **April 15, June 16, September 15, 2026, and January 15, 2027**. Confirm with your advisor whether your payroll withholding is sufficient to cover your total 2026 tax liability or whether you need to make separate estimated payments.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Starting as an LLC without an S corporation election:**
You can form the LLC now for liability protection and remain on Schedule C for tax purposes. The S corporation election can be made later (by March 15 of the first year you want it effective, or at any time during the preceding year). This two-step approach makes sense if your current profit is below the break-even threshold but you expect growth, or if you want to establish the entity before committing to the additional payroll compliance burden.

**Mid-year elections:**
If you file Form 2553 after March 15 of the current calendar year, the election is normally effective January 1 of the following year. Plan for this timing; you cannot retroactively elect S corporation status for a year that has already ended.

**Payroll setup must begin immediately when the election takes effect:**
If your election is effective January 1, 2026, and you do not set up payroll until March or April, you will need to run catch-up payroll for the prior months and may owe payroll tax penalties and interest. Set up payroll before or immediately after filing Form 2553, and begin running payroll in the first month the election is effective.

**Changing your filing status from Schedule C:**
In your first year as an S corporation, your Schedule C on your personal Form 1040 will be replaced by Schedule K-1 income from the S corporation. Your W-2 wages from the S corporation also appear on your personal return (Box 1 of your W-2). Both are subject to income tax, but only the W-2 wages are subject to FICA. The K-1 income is not subject to self-employment tax.

**State conformity:**
Most states automatically recognize the federal S corporation election and do not impose entity-level income tax on the S corporation. However, many states impose annual LLC fees or minimum franchise taxes. Notable exceptions and additional considerations:
- **California:** Imposes an $800 minimum annual franchise tax on LLCs and a separate 1.5% California S corporation income tax. A separate California Form 3560 (S Corporation Election or Termination/Revocation) may be required. California's costs significantly reduce the after-tax benefit of the S corporation election for lower-revenue businesses in that state.
- **New York and New Jersey:** Require a separate state S corporation election filed within a specific deadline; the federal Form 2553 alone is not sufficient.
- **Texas:** Has a franchise ("margin") tax that may apply to entities in Texas. Confirm with your advisor.
- **Other states:** Several additional states impose S corporation-specific fees, recognition requirements, or separate election forms. Confirm your specific state's rules with your advisor before assuming the federal election is the only step required.

**Pass-through entity (PTE) tax election:**
More than 35 states now offer an elective pass-through entity tax, under which the S corporation pays state income tax at the entity level and deducts that payment as a federal business expense. This election was originally designed to work around the $10,000 federal SALT (state and local tax) deduction cap. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, Public Law 119-21), the federal SALT cap was raised to $40,000 for 2025 and $40,400 for 2026 (increasing approximately 1% per year through 2029, then reverting to $10,000 in 2030). The cap phases down for taxpayers with modified adjusted gross income above $500,000 (the phase-down threshold also increases approximately 1% annually through 2029), but may not fall below $10,000. The PTE election may still provide additional benefit for high-income owners or in states with high tax rates even after the cap increase. If your state offers this election, ask your advisor whether layering the PTE election on top of the S corporation election makes sense for your situation, taking into account the current SALT cap for your filing year.

**QBI deduction interaction with salary:**
The 20% QBI deduction under Section 199A applies to the pass-through profit portion of your S corporation income (reported on Schedule K-1), not to your W-2 salary. Above the income thresholds listed in The Numbers section, the QBI deduction for non-SSTB owners is limited to the greater of 50% of W-2 wages paid by the business or 25% of W-2 wages plus 2.5% of qualified property. Because your own salary is a W-2 wage of the S corporation, paying yourself a higher salary can actually help satisfy the W-2 wage limitation and support a larger QBI deduction at high income levels -- even though it reduces the pool of income eligible for the deduction. These two factors work in partial tension, and the combined effect should be modeled with your advisor at your specific income level.

**Self-employment tax deduction transition:**
As a Schedule C filer, you currently deduct 50% of your self-employment tax as an above-the-line deduction on Form 1040. After transitioning to an S corporation, this deduction disappears for income taken as distributions. However, the S corporation deducts the employer half of FICA on your salary as a business expense, which reduces your taxable K-1 income. The net effect is similar but not identical; your advisor should model both scenarios at your income level.

**Estimated tax payments:**
As an S corporation owner, you will need to ensure your total withholding (from W-2 payroll) plus any quarterly estimated payments (Form 1040-ES) is sufficient to avoid underpayment penalties. The withholding on your W-2 salary may not cover the income tax due on your K-1 pass-through income. Discuss estimated payment planning with your advisor before the first quarter of your first S corporation year.

**Reversion and revocation:**
Once you elect S corporation status, you must remain an S corporation unless you revoke the election (requiring consent of shareholders holding more than 50% of stock) or inadvertently violate an eligibility requirement (which terminates the election automatically). After a voluntary revocation, you generally cannot re-elect S corporation status for five tax years without IRS consent.

## Common Mistakes

**Forming the LLC but not opening a separate bank account.** An LLC without a separate business bank account offers weakened liability protection because courts can "pierce the corporate veil" (disregard the LLC as a separate entity) if the owner treats business and personal funds as interchangeable. The separate account is not optional.

**Assuming the LLC formation automatically creates tax benefits.** A single-member LLC that does not file Form 2553 is a disregarded entity. It still pays self-employment tax on all net profit, exactly like a bare sole proprietor. The liability protection is real; the tax savings require an additional election.

**Filing Form 2553 late and losing the entire year's tax savings.** Missing the March 15 deadline means the election takes effect the following January 1. There is no way to recover the lost savings for the missed year.

**Setting an unreasonably low salary.** The IRS can reclassify distributions as wages and assess back payroll taxes, plus interest and penalties. Courts have consistently upheld this reclassification. Your salary must be documented and defensible.

**Not setting up payroll at all.** Taking S corporation distributions without running any payroll defeats the purpose of the election. The IRS will treat all distributions as wages when no salary is paid.

**Running payroll irregularly or once per year.** A single year-end payroll payment after profits are known is a recognized audit red flag. Payroll must be consistent and regular.

**Failing to document reasonable compensation.** Even a salary at the right level is vulnerable if there is no contemporaneous written record showing how the amount was determined. Maintain a dated memo each year.

**Forgetting to file a state-level S corporation election.** In New York, New Jersey, and some other states, the federal election alone is not sufficient. Verify your state's requirements before concluding that filing Form 2553 completes the process.

**Not making estimated tax payments on K-1 income.** Payroll withholding on your salary may not cover the income tax due on your pass-through distributions. Underpayment penalties compound quarterly. Build estimated payments into your cash flow plan from day one.

**Ignoring the accumulated adjustments account (AAA) and stock basis.** Distributions in excess of your stock basis are taxable as capital gains. If your accountant is not tracking your AAA and personal stock basis annually using Form 7203, you may face unexpected tax bills and lose the ability to deduct any S corporation losses.

**Commingling salary and distribution payments.** Salary must flow through payroll (with W-2, FICA withholding, and EFTPS deposits). Distributions must be separate, documented transactions from the business bank account. Never pay yourself informally and label it payroll or a distribution after the fact.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before acting if any of the following apply:

1. **You are unsure whether an LLC, an LLC with S corporation election, a C corporation, or another entity type is the right structure for your business.** The choice of entity has tax, legal, and operational consequences that depend on your income level, state, growth plans, and exit strategy. Do not file any formation documents until you have discussed the options with your advisor.

2. **Your expected net profit is below $40,000 to $50,000.** The S corporation election compliance costs may exceed the payroll tax savings. Confirm the net benefit with your advisor before electing.

3. **You have any non-U.S. individuals, corporate entities, or partnerships as current or planned co-owners.** These ownership structures disqualify the S corporation election. Do not file Form 2553 until your advisor confirms all owners qualify.

4. **You operate in California, New York, New Jersey, Texas, or any other state where you are uncertain about state recognition, state-level election requirements, or franchise taxes.** State costs and requirements must be reviewed and modeled before the election.

5. **You want the election effective for a year that has already begun and you are past the March 15 deadline.** Late election relief under Revenue Procedure 2013-30 requires a reasonable cause statement. Your advisor needs to prepare and review that statement before you file.

6. **You plan to bring in investors, co-owners, or partners in the next two to three years.** Adding the wrong type of shareholder terminates the S corporation election automatically. Discuss ownership plans with your advisor before electing.

7. **You are planning to sell the business or transfer significant assets to the LLC at or around the time of formation.** Asset transfers, valuation, and tax basis issues require analysis before action.

8. **You have not yet determined a defensible reasonable compensation amount and documented the basis for it.** Do not begin operating as an S corporation and taking distributions until the salary is in place and documented.

9. **You currently have employees as a sole proprietor.** Transitioning to an LLC that elects S corporation status changes the employer of record for those employees, which may trigger state payroll registration, unemployment insurance account changes, and workers' compensation policy updates. Confirm the transition plan with your advisor and your payroll service.

10. **You are considering the S corporation election AND your state offers a pass-through entity tax election.** These two elections interact and should be coordinated. Do not file either one without reviewing the combined impact with your advisor.

## Before You Act

This playbook teaches the mechanics of transitioning from a Schedule C sole proprietorship to a formal entity and, where appropriate, electing S corporation tax treatment. It is educational guidance, not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Every step in this guide involves facts specific to you -- your income level, your state, your role, your ownership plans, and your overall tax picture -- that your advisor must review before you form an entity, file any election, run payroll, take any distribution, or change how your business is taxed.
