# Business Travel Trip Memo Kit

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

---

## Overview

When a business owner travels away from home overnight for legitimate business purposes, the costs of that travel -- transportation, lodging, and a portion of meals -- are deductible business expenses under IRC Section 162 and the rules in IRS Publication 463. The deduction is only as strong as the documentation behind it. A trip memo (a short written record created before or immediately at the start of a trip) establishes the business purpose in your own words, at the time closest to when you know it best. The attached Business Travel Trip Memo template is the worksheet that captures every element the IRS requires for substantiation in a single two-page document. Used consistently on every business trip, it converts good travel spending into clean, audit-proof deductions.

---

## Who This Applies To

This strategy applies to you if ALL of the following are true:

- You own or operate a business (sole proprietorship, single-member LLC, S-corp, C-corp, partnership, or multi-member LLC), AND
- You travel overnight away from your regular place of business for a legitimate business reason (client meetings, conferences, vendor visits, scouting a new market, speaking engagements, professional training directly related to your current business, etc.), AND
- The trip is temporary -- expected to last one year or less at any single location.

This applies whether you are self-employed (filing Schedule C), an S-corp owner-employee, a C-corp owner-employee, or a partner who travels for the partnership's business.

---

## When This Is Not A Fit

**Do not claim the business travel deduction -- and do not use this kit -- if any of the following apply:**

- **The trip is primarily personal.** If the majority of your days on the trip are personal days (vacation, family visits, sightseeing), the round-trip transportation cost is entirely nondeductible and only specific, isolated business expenses at the destination may be deductible. See "Primary Purpose Test" in The Numbers section.
- **You are a W-2 employee (not an owner).** The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 suspended unreimbursed employee business expense deductions for most employees through 2025. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed July 4, 2025, permanently eliminated this deduction starting in 2026. If you are a regular W-2 employee, ask your employer to reimburse travel costs through an accountable plan instead; you cannot deduct unreimbursed travel expenses on your own return.
- **The assignment is indefinite (expected to last more than one year).** Expenses for travel to a location where you are working on an assignment expected to exceed one year -- or that in fact exceeds one year -- are not deductible travel expenses. The location becomes your new tax home.
- **The travel is local (no overnight stay required).** If you drive to a meeting across town and return the same day without needing sleep away from home, that is a local business transportation expense (use the standard mileage rate), not a deductible travel expense under the away-from-home rules. The trip memo kit is designed for overnight travel only.
- **Expenses are lavish or extravagant.** The IRS disallows deductions for costs that are unreasonable given the purpose of the trip. First-class airfare and penthouse suites are not automatically denied, but you must be prepared to defend them as reasonable and ordinary for your business context.
- **The trip is to attend a convention outside North America.** Special additional rules apply; confirm with your advisor before booking.

## Key Terms

**Tax home:** The city or general area of your main regular place of business or work, regardless of where your personal home or family is located. If you have no regular place of business, your tax home may be where you live. You must be traveling away from your tax home for the travel expense deduction to apply. (IRS Publication 463, Chapter 1.)

**Away from home:** You are traveling away from home when your duties require you to be away from your tax home substantially longer than an ordinary day's work AND you need sleep or rest to meet the demands of your work while away. A brief nap in a car does not satisfy the rest requirement; an actual period of sleep away from home is required. (IRC Section 162(a)(2); IRS Publication 463.)

**Ordinary and necessary:** "Ordinary" means the expense is common and accepted in your trade or business. "Necessary" means it is helpful and appropriate for your work. Both tests must be met. Unusual or personal expenses do not qualify even if paid during a business trip.

**Primary purpose test:** For domestic (U.S.) business trips that mix business and personal days, the IRS looks at whether the trip is primarily for business. The key factor is days: if business days exceed personal days, transportation to and from your destination is fully deductible and daily lodging and meals are deductible only on the business days. If personal days exceed business days, none of the transportation is deductible and only specific business expenses at the destination may be deductible.

**Business days:** Days on which you spend the majority of your working hours on business activities. Travel days (days you are in transit to or from the business destination) count as business days. Weekends and holidays that fall between two business days also count as business days even if you do no business on those intervening days -- but only if they fall between business days, not if you stay on after business is done for personal reasons.

**Substantiation:** The IRS process of proving an expense by adequate records. For travel expenses under IRC Section 274(d), the IRS requires you to prove four elements for each expenditure: (1) the amount, (2) the time (date and number of days), (3) the place (destination), and (4) the business purpose. Without adequate substantiation, the deduction is disallowed entirely -- even if the IRS believes you spent the money.

**Adequate records:** Under Treasury Regulation 1.274-5A, adequate records means an account book, diary, log, statement of expense, or similar record PLUS supporting documentary evidence. Together they must establish each required element. Records made at or near the time of the expense have a higher degree of credibility than records reconstructed later.

**Contemporaneous:** Made at or near the time of the event or expense. The IRS specifically requires that records be contemporaneous. A trip memo written on the day you leave (or the night before) is contemporaneous. A memo written six months later from memory is reconstructed and carries much less weight.

**Documentary evidence:** Receipts, paid bills, credit card statements, or similar documents proving an expense was paid. Required for ALL lodging (regardless of amount) and for any other individual expense of $75 or more. Below $75 (except lodging), a receipt is not required by the employer's accountable plan rules, but you should still log the expense with all four elements in writing.

**IRC Section 162:** The Internal Revenue Code provision that allows deduction of ordinary and necessary business expenses, including travel while away from home.

**IRC Section 274(d):** The provision that imposes the strict substantiation requirement for travel, meals, and certain other expenses. Without meeting Section 274(d), no deduction is allowed regardless of the actual expenditure.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The tax form used by sole proprietors and single-member LLCs to report business profit or loss. Business travel expenses are reported on Line 24a (travel) and Line 24b (deductible meals, at 50%).

**Form 1120S / Form 1065:** The tax returns for S-corporations and partnerships, respectively. Travel deductions flow through the entity return.

**Per diem:** A fixed daily allowance that can substitute for tracking actual meal and incidental expenses. The IRS accepts per diem payments up to the applicable GSA or high-low rate as substantiated without requiring individual meal receipts, but you still must document the trip date, destination, and business purpose.

**M&IE rate (Meals and Incidental Expenses rate):** The GSA-published daily allowance for food and incidentals for each location. For FY 2026, the standard CONUS (continental U.S.) M&IE rate is $68 per day; the high-low method M&IE rate is $86 per day for high-cost localities and $74 per day for all other CONUS localities.

**Standard mileage rate:** The IRS-published per-mile rate for deducting business use of your personal vehicle. For 2026, the rate is 72.5 cents per mile (IRS Notice 2026-10, effective January 1, 2026). Use this rate on your mileage log for driving to the airport, between hotels and meeting locations, and for local transportation at your destination.

**OBBBA:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025. Among other changes, it permanently eliminated miscellaneous itemized deductions (including unreimbursed employee business expenses) for most W-2 employees starting in 2026, making it impossible for those employees to deduct unreimbursed travel costs on their own returns.

**Accountable plan:** A written employer reimbursement policy that meets three IRS tests (business connection, substantiation, and return of excess), causing reimbursements to be excluded from the employee's wages. If you operate as an S-corp or C-corp and your business reimburses your travel costs through an accountable plan, the reimbursement is tax-free to you and deductible by the business. (Treasury Regulation 1.62-2.)

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before your trip and before completing the Trip Memo:

1. **Business purpose:** A clear, specific description of why you are making the trip -- not "business meeting" but "meeting with ABC Distributors to negotiate 2026 supply contract and conduct facility inspection."
2. **Itinerary with dates:** Your full travel schedule showing departure and return dates, destination city and state, and each day's planned business activities.
3. **Names and roles of people you will meet:** Who are the clients, vendors, partners, or other business contacts? What are their names and companies?
4. **Estimated budget by category:** Airfare or mileage, lodging per night, estimated meal costs, estimated incidentals (taxi, parking, baggage).
5. **Entity structure:** Know whether you are filing Schedule C, operating through an S-corp, C-corp, or partnership -- because where you deduct the expense differs.
6. **Payroll / accountable plan status (if you are an S-corp or C-corp owner):** Confirm with your advisor whether travel costs will be reimbursed by the entity under an accountable plan or paid personally and deducted on the business return.
7. **Prior-year mileage log (if driving):** Your cumulative business mileage for the year to date, so you can add this trip's miles correctly.

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect before the trip:**
- Completed Trip Memo (the attached template) -- filled out as fully as possible before departure
- Airline, train, or bus booking confirmation
- Hotel reservation confirmation
- Meeting invitation, conference registration, or written evidence of business appointments

**Collect during and immediately after the trip:**
- All lodging receipts or folios (required regardless of amount)
- Receipts for all other individual expenses of $75 or more (airfare, car rental, taxi/rideshare, parking, business meals)
- Written log of expenses under $75 (with date, amount, location, and business purpose for each)
- Mileage log if driving your own vehicle (date, start and end odometer or total miles, from/to locations, business purpose)
- Business-related documentation created during the trip: meeting agendas, signed contracts, conference programs, client notes, or emails summarizing what was discussed and decided
- Names and titles of all people present at business meals (required for meal deduction substantiation)

**Retention:**
Keep all trip records -- the Trip Memo, receipts, and supporting documents -- for a minimum of three years from the date you file the tax return on which the deduction is claimed. The standard IRS audit window is three years from the filing date (IRC Section 6501(a)). If your return is selected for examination, you must be able to produce these records on demand.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Before the Trip

**Step 1: Confirm the trip qualifies.**
Ask yourself: Is my tax home in a different city or general area than where I am going? Will I be away long enough to require sleep? Is the trip's primary purpose business? Is the assignment expected to last one year or less? If the answer to all four is yes, proceed. If you are unsure, stop and confirm with your advisor before booking.

**Step 2: Open the Business Travel Trip Memo template.**
Download the attached "Business Travel Trip Memo" worksheet. Fill in Part 1 (Trip Identification) immediately: your name, entity name, trip title, destination city, departure date, and return date.

**Step 3: Write the business purpose in specific terms.**
In the Business Purpose field on the Trip Memo, write a sentence or two describing exactly what business outcome this trip is designed to accomplish. Be specific: name the client or vendor, describe the transaction or project, and state what decision, agreement, or result you expect. "Attending annual industry conference to meet with three prospective distributors and attend two sessions on supply-chain compliance relevant to our product line" is good. "Business meeting" is not enough.

**Step 4: List the people and companies you will meet.**
In the Contacts section of the Trip Memo, list each person's name, company, and the business relationship (prospective client, current vendor, strategic partner, etc.). For business meals, this list is your substantiation of the business relationship requirement.

**Step 5: Enter your planned itinerary day-by-day.**
In the Daily Schedule section, write what you plan to do on each day of the trip. Mark each day as Business (B), Personal (P), or Travel (T). Count your planned business days vs. personal days. If personal days will outnumber business days, stop and consult your advisor before the trip -- the transportation may not be deductible.

**Step 6: Keep the Trip Memo with your travel documents.**
Print it or save it to your phone. You will add to it during and after the trip.

---

### During the Trip

**Step 7: Collect every lodging receipt.**
At checkout, get a printed or emailed itemized folio showing dates, room rate, and total. Do not rely on your credit card statement alone -- it does not show the business purpose or itemized charges.

**Step 8: Collect receipts for expenses of $75 or more.**
For each individual expense of $75 or more (airfare, car rental, taxi/rideshare fare, restaurant meals, parking), keep the receipt or save the digital confirmation. Note the business purpose on the receipt or in your log immediately.

**Step 9: Log expenses under $75.**
For cash tips, small taxi fares, and other expenses under $75, record each one on the Trip Memo's expense log with date, amount, vendor name, and business purpose before the end of that day.

**Step 10: Note actual business vs. personal days.**
At the end of each day, update the Daily Schedule on your Trip Memo: mark the day as Business (B), Personal (P), or Travel (T) based on what actually happened. This record will determine which lodging and meal expenses are deductible.

**Step 11: Record any business meals separately.**
For each business meal, note on the receipt or in your log: the date, restaurant name and city, total amount, names and business roles of everyone present, and the business topic or purpose discussed. Meals during travel days (when you are in transit) are also deductible as travel meals.

**Step 12: Log all business driving.**
If you drive your personal vehicle at the destination (to a meeting, from the airport, etc.), record the date, starting and ending locations, total miles, and business purpose in your mileage log or in the Trip Memo's vehicle section.

---

### After the Trip (Within 60 Days of Return)

**Step 13: Complete the Trip Memo.**
Fill in the Actual Results section: list every expense by category, total the business days vs. personal days, and confirm the business purpose was carried out. Attach or staple all receipts.

**Step 14: Calculate your deductible amounts.**
Using the Trip Memo's expense summary:
- Transportation (airfare, train, mileage, car rental): fully deductible if business days exceed personal days
- Lodging: deductible only for business days and travel days; not deductible for personal days
- Meals: deductible at 50% for business days and travel days (see The Numbers section for the exact rate)
- Other ordinary and necessary expenses (baggage fees, business calls, laundry for extended trips, tips on covered services): deductible on business days

**Step 15: Submit to your bookkeeper or enter in your accounting software.**
Record the deductible amounts by category in your books. If you operate through an S-corp or C-corp with an accountable plan, submit the completed Trip Memo and receipts to your entity within 60 days of returning. The entity reimburses you and deducts the cost. If you are a sole proprietor or single-member LLC, record the expenses on Schedule C for the tax year of the trip.

**Step 16: File the completed Trip Memo and all supporting documents.**
Create a folder (physical or digital) labeled with the trip name and dates. Store the Trip Memo, all receipts, the itinerary, meeting confirmations, and any business documents produced during the trip. Retain this folder for at least three years from the filing date of the return on which the deduction is claimed.

---

## Timing And Deadlines

**Before the trip:** Complete Parts 1-3 of the Trip Memo (identification, business purpose, planned itinerary) before or on your departure date.

**During the trip:** Record expenses and update actual vs. planned activities daily -- do not wait until the end of the trip to reconstruct.

**Within 60 days of return:** If you operate through an S-corp or C-corp accountable plan, submit your completed Trip Memo and receipts to the entity within 60 days of returning. This 60-day window is the IRS safe harbor under Treasury Regulation 1.62-2(g)(2) for the substantiation requirement of an accountable plan. Submissions after 60 days can still be processed, but they fall outside the safe harbor and may be treated as a nonaccountable plan arrangement (meaning the reimbursement becomes taxable wages).

**Year-end (December 31):** For sole proprietors and single-member LLCs, all business travel expenses for the year must be reflected in your books before year-end for the tax year you are deducting them. Trips taken in December should be logged and submitted by December 31.

**Tax filing date:** Deductions are claimed on the return for the tax year in which the trip occurred, due by April 15 of the following year (or the extended due date if you file an extension).

**Record retention:** Keep Trip Memos and all supporting records for at least three years from the date you file the return. Example: a 2026 trip deducted on a 2026 return filed April 15, 2027 should be retained until at least April 15, 2030.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**S-corp and C-corp owners -- use an accountable plan:**
If you operate through an S-corp or C-corp, the cleanest way to handle business travel is to have the entity reimburse you under a written accountable plan. The reimbursement is deductible by the entity and excluded from your W-2 wages. Without a plan, you must pay business travel costs from personal after-tax funds (because OBBBA permanently eliminated unreimbursed employee expense deductions), and the entity gets no deduction unless you have a different arrangement in place. Confirm your plan is in writing with your advisor.

**Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs:**
Deduct travel expenses directly on Schedule C, Line 24a (travel) and Line 24b (deductible meal expenses at 50%). No accountable plan is needed because you are the business. Keep the same records described above.

**Partners in a partnership:**
Partners are generally not employees of the partnership. Travel reimbursements paid to partners are often treated as guaranteed payments or expense reimbursements under the partnership agreement, not under an accountable plan. The tax treatment can differ significantly. Ask your advisor how your partnership agreement handles travel costs before assuming deductibility.

**State income tax caveats:**
State deductibility of business travel generally follows federal rules, but several states have differences:
- California, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania impose their own rules on employee expense deductions that do not always mirror federal law. Confirm state treatment with your advisor.
- Some states have not conformed to all OBBBA changes. Check your state's conformity status for tax year 2026.
- If you travel across state lines and conduct business in multiple states, you may have nexus (a taxable presence) in those states. One business trip generally does not create nexus, but a pattern of trips can. Ask your advisor if you travel frequently to the same state.

**One-year rule monitoring:**
If you begin a temporary work assignment expecting it to end within one year and circumstances change so that it is now expected to exceed one year, your travel expenses become nondeductible starting on the date you determined it would exceed one year. Flag this scenario immediately with your advisor.

**OBBBA meal deduction change (effective January 1, 2026):**
The OBBBA eliminated the deduction for employer-provided convenience meals (on-premises cafeteria meals, meals provided for the employer's convenience on business premises). This does NOT affect travel meals while you are away from home, which remain 50% deductible. The two categories should not be confused.

**Mixed business and personal travel outside the continental U.S.:**
Different allocation rules apply to international trips. The primary purpose test and day-counting rules for international travel are more complex than for domestic travel. Use a separate worksheet and consult your advisor before deducting transportation costs for any international business trip with personal days.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Vague or generic business purpose on the Trip Memo.**
Writing "client meeting" or "business trip" is not adequate substantiation. The IRS requires you to state the nature of the business benefit derived or expected. Be specific: name the client, describe the transaction or project, and say what outcome was expected.

**2. Claiming a personal-primary trip as a business trip.**
If you book a vacation and tack on one conference call, that is not a business trip. Count the days honestly. If personal days exceed business days, do not deduct transportation. Misclassifying personal trips as business travel is one of the most frequently audited items on Schedule C.

**3. Missing lodging receipts.**
Lodging receipts are required for ALL amounts, with no exception. Relying on a credit card statement is not enough -- it does not show the dates, daily rate, or incidentals. Always get an itemized folio at checkout.

**4. Deducting 100% of meals.**
Meals while traveling are only 50% deductible. Deducting the full amount is an error that will be adjusted in an audit.

**5. Failing to log expenses under $75.**
You do not need a receipt for expenses under $75 (other than lodging), but you still need a written log with the four required elements (amount, date, place, business purpose). An undocumented $40 taxi fare is not deductible even though no receipt was required.

**6. Counting weekend days that come after the last business activity.**
You can count Saturday and Sunday as business days only if they fall between two business days. If your last business meeting is Thursday and you stay through the weekend for personal reasons before flying home Monday, Saturday and Sunday are personal days, and lodging and meals for those days are not deductible.

**7. Reconstructing records after the fact.**
The IRS gives significantly less weight to records created weeks or months after the trip. A reconstructed log based on calendar entries and credit card statements is better than nothing, but it is weaker evidence than a Trip Memo completed during or immediately after the trip. The IRS can -- and does -- disallow deductions entirely when records are clearly reconstructed.

**8. Not separating business and personal expenses on a mixed trip.**
Even on a primarily-business trip, your personal expenses (upgraded flight for a side vacation, hotel nights for personal days, non-business meals with family) are NOT deductible. Keep them on a separate credit card or track them separately and do not include them in the Trip Memo expense summary.

**9. Using a per diem rate above the published limit.**
If you reimburse yourself or employees at a per diem rate above the applicable GSA or high-low rate, the excess is taxable compensation. Confirm your per diem rate against the current published rates each year.

**10. Deducting travel for a spouse, dependent, or companion who is not an employee with a genuine business purpose.**
Travel costs for a companion are not deductible unless the companion is your employee, the travel is for a genuine business purpose, and the expenses would otherwise be deductible by that person. The companion's personal presence during a business trip does not convert personal expenses to business expenses.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and confirm with your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

- **Before the trip:** Any trip where you are unsure whether business days will outnumber personal days, or where the trip has both business and vacation elements -- confirm the primary purpose test result and the specific days that will be counted before you book.
- **New or unusual destination:** Any trip to a non-U.S. location, or a domestic location where you will conduct business across state lines for the first time, to evaluate nexus risk.
- **Assignment length approaching one year:** Any temporary assignment that may run longer than originally planned. The moment you expect the assignment to exceed one year, your travel expenses at that location become nondeductible. Get guidance before that date arrives.
- **High per diem or premium travel costs:** If your lodging or total daily travel costs materially exceed the published GSA or high-low per diem benchmarks, confirm with your advisor that the amounts are defensible as ordinary and necessary and not lavish or extravagant.
- **S-corp or C-corp reimbursement setup:** If you have not yet confirmed you have a written accountable plan in place, or if this is your first time submitting travel expenses for reimbursement through the entity, review the plan document and submission process with your advisor first.
- **Partnership travel:** Before claiming any partnership-paid travel expense or guaranteed payment treatment for travel costs, confirm the correct treatment with your advisor.
- **Large or multi-week trips:** Any trip over five business days or involving significant total expenditure where the deduction is material to your tax position -- review the documentation package with your advisor before filing.

## Using The Download(s)

**Attached download: Business Travel Trip Memo**

This two-page template is the core tool this playbook is built around. Here is how to use it at each stage:

**Part 1 -- Trip Identification (complete before departure):**
Fill in your name, entity name, trip name or title (e.g., "Q2 Client Visit -- Chicago"), destination city and state, departure date, and expected return date. This locks in the basic facts while they are current.

**Part 2 -- Business Purpose Statement (complete before departure):**
Write 2-4 sentences describing the specific business reason for the trip. Name the clients, vendors, conferences, or other activities. This is the most important field on the form. The IRS looks here first to understand why you traveled.

**Part 3 -- Business Contacts (complete before and during the trip):**
List each person you will meet: full name, company name, and business relationship (client, vendor, partner, prospect). Add anyone you actually met if they were not on the original list.

**Part 4 -- Day-by-Day Schedule (complete before departure; update during and after):**
Enter each calendar day of the trip. Before you leave, note the planned activity for each day and mark it B (business), P (personal), or T (travel). After each day, confirm or update the actual activity. This day log is what determines how many lodging and meal days are deductible.

**Part 5 -- Expense Log (complete during and immediately after the trip):**
Use this section to record every expense with date, vendor, amount, and business purpose. Note whether you have a receipt. For each business meal, list the attendees and business topic. Attach receipts to the completed form.

**Part 6 -- Deductible Summary (complete after the trip, before submitting to bookkeeper):**
Tally total business days and personal days. Calculate the deductible amounts for each category: transportation (business-primary trips: 100%; personal-primary trips: 0%), lodging (business days only), meals (business days only, at 50%), other deductible expenses. This is the number that goes into your books or your accountable plan reimbursement request.

Keep the completed Trip Memo and all attached receipts together in a single file, named with the trip date and destination, for your records.

## Before You Act

This playbook teaches you the IRS rules and gives you a framework to implement them. It is educational, not personalized advice. Before you:

- claim a deduction for any business trip
- submit expenses through your S-corp or C-corp accountable plan
- treat a mixed personal/business trip as primarily business
- apply a per diem rate instead of tracking actual expenses
- deduct travel costs for a companion or family member

confirm with your advisor that your specific facts qualify, that your entity structure is set up to handle the deduction correctly, and that your state follows the federal rules described here.

Errors in business travel deductions are frequently audited. A completed Trip Memo with contemporaneous records is your defense. A vague memo or missing receipts is an invitation for disallowance.

2. **IRS Publication 463 (2025), Travel, Gift, and Car Expenses** -- primary purpose test for domestic trips, business day counting (weekend days between business days), five substantiation elements, standard meal allowance, per diem rules, record retention.
   https://www.irs.gov/publications/p463

3. **IRS Newsroom: Understanding Business Travel Deductions** -- deductible expense categories, Schedule C and Schedule F filing, substantiation overview.
   https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/understanding-business-travel-deductions

4. **26 CFR Section 1.274-5A, Substantiation Requirements** -- four required elements for travel expenses (amount, time, place, business purpose), adequate records standard, contemporaneous requirement, lodging documentary evidence rule.
   https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.274-5A

5. **26 U.S. Code Section 274, Disallowance of Certain Entertainment, Etc., Expenses** -- Section 274(d) strict substantiation requirement; Section 274(n) 50% meal limit.
   https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/274

6. **IRS Notice 2026-10, Standard Mileage Rates for 2026** -- 2026 business mileage rate of 72.5 cents per mile, effective January 1, 2026.
   https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-sets-2026-business-standard-mileage-rate-at-725-cents-per-mile-up-25-cents

7. **IRS Notice 2025-54, Special Per Diem Rates 2025-2026** -- high-low substantiation method rates: $319 per day for high-cost localities ($233 lodging + $86 M&IE), $225 per day for all other CONUS localities ($151 lodging + $74 M&IE); incidental-expenses-only rate $5 per day; effective October 1, 2025.
   https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-25-54.pdf

8. **GSA FY 2026 CONUS Per Diem Rates** -- standard CONUS rate of $178 per day ($110 lodging + $68 M&IE), effective October 1, 2025.
   https://www.gsa.gov/travel/plan-book/per-diem-rates

9. **IRS: How Long Should I Keep Records?** -- record retention minimum of 3 years from filing date for business expense documents supporting deductions.
   https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/how-long-should-i-keep-records

10. **Treasury Regulation 1.62-2** -- accountable plan three-test requirement; 60-day safe harbor for substantiation; 120-day safe harbor for return of excess.
    (Cited by reference; see IRS Publication 463 and IRS Topic 311 for accessible summaries.)

11. **One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025** -- permanent elimination of miscellaneous itemized deductions (unreimbursed employee business expenses) starting 2026; elimination of employer-provided convenience meal deductions starting January 1, 2026.
    https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/one-big-beautiful-bill-provisions

12. **IRS Instructions for Schedule C (Form 1040), 2025** -- Line 24a for travel expenses; Line 24b for deductible meals.
    https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sc

13. **IRS About Form 2106, Employee Business Expenses** -- confirms Form 2106 is available only to armed forces reservists, qualified performing artists, fee-basis state or local government officials, and employees with impairment-related work expenses (not general employees) for tax year 2026.
    https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-2106
