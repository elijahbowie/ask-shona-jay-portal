# LLC to S-Corp Conversion

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

An LLC (limited liability company) that elects to be taxed as an S corporation pays payroll taxes only on the owner's salary, not on all business profits. The remaining profits are distributed to owners as non-salary income that is not subject to the 15.3% self-employment tax. For business owners with consistent net profits above roughly $50,000 to $70,000 per year, the annual payroll tax savings typically exceed the added compliance costs (payroll processing, a separate S-corp tax return, state fees). The election is made by filing one IRS form and does not require dissolving or restructuring the LLC under state law.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is most valuable for:

- **Single-member LLC owners taxed as sole proprietors** who consistently net $50,000 or more per year from the business and pay self-employment tax on all of that profit.
- **Multi-member LLCs taxed as partnerships** where the active, working members want to reduce the portion of business income subject to payroll taxes.
- **Service-business owners** (consultants, coaches, designers, health practitioners, real estate agents, insurance agents, and similar) whose income is driven primarily by their own labor.
- **Business owners already planning to hire themselves as an employee** and run payroll, who want the profits above their salary to avoid payroll tax.
- **Owners planning at least several more years in the business**, because the setup investment and ongoing compliance costs require a multi-year runway to pay off.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Net profit consistently below $40,000 to $50,000.** The cost of payroll setup, quarterly payroll filings (Form 941), an annual S-corp tax return (Form 1120-S), potential state fees, and bookkeeping overhead typically runs $3,000 to $6,000 per year. Below this profit level, those costs often erase the payroll tax savings. The break-even varies by state and accountant fees; confirm with your advisor.
- **Owners who cannot commit to running payroll on a regular schedule.** An S-corp owner-employee must receive a regular salary that is reported on a W-2 and processed through payroll. Missing or irregular payroll is a significant audit red flag.
- **Businesses with inconsistent or seasonal income** where profits fluctuate sharply year to year. Setting a salary and then having an unexpectedly low-profit year can create cash-flow problems and may not produce meaningful tax savings.
- **LLCs that do not qualify for S-corp status.** An S-corp cannot have more than 100 shareholders, cannot have non-U.S. shareholders, cannot have shareholders that are corporations or partnerships, and can have only one class of stock. If your LLC has investors or a complex ownership structure, these rules may disqualify it.
- **C corporation conversions with significant appreciated assets.** If you are converting from a C corporation (not an LLC), assets that appreciated before the S-corp election may be taxed under the built-in gains rules for five years after the election. This does not typically apply to LLCs electing S-corp treatment for the first time, but confirm with your advisor.
- **States with high S-corp franchise taxes or mandatory fees.** California, for example, imposes a franchise tax equal to the greater of 1.5% of net income or the $800 annual minimum franchise tax. These costs can significantly reduce or eliminate the payroll tax savings for lower-revenue businesses in those states.
- **Owners planning to sell the business in the near term.** The S-corp election can complicate a business sale and may affect how the sale is taxed. Discuss timing with your advisor before electing if a sale is possible within the next two to three years.

## Key Terms

**LLC (limited liability company):** A business entity formed under state law that provides owners (called members) with personal liability protection. By default, a single-member LLC is taxed as a sole proprietorship (the IRS ignores it as a separate entity, and all profit goes on Schedule C of the owner's personal return). A multi-member LLC is taxed as a partnership by default.

**S corporation (S-corp):** Not a type of entity, but a tax classification. An S corporation is a corporation or eligible LLC that has elected to be treated as a "pass-through" entity under Subchapter S of the Internal Revenue Code. The S-corp itself pays no federal income tax (with limited exceptions). Instead, profits and losses pass through to the shareholders and are reported on their personal returns.

**Self-employment tax (SE tax):** The tax that sole proprietors and partners pay in place of the payroll taxes that employers and employees normally split. The self-employment tax rate is 15.3%, which covers 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. You pay this tax on your net business profit, not just a salary portion of it.

**FICA (Federal Insurance Contributions Act) taxes:** The payroll taxes paid on wages. Both the employer and employee each pay 6.2% for Social Security (for 2026, on wages up to $184,500) and 1.45% for Medicare (no cap). The combined FICA rate on wages is 15.3%, split evenly between employer and employee. An S-corp owner-employee pays FICA only on their salary, not on profit distributions above the salary.

**Social Security wage base:** The maximum amount of wages or self-employment income subject to Social Security tax in a given year. For 2026, the wage base is $184,500. Wages and net self-employment income above this amount are not subject to the 12.4% Social Security tax, though Medicare tax continues with no cap.

**Additional Medicare Tax:** A 0.9% tax on wages exceeding $200,000 (for single filers) or $250,000 (for married filing jointly) in a calendar year. Employers withhold this on wages above $200,000 per employee and there is no employer match. The threshold for the additional tax is based on total wages across all jobs; the employee reconciles the full amount on Form 8959 of their personal return.

**Reasonable compensation:** The salary an S-corp must pay its owner-employees before taking any profit distributions. The IRS requires this salary to reflect what a similarly qualified person doing the same work would be paid in the open market. There is no fixed minimum or formula; the IRS uses a facts-and-circumstances test. Reasonable compensation is the single most audited aspect of S-corp taxation.

**Profit distribution:** A payment from the S-corp to a shareholder that is NOT wages. Distributions come from the S-corp's after-salary profits and are not subject to FICA or self-employment tax. This is the mechanism through which S-corp owners save on payroll taxes. Distributions cannot substitute for reasonable compensation.

**Pass-through taxation:** The system by which S-corp income, losses, deductions, and credits flow to shareholders' individual tax returns in proportion to their ownership. The S-corp files an information return (Form 1120-S) but the entity itself generally does not pay federal income tax on profits.

**Form 2553:** The IRS form titled "Election by a Small Business Corporation" used to elect S-corp tax treatment. A single-member LLC files this one form to simultaneously elect corporate tax classification and S-corp status; no separate Form 8832 is required.

**Form 8832:** The IRS form used to change an entity's tax classification. An LLC filing Form 2553 is deemed to have made the corporate classification election automatically; Form 8832 is not required for an LLC making an S-corp election.

**Form 1120-S:** The annual S corporation income tax return filed with the IRS. Unlike a Schedule C on a personal return or a Form 1065 partnership return, the 1120-S is a stand-alone corporate-style return. It is due by the 15th day of the third month after the tax year ends (March 15 for calendar-year S-corps, or March 16 in 2026 when March 15 falls on a Sunday).

**Schedule K-1 (Form 1120-S):** A statement the S-corp prepares for each shareholder, showing that shareholder's allocated share of income, deductions, and credits. Shareholders use Schedule K-1 to complete their personal Form 1040.

**Form 941:** The quarterly employer payroll tax return. S-corp owner-employees are employees of their own S-corp and the S-corp must file Form 941 each quarter reporting wages paid and payroll taxes withheld and remitted. Due dates are April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31.

**Form 940:** The annual federal unemployment tax (FUTA) return. S-corps must file this for any employee (including owner-employees) whose wages exceed $1,500 in any quarter.

**Form W-2:** The wage statement the S-corp provides each employee (including the owner-employee) by January 31 each year, showing wages paid, payroll taxes withheld, and, for 2% or more shareholders, health insurance premiums included in taxable wages.

**EFTPS (Electronic Federal Tax Payment System):** The free IRS system for making federal tax deposits electronically. S-corps must enroll in EFTPS and use it to remit payroll tax deposits.

**QBI deduction (Section 199A):** A deduction on the owner's personal return equal to up to 20% of qualified business income (the S-corp profits that pass through to the shareholder's return, excluding the W-2 salary). Made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025, for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. The deduction phases in limitations above certain taxable income thresholds (see The Numbers section).

**Built-in gains (BIG) tax:** A corporate-level tax that can apply when a C corporation converts to an S corporation and then sells appreciated assets within a specified recognition period. This tax generally does not apply to an LLC that is making its first S-corp election with no prior C-corp history.

**2% shareholder:** For S-corp fringe benefit purposes, any person who owns more than 2% of the corporation's outstanding stock. Most S-corp owner-employees qualify. The 2% threshold affects how health insurance, HRAs, and certain other benefits are taxed.

**Accumulated adjustments account (AAA):** An account tracked on the S-corp's books that records the cumulative taxable income and deductions since S-corp status began. AAA determines whether distributions to shareholders are tax-free or taxable; distributions up to the AAA balance are generally not taxed again at the shareholder level.

**EIN (Employer Identification Number):** A nine-digit number the IRS uses to identify a business for tax purposes. An existing LLC electing S-corp treatment keeps its current EIN; no new EIN is required for a tax classification change.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before starting:

1. **Current entity type and tax classification:** Is it a single-member LLC (taxed as sole proprietor) or a multi-member LLC (taxed as a partnership)? Confirm how it currently files.
2. **Net business profit for the past two to three years:** Pull your Schedule C or partnership K-1 amounts. You need these to project S-corp savings and set a salary.
3. **Your primary role in the business:** Do you provide services that directly generate revenue? How many hours per week? The IRS ties reasonable compensation to the services you personally perform.
4. **Market salary for your role:** Research what someone in your geographic area with your skills and experience would be paid on the open market in an arm's-length employment relationship. Sites such as Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS.gov) and industry salary surveys provide benchmarks.
5. **State of formation and operating states:** Identify every state where the LLC does business or has employees. Some states require a separate state-level S-corp election or do not recognize the federal S-corp election.
6. **Fiscal year:** Most small businesses use the calendar year (January 1 to December 31). Confirm before filing.
7. **All current LLC members and ownership percentages:** Every member must consent to the S-corp election on Form 2553.
8. **Whether the LLC has ever been taxed as a C corporation:** If yes, discuss built-in gains exposure with your advisor before electing.
9. **Current annual compliance costs:** What do you currently spend on bookkeeping and tax prep? Add the estimated new costs of payroll processing and Form 1120-S preparation to project the net benefit.
10. **Target effective date of election:** The election can be retroactive to the beginning of the current tax year if filed on time. Confirm the date you want S-corp treatment to begin.

## Required Documents And Records

**Documents to gather before filing:**

- Current LLC operating agreement (to verify membership percentages)
- Current EIN confirmation letter (IRS Form CP-575 or 147C) -- the same EIN is used after the election
- State formation documents (articles of organization)
- Prior-year federal tax returns (Schedule C or Form 1065) showing net income history
- Social Security numbers or EINs of all members who must sign Form 2553
- State S-corp registration requirements for your state (varies by state)

**Documents to create at election:**

- Signed Form 2553 (keep a date-stamped copy)
- IRS acceptance letter confirming the S-corp election (file this permanently)
- Corporate resolution or member consent authorizing the S-corp election and establishing payroll
- Written reasonable compensation analysis documenting how the salary was determined and the market data used (keep this in the corporate records)

**Ongoing records to maintain (minimum retention: 7 years):**

- All payroll records: pay stubs, payroll register, payroll tax deposits, and remittance confirmations
- Quarterly Forms 941 filed and the corresponding tax payment receipts
- Annual Form 940 and W-2 forms for all employees
- Form 1120-S and all Schedule K-1s for each year
- Minutes of any annual meetings or member resolutions
- Records of all distributions made to shareholders: amount, date, and allocation
- Bank statements showing salary payments and distributions as separate line items
- Documentation of health insurance premiums paid for 2% shareholders and how they were reported on W-2s
- Accumulated adjustments account (AAA) tracking schedule maintained by your accountant

## Step-By-Step Implementation

**Phase 1: Confirm eligibility and analyze the numbers**

1. Confirm eligibility. Verify your LLC meets all S-corp requirements: 100 or fewer shareholders, all shareholders are U.S. individuals (or qualifying trusts or estates), and only one class of economic interest. Multi-member LLCs with non-U.S. members or corporate members do not qualify. If you are unsure, stop here and consult your advisor.

2. Project the annual tax savings. Take your expected annual net profit. Subtract a reasonable compensation amount (see Step 3). The remaining "distribution" amount is what avoids FICA. Multiply that distribution amount by 15.3% to estimate the gross FICA savings. Then subtract the new annual compliance costs (payroll service: roughly $500 to $1,500 per year; Form 1120-S tax preparation: roughly $1,500 to $3,000 per year; state fees: varies). If the net savings are positive and you expect this income level to continue, the election makes sense.

   *Example (for illustration only -- your numbers will differ):* Net profit $150,000. Reasonable salary $75,000. Distribution $75,000. Gross FICA savings: $75,000 x 15.3% = $11,475. Subtract $4,000 in compliance costs. Net savings: approximately $7,475 per year.

3. Determine your reasonable compensation. Research what the open market pays for someone with your qualifications doing your specific work in your location. Document the sources (BLS.gov, trade association surveys, job postings). The IRS examines the following factors: your training and experience, the duties and responsibilities you perform for the S-corp, time devoted to the business, your bonus history, comparable business compensation, and how much the S-corp's gross receipts depend on your personal services versus other employees or capital. Set your salary at or above the figure you can defend with this documentation. Do not attempt to minimize the salary to a token amount; this is the primary audit trigger.

4. Check your state rules. Research whether your state requires a separate state S-corp election form, imposes a franchise tax or minimum fee on S-corps, or recognizes the federal S-corp election automatically. Your advisor can confirm these state-specific requirements.

**Phase 2: File the election**

5. Obtain and complete Form 2553. Download the current version from IRS.gov/forms. Complete all parts:
   - Part I: Entity name, EIN, address, tax year (enter "calendar year" for most businesses), the date you want the election to take effect, and the name and title of a corporate officer to contact.
   - The elected tax year section: confirm you are adopting the calendar year (January 1 to December 31) unless you have a specific business-cycle reason for a fiscal year and have confirmed the rules with your advisor.
   - Column K (shareholder consent): Every LLC member must provide their name, address, Social Security number, ownership percentage, and signature with the date acquired. If you are a single-member LLC, only you sign.

6. File Form 2553 by the deadline. For S-corp status to take effect for the 2026 tax year:
   - File by **Monday, March 16, 2026**. The general deadline is 2 months and 15 days after the start of the tax year (March 15 for a calendar year beginning January 1, 2026). Because March 15, 2026 falls on a Sunday, the deadline shifts to the next business day: Monday, March 16, 2026.
   - Alternatively, the election may be filed at any point during the preceding tax year (2025) to take effect January 1, 2026.
   - Mail or fax Form 2553 to the correct IRS Service Center for your state (see The Numbers section for addresses and fax numbers).
   - Keep a date-stamped copy and use certified mail or a confirmed fax transmission sheet as proof of timely filing.

7. Wait for IRS acceptance. The IRS issues a written acceptance letter for Form 2553. This typically takes four to eight weeks but can be longer. Do not proceed with payroll setup until you have verified the election was received (you can call the IRS Business Tax Line at 800-829-4933 to confirm). Keep the acceptance letter permanently.

8. File the state-level election if required. Some states (such as New York and New Jersey) require a separate state election form to be filed within a specific deadline. Confirm with your advisor or your state's Department of Revenue whether a separate filing is needed.

**Phase 3: Set up payroll**

9. Enroll in EFTPS. Go to EFTPS.gov and register your business to make federal payroll tax deposits electronically. Enrollment takes five to seven business days to receive a PIN by mail. This must be done before you run the first payroll.

10. Set up a payroll system. Choose a payroll provider (examples include Gusto, QuickBooks Payroll, ADP, Paychex, or a payroll module from your bookkeeping software). The payroll system will:
    - Calculate federal and state income tax withholding based on the W-4 you file as an employee of your own S-corp
    - Calculate FICA withholding (employee share: 6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare, up to the wage base)
    - Calculate the employer's matching FICA contribution (another 6.2% + 1.45%)
    - Generate pay stubs and direct deposit payments
    - File Form 941 quarterly with the IRS
    - Generate your W-2 by January 31 of the following year

11. Set your pay schedule. Run payroll for yourself on a regular, predictable schedule (weekly, biweekly, or monthly). Irregular or infrequent payroll is a red flag. Pay yourself using the reasonable compensation salary you documented in Step 3.

12. Open a separate corporate bank account if you have not already. S-corp owners should keep business income and expenses clearly separated from personal funds. Salary payments should run through payroll and show on your bank statement as payroll disbursements, distinct from profit distributions.

**Phase 4: Make profit distributions**

13. After paying your salary through payroll, additional profits may be distributed to shareholders as profit distributions. There is no required timing for distributions, but they must be: (a) made proportionally to ownership (in a single-class S-corp, all shares must receive the same distribution per share), (b) documented in writing (a distribution resolution or minutes), and (c) never used to substitute for compensation. Record every distribution as a separate transaction in your accounting system.

**Phase 5: File S-corp tax returns**

14. File Form 1120-S each year by March 15 (or the next business day if March 15 falls on a weekend or holiday). The S-corp does not pay income tax; the 1120-S is an information return showing income, deductions, and the allocation of each shareholder's share. Attach a Schedule K-1 for each shareholder.

15. Include your K-1 income on your personal Form 1040. Your share of S-corp income (from Schedule K-1) is reported on your personal return and is subject to federal income tax but NOT self-employment tax.

16. File Form 941 quarterly and Form 940 annually. See the Timing and Deadlines section for due dates.

17. Issue W-2 forms to all employees (including yourself) by January 31.

**Phase 6: Annual review**

18. Review your reasonable compensation annually. As your revenue and role change, the salary must be updated. A compensation level that was reasonable at $80,000 in revenue may be unreasonably low at $500,000 in revenue. Update your salary analysis documentation each year and adjust payroll accordingly.

## Timing And Deadlines

**Election deadline for 2026 tax year effect:**
- File Form 2553 by **Monday, March 16, 2026** to have the election effective January 1, 2026. The statutory deadline is 2 months and 15 days after the start of the tax year (March 15); because March 15, 2026 falls on a Sunday, the deadline shifts to the next business day, Monday, March 16, 2026, or
- File Form 2553 any time during 2025 (the preceding tax year).

**Late election relief:**
If you miss the deadline, you can request relief under Revenue Procedure 2013-30 by filing Form 2553 within **3 years and 75 days** of the intended effective date. You must attach a statement showing reasonable cause for the late filing and evidence that the entity and all shareholders have reported income consistent with S-corp status for all affected years.

**Annual S-corp return (Form 1120-S):**
Due the 15th day of the 3rd month after the tax year ends. For calendar-year S-corps: **March 15** each year (March 16, 2026 for the 2025 return because March 15, 2026 falls on a Sunday). An automatic 6-month extension is available by filing Form 7004 by the original due date.

**Quarterly payroll returns (Form 941):**
- Q1 (Jan-Mar): due **April 30**
- Q2 (Apr-Jun): due **July 31**
- Q3 (Jul-Sep): due **October 31**
- Q4 (Oct-Dec): due **January 31** of the following year

**Payroll tax deposits:**
New employers are monthly depositors: deposit the prior month's payroll taxes by the **15th of the following month** using EFTPS. Once your lookback period shows more than $50,000 in annual employment taxes, you become a semi-weekly depositor. If any payroll tax liability reaches $100,000 on a single day, you must deposit by the next business day.

**Annual unemployment return (Form 940):**
Due **January 31** each year.

**Employee W-2 forms:**
Must be distributed to employees (including the owner-employee) and filed with the Social Security Administration by **January 31** each year.

**Annual compensation review:**
Review and document reasonable compensation at the start of each calendar year, or whenever revenue changes materially.

**Minimum break-even profit level for S-corp election:**
Approximately $40,000 to $70,000 in consistent annual net profit, depending on state costs and local compliance fees. [Confirm with your advisor -- the break-even point depends on your specific state fees, payroll service costs, and accountant fees, which vary significantly.]

**Built-in gains recognition period:**
5 years (applicable recognition period under IRC Section 1374). If an S-corp sells an asset that was appreciated when the entity converted from C-corp status, the built-in gains tax applies during this 5-year window. This generally does not apply to an LLC making its first S-corp election with no prior C-corp history.
Source: IRS Instructions for Schedule D (Form 1120-S) (2025); 26 CFR 1.1374-4

**2% shareholder health insurance:**
Health and accident insurance premiums paid by the S-corp for a shareholder owning more than 2% of stock must be included in that shareholder's W-2 Box 1 as taxable income for federal income tax withholding purposes, but are NOT subject to Social Security, Medicare, or FUTA taxes when provided under a plan covering a class of employees. The shareholder may deduct the premiums on their personal return under IRC Section 162(l) (the self-employed health insurance deduction), provided the S-corp has established the plan and properly reported the premiums on the W-2.
Source: IRS.gov, S Corporation Compensation and Medical Insurance Issues

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Single-member LLC (taxed as disregarded entity):**
A single-member LLC is treated by the IRS as if it does not exist (a "disregarded entity"); all profit currently flows to your Schedule C. After the S-corp election, the LLC is now treated as a separate taxpayer for federal purposes. This means you must run payroll through the LLC, file Form 1120-S, and issue yourself a W-2. Your Schedule C is replaced by Schedule K-1 on your personal return.

**Multi-member LLC (taxed as a partnership):**
A multi-member LLC currently files Form 1065 and issues K-1s to all partners. After the S-corp election, it files Form 1120-S instead, and the reasonable compensation requirement applies to each working member. Each working member must be paid a salary commensurate with their contribution before any profit distributions are made. All members (even passive ones) must consent to the election on Form 2553.

**Mid-year elections:**
Filing Form 2553 after March 16, 2026 (or after March 15 in non-weekend years) will normally make the election effective January 1 of the following tax year. If you file after March 16, 2026, the election is typically effective January 1, 2027. Plan accordingly. Late election relief exists but adds complexity.

**Payroll setup timing:**
Once the election is effective, you must run payroll from the beginning of the first S-corp year. If your election is retroactive to January 1, 2026, and you do not set up payroll until April, you will need to run catch-up payroll and may owe payroll tax penalties. Set up payroll before or immediately after filing Form 2553.

**State conformity:**
Most states automatically recognize the federal S-corp election and do not tax the S-corp at the entity level (except for minimum franchise taxes or fees). Exceptions include:
- **California:** Recognizes the S-corp election but imposes a franchise tax equal to the greater of 1.5% of California net income or the $800 annual minimum franchise tax (whichever is larger -- not both added together). A separate California Form 3560 may be required.
- **New York and New Jersey:** Require a separate state S-corp election form within a specific time period. The federal election alone is not sufficient.
- **Texas:** Has a franchise ("margin") tax that may apply to S-corps. Consult your advisor.
- **Several other states** impose S-corp-specific fees or recognition requirements. Confirm your state's rules with your advisor before assuming the federal election is sufficient.

**Pass-through entity (PTE) taxes:**
More than 35 states now offer an elective pass-through entity tax that allows S-corps to pay state income tax at the entity level, generating a federal deduction that effectively circumvents the $10,000 individual SALT deduction cap. If you are in one of these states, ask your advisor whether electing the PTE tax on top of the S-corp election makes sense for your situation.

**QBI deduction and the salary trade-off:**
The 20% QBI deduction applies to your S-corp distribution income (the pass-through profit), not your salary. Paying yourself a higher salary to satisfy the reasonable compensation standard reduces the amount subject to the QBI deduction. These two tax benefits work in partial tension; your advisor should model the combined effect at your income level.

**Consistency requirement:**
Once you elect S-corp status, you must continue as an S-corp unless you revoke the election (which requires consent of shareholders holding more than 50% of stock) or you inadvertently violate the eligibility rules (which terminates the election automatically). Re-electing S-corp status after a voluntary revocation is generally restricted for five tax years.

**Self-employment tax deduction loss:**
Sole proprietors currently deduct half of their self-employment tax as an above-the-line deduction on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 15 (Schedule SE computes the tax amount; the deduction is claimed on Schedule 1). S-corp owner-employees lose this deduction to the extent income is taken as a distribution instead of salary. The employer half of FICA on your salary remains deductible by the S-corp, which reduces your taxable pass-through income.

**Medicare surtax (Net Investment Income Tax):**
S-corp distributions are not subject to self-employment tax, but if your modified adjusted gross income exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), S-corp distributions may be subject to the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) if you are not a material participant in the business. Most active S-corp owner-operators are material participants and are not subject to NIIT on their distributions, but passive investors in an S-corp may be. Confirm with your advisor.

## Common Mistakes

**Setting an unreasonably low salary.** This is the single most common and most costly mistake. IRS auditors and Tax Court judges have consistently recharacterized token salaries (such as $1 per year or a fraction of what the market would pay) as wages, assessing back payroll taxes, interest, and penalties. Your salary documentation must be defensible.

**Not setting up payroll at all.** Some business owners make the S-corp election and then simply take distributions without running payroll. The IRS treats all distributions as wages when no salary is paid, defeating the purpose of the election and triggering penalties.

**Running irregular or one-time annual payroll.** A pattern of paying yourself once per year (often in December) when year-end profits are known is a red flag for the IRS. Run payroll on a consistent, regular schedule.

**Missing the election deadline.** Filing Form 2553 after the deadline (March 16, 2026 for the 2026 tax year) results in the election taking effect the following year. Lost tax savings for the missed year are not recoverable.

**Forgetting to file a state-level S-corp election.** In states like New York and New Jersey, the federal Form 2553 election is not sufficient. A state election must be filed separately and often has a shorter or different deadline.

**Treating distributions and payroll as interchangeable.** Distributions and payroll are two legally distinct payment types with different tax treatment. All salary must flow through payroll (with W-2, withholding, FICA, and EFTPS deposits). Never pay yourself informally and call it payroll later.

**Failing to document reasonable compensation.** Even if the salary amount is defensible, failing to document how it was determined is a gap that creates risk. Keep a dated memo in your corporate records each year showing your analysis and the market data you used.

**Commingling accounts.** Depositing S-corp revenue into a personal bank account and pulling out "distributions" informally blurs the entity's books and undermines the legitimacy of the payroll structure. Use a separate business bank account.

**Ignoring the QBI W-2 wage component.** Above the QBI income thresholds, the deduction is limited by the W-2 wages paid by the business. Paying yourself a salary actually helps satisfy the W-2 wage test, meaning higher reasonable compensation can support a larger QBI deduction at high income levels. This is counterintuitive and worth modeling with your advisor.

**Not reviewing compensation annually.** A salary set in year one may become unreasonably low as revenue grows. The IRS can look back at prior years; update your compensation analysis each year.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before acting if any of the following apply:

1. **Your LLC has non-U.S. members, corporate members, or more than 100 members.** The S-corp election will be invalid. Do not file Form 2553 until your advisor confirms all members qualify as eligible shareholders.

2. **Your business previously operated as or inside a C corporation.** Built-in gains and other transition issues require analysis before the S-corp election is made.

3. **You want the election effective for a year that has already begun and you are past March 16, 2026 (or past March 15 in future calendar years when March 15 is a weekday).** Retroactive elections require late election relief under Rev. Proc. 2013-30. Your advisor needs to prepare the reasonable cause statement.

4. **You operate in California, New York, New Jersey, Texas, or any other state where you are uncertain about state recognition or franchise taxes.** State requirements and costs must be modeled before the election.

5. **Your annual net profit is below $60,000 or fluctuates significantly.** The compliance costs may not be justified; confirm the numbers with your advisor.

6. **You are considering electing AND your state offers a pass-through entity (PTE) tax election.** Layering these two elections requires careful coordination; consult your advisor before filing either.

7. **You are planning to sell the business or bring in outside investors within the next two to three years.** S-corp status can complicate a sale or an investor round. Review the entity strategy with your advisor before locking in the election.

8. **You have a pending or existing IRS audit.** Filing a new election during an active audit should be reviewed with your advisor and potentially a tax attorney.

9. **You have not yet determined your reasonable compensation amount and documented the basis for it.** Do not begin operating as an S-corp and taking distributions until you have a documented, defensible salary in place.
