# IRS Notice Response Packet

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

When the IRS sends you a notice or letter, you have a limited window to respond -- and ignoring it almost always makes the situation worse. This playbook walks you through how to read any IRS notice, identify what type it is, gather the right documents, write a compliant response, and submit it correctly. It also explains when a notice can be resolved with a simple reply versus when you must stop and involve your advisor before taking any action. Business owners who respond accurately, completely, and on time protect their appeal rights and avoid automatic assessments, levies, and additional penalties.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook applies to you if:

- You received any letter or notice from the IRS (identified by a "CP" number or "Letter" number in the upper-right corner of the document).
- You received a notice proposing changes to your tax return based on a mismatch with information the IRS received from a third party (a bank, employer, or payment processor).
- You received a balance-due notice and want to understand your options before paying, disputing, or requesting a payment plan.
- You received an audit notice (also called an examination notice) by mail asking for documentation.
- You received a Notice of Intent to Levy or a Final Notice warning that the IRS plans to seize property or wages.
- You want to build a system for responding to any IRS notice quickly and without panic.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- You have already received a Notice of Deficiency (also called the "90-day letter," IRS Letter 3219, or CP3219A). If you are at that stage, the deadline to petition the U.S. Tax Court is statutory and cannot be extended. Stop and contact your advisor immediately.
- You have received an LT11 or Letter 1058 (Final Notice of Intent to Levy with Collection Due Process rights). You have 30 days from the notice date to request a CDP hearing; once that window closes, critical rights are lost. This is an advisor-required situation.
- Your notice involves a criminal investigation or a fraud allegation of any kind. Nothing in this playbook substitutes for immediate representation by a qualified tax attorney in that situation.
- You have already missed the response deadline shown on your notice and the IRS has assessed additional tax, filed a lien, or begun levying assets. At that stage, audit reconsideration, an installment agreement, or collection appeals may apply -- all of which require advisor guidance.
- Your notice is from a state Department of Revenue (state tax agency), not the IRS. State notices have separate deadlines and procedures. Confirm the issuing agency before following this playbook.

## Key Terms

**CP Notice:** A notice issued by the IRS's automated systems. "CP" stands for Computer Paragraph. Each CP number corresponds to a specific issue (for example, CP2000 means a proposed income mismatch; CP504 means notice of intent to levy a state tax refund). The notice number appears in the upper-right corner of every IRS letter.

**LTR or Letter Notice:** A notice issued by a specific IRS function (such as an examiner or a revenue officer) rather than by the automated system. Examples include Letter 525 (30-day audit report), Letter 3219 (Notice of Deficiency), and Letter 1058 (Final Notice of Intent to Levy).

**CP2000 (Automated Underreporter Notice):** A notice proposing changes to your return because income or payment information the IRS received from a third party (such as a bank, employer, or payment processor) does not match what you reported. The CP2000 is not a bill and is not a full audit -- it is a proposal. You must respond by the date shown on the notice, even if you agree with the proposed changes.

**CP2501 (Income/Payments Mismatch Notice):** Similar to the CP2000 but issued earlier in the process, before a formal tax assessment is proposed. It asks you to explain the discrepancy. You must reply by the date listed on the notice.

**Correspondence Audit (Audit by Mail):** An IRS examination of your tax return conducted entirely by mail. The IRS sends a letter (often starting with Letter 566, Letter 2205, or a CP notice) identifying specific line items or deductions it wants you to support with documentation. This is the most common type of audit.

**30-Day Letter (Letter 525 or Letter 915):** A letter sent after the IRS completes a correspondence audit. It encloses Form 4549 (the proposed adjustment report) and gives you 30 days to agree, provide more documentation, or request an appeal to the IRS Office of Appeals.

**Form 4549 (Income Tax Examination Changes):** The IRS form enclosed with a 30-day letter that lists each proposed adjustment, the reasons for it, and the resulting tax, penalty, and interest changes.

**Notice of Deficiency (90-Day Letter, CP3219A, or Letter 3219):** A formal legal notice proposing additional tax. From the date on this letter, you have 90 days (150 days if your address is outside the United States) to file a petition with the U.S. Tax Court. This deadline is set by statute and cannot be extended by any party, including the IRS.

**CP501, CP503, CP504 (Balance-Due Notices):** A series of escalating notices the IRS sends when you have an unpaid tax balance. CP501 is the first reminder. CP503 is a second notice. CP504 is a Notice of Intent to Levy, specifically telling you the IRS may seize your state tax refund if you do not pay or contact the IRS within 30 days of the notice date.

**LT11 / Letter 1058 (Final Notice of Intent to Levy with CDP Rights):** A notice that gives the IRS legal authority to levy (seize) wages, bank accounts, and other property. This notice also triggers your Collection Due Process (CDP) rights -- the right to request a formal hearing before Appeals. You have exactly 30 days from the notice date to file Form 12153 and request a CDP hearing. After 30 days, the levy right is preserved and your opportunity for Tax Court review is gone.

**Levy:** The IRS's legal authority to seize property to satisfy a tax debt. A levy can attach to wages (the employer must send a portion of each paycheck to the IRS), bank accounts (funds are frozen and sent to the IRS after 21 days), state tax refunds, accounts receivable, and other assets. A levy is different from a lien.

**Notice of Federal Tax Lien (NFTL):** A public notice filed by the IRS with a local government authority (such as a county recorder) that establishes the IRS's legal claim against your property as security for a tax debt. A lien does not immediately seize property, but it attaches to all current and future property you own and damages your ability to sell assets or obtain credit.

**Collection Due Process (CDP) Hearing:** A formal administrative hearing before the IRS Independent Office of Appeals, available after you receive a CDP notice (LT11, Letter 1058, or a lien notice). You request it using Form 12153. A timely CDP request suspends IRS levy action during the hearing process and preserves your right to petition the U.S. Tax Court if you disagree with the Appeals outcome.

**Equivalent Hearing:** An Appeals hearing available when you miss the 30-day CDP deadline but file within one year of the notice date. An Equivalent Hearing suspends levy action but does not preserve your right to petition Tax Court.

**Form 12153 (Request for Collection Due Process or Equivalent Hearing):** The form used to request a CDP hearing or an Equivalent Hearing. Mail it to the address on your CDP notice within 30 days (for CDP) or within one year (for an Equivalent Hearing).

**IRS Independent Office of Appeals:** An independent function within the IRS that offers taxpayers a conference to dispute proposed tax changes or collection actions before going to court. Appeals officers are required to be impartial. Most cases that reach Appeals are settled without litigation.

**Form 2848 (Power of Attorney and Declaration of Representative):** The IRS form that authorizes a representative (such as an attorney, CPA, or enrolled agent) to act on your behalf before the IRS. Once on file, the IRS must communicate with your representative rather than (or in addition to) you.

**Enrolled Agent (EA):** A tax professional licensed by the IRS who has passed a comprehensive IRS examination or worked for the IRS for at least five years. An EA is authorized to represent taxpayers in all matters before the IRS.

**Form 9465 (Installment Agreement Request):** The form used to request a monthly payment plan with the IRS when you cannot pay your tax balance in full. You can also apply online at IRS.gov/OPA.

**Installment Agreement:** A formal payment plan between you and the IRS that lets you pay an unpaid tax balance in monthly installments rather than all at once. The IRS charges a setup fee and interest continues to accrue on the unpaid balance.

**Form 843 (Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement):** The form used to request a refund of taxes, penalties, or interest that have already been assessed and paid, or to request abatement (cancellation) of penalties after the fact.

**First Time Abatement (FTA):** An administrative IRS waiver that removes failure-to-file, failure-to-pay, or failure-to-deposit penalties for taxpayers who have a clean compliance history for the prior three tax years (no penalties during those years). FTA does not apply to accuracy-related penalties. You do not need to mention "First Time Abatement" specifically -- the IRS reviews your account and applies it automatically if you qualify.

**Reasonable Cause Penalty Relief:** A basis for abating (canceling) penalties when you can show you exercised ordinary business care and prudence but still could not comply with the tax law. Documentation of the reason (illness, natural disaster, reliance on professional advice, etc.) is required.

**Statutory Notice of Deficiency:** See "Notice of Deficiency" above. "Statutory" means the IRS is required by law to issue this notice before assessing additional tax, and the notice triggers your right to petition Tax Court.

**IRS Document Upload Tool (DUT):** The IRS's secure online portal for submitting audit response documents electronically. You access it using a link or QR code printed on your specific notice. You can upload JPG, PNG, or PDF files (up to 15 MB per file, up to 40 files per submission). The DUT is only for responding to notices; do not use it to file original tax returns.

**Taxpayer Advocate Service (TAS):** An independent organization within the IRS that helps taxpayers resolve problems when normal IRS channels have failed or when you are experiencing significant financial hardship as a result of IRS action. TAS assistance is free.

**CP3219A (Statutory Notice of Deficiency for Automated Underreporter Cases):** The escalation notice sent when you do not respond to a CP2000. It is legally identical to the Notice of Deficiency described above -- the 90-day Tax Court petition clock starts from the date on this notice.

**Form 433-A / Form 433-B (Collection Information Statements):** Financial disclosure forms the IRS uses to evaluate your ability to pay. Form 433-A covers individuals and self-employed taxpayers; Form 433-B covers businesses. These are required if you request collection alternatives during a CDP hearing or if you apply for hardship-based payment arrangements.

**Offer in Compromise (OIC):** An IRS program that allows eligible taxpayers to settle their tax debt for less than the full amount owed, based on their ability to pay, income, expenses, and asset equity. OIC applications require Form 656 and Form 433-A or 433-B. An accepted OIC is legally binding. This playbook does not cover the full OIC process -- discuss eligibility with your advisor.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before you begin building your response:

1. **The original IRS notice or letter.** Locate the CP number or LTR number in the upper-right corner, the notice date (the clock for deadlines runs from this date, not the date you received it), the tax year or period under examination, the response deadline, and the fax number or mailing address for submitting your response.
2. **The specific items the IRS is questioning.** The notice will identify which income items, deductions, or credits it is questioning, and often provides a comparison table showing what you reported versus what the IRS has on file from third-party information returns.
3. **Your original tax return (Form 1040, 1120-S, 1065, etc.) for the year at issue.** You need this to compare your reported figures against the IRS's figures.
4. **All third-party information returns for the year at issue.** This means all Forms W-2, 1099-NEC, 1099-MISC, 1099-K, 1099-INT, 1099-DIV, 1099-B, and 1099-R that you received for that tax year. These are the source documents the IRS used to generate most CP2000 and CP2501 notices.
5. **Supporting documentation for any disputed item.** Examples include bank statements, brokerage account statements, invoices, contracts, receipts, cancelled checks, and prior-year basis records.
6. **Your EIN (Employer Identification Number) or Social Security Number.** You must include one of these on every written communication with the IRS so the IRS can match your response to your account.
7. **Prior IRS correspondence on the same issue**, if any. If this is a follow-up notice, include the earlier notice so you can identify what was previously submitted.

## Required Documents And Records

### To Submit With Your Response

- A copy of the IRS notice (always include this with any mailed or faxed response).
- Copies (never originals) of W-2s, 1099s, or other information returns at issue.
- Bank statements or account statements that reconcile disputed income or expense amounts.
- Invoices, receipts, contracts, or other business records that support a deduction the IRS is questioning.
- Any amended return (Form 1040-X or other amended return form) if the facts show you need to correct a different error than the one the IRS proposed.
- A signed, written statement if you disagree with any portion of the proposed changes. The statement must include your name, taxpayer identification number, the notice number and date, and a clear explanation of each item you dispute.
- Form 2848 if you are authorizing a representative to communicate with the IRS on your behalf.

### To Keep In Your Own Files After Responding (Retain for at Least 3 Years From the Date of Resolution)

- A complete copy of every document you submitted.
- Proof of submission: certified mail receipt with tracking number, fax confirmation page, or IRS Document Upload Tool confirmation screen (print or save as PDF).
- A communication log recording every phone call, mail submission, fax, or upload: date, method, the name and employee ID number of any IRS employee you spoke with, and a summary of what was discussed.
- The IRS's final determination letter once the matter is resolved.
- Any Form 4549 (proposed adjustment), any 30-day letter, and any Notice of Deficiency you receive during the process.

### Retention Guidance

Keep all records related to an IRS notice until the matter is fully closed and then for the full statute of limitations period for the tax year at issue -- at minimum 3 years from the filing date of that return, or 6 years if there was a substantial understatement of income (more than 25% of gross income unreported). If the notice involves employment taxes, retain records for at least 4 years after the tax became due or was paid, whichever is later.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Triage the Notice (Do This the Day You Receive It)

**Step 1: Read the entire notice from beginning to end.**
Do not skip or skim. Note the notice number (CP or LTR), the notice date, the tax year at issue, the response deadline, and the specific items the IRS is questioning. If the notice includes a response form, read that too.

**Step 2: Identify which category your notice falls into.**
Use this table to classify the notice and determine your immediate next step:

| Notice Type | What It Means | Immediate Action Required |
|---|---|---|
| CP2000 or CP2501 | IRS proposes changes based on third-party income mismatch | Respond within 30 days of notice date (60 days if outside the U.S.) |
| CP501, CP503 | Balance due reminder | Pay, set up a payment plan, or dispute by the date shown |
| CP504 | Notice of Intent to Levy (state tax refund) | Pay, arrange installment agreement, or call IRS within 30 days of notice date |
| LT11 or Letter 1058 | Final Notice of Intent to Levy with CDP rights | File Form 12153 within 30 days of notice date -- stop and call your advisor today |
| Letter 525 or Letter 915 | 30-day audit report (Form 4549 enclosed) | Agree, provide more documentation, or request Appeals conference within 30 days |
| CP3219A or Letter 3219 | Notice of Deficiency (90-day letter) | File Tax Court petition within 90 days of notice date -- stop and call your advisor today |
| Correspondence audit letter (e.g., Letter 566, CP75) | Mail audit: IRS requests documentation | Send requested documents by the deadline shown |
| Other CP or LTR notices | Varies | Read the instructions on the notice; respond by the deadline shown |

Output: You know exactly what type of notice you have, what the deadline is, and whether you need your advisor involved before taking any action.

**Step 3: Calendar the response deadline immediately.**
Add a calendar reminder for three to five days before the response deadline. The deadline runs from the date printed on the notice, not the date you received it. Mail delays are common. Do not wait until the last day.

**Step 4: Check for advisor-required situations.**
If your notice is an LT11, Letter 1058, CP3219A, Letter 3219, or any notice proposing an adjustment greater than $5,000, or if the notice covers employment taxes, stop and contact your advisor before taking any other step. See "Advisor Review Gates."

### Phase 2: Gather and Organize Your Evidence

**Step 5: Pull your original return and all third-party forms for the year at issue.**
Compare what you reported on your return to the figures the IRS shows on the notice. The IRS's comparison table in a CP2000 will show each income item side-by-side with what a third party reported. Identify every discrepancy.

**Step 6: Determine your position on each line item the IRS is questioning.**
For each item, decide one of three things:
- You agree the IRS is correct (you missed reporting that income or overclaimed a deduction).
- You disagree (you reported it correctly and can document why).
- You partially agree (some proposed changes are correct and some are not).

Write out your position clearly in plain notes at this step. This becomes the basis of your response letter.

**Step 7: Gather supporting documents for every disputed item.**
For each item you are disputing:
- Income items: locate the corresponding information return or bank records showing the income was already reported elsewhere on the return, was non-taxable, or was a duplicate.
- Expense or deduction items: locate receipts, invoices, bank statements, or other records substantiating the expense.
- Basis issues (common in 1099-B brokerage cases): locate purchase records, prior returns showing carryover basis, or account statements showing original cost.

Organize documents in the same order the IRS listed the items in the notice. Label each document with the line item it supports.

**Step 8: Prepare your response letter.**
Every written response to the IRS must include:
1. Your full legal name (and your spouse's name if filing jointly).
2. Your Social Security Number or Employer Identification Number.
3. The notice number and the notice date (found on the first page of the notice).
4. The tax year at issue.
5. A clear statement of whether you agree, partially agree, or disagree with each proposed change.
6. For each item you are disputing: a brief, factual explanation of why you disagree and what the correct amount should be.
7. A list of the documents you are enclosing.
8. Your signature (and your spouse's signature if filing jointly on a joint return).

Keep the tone neutral and factual. Do not include documents, explanations, or information about items not listed in the notice. Volunteering additional information can expand the IRS's inquiry into unrelated areas.

Output: A signed, organized response letter with all supporting documents labeled and attached.

### Phase 3: Submit Your Response

**Step 9: Make a complete copy of everything before you submit.**
Photocopy or scan every page you are sending, including the cover letter and all attachments. This copy stays in your files.

**Step 10: Choose your submission method.**
The IRS accepts responses through three channels. Use the method specified on your notice:

- **IRS Document Upload Tool (DUT):** The fastest and most traceable method. Use the QR code or the unique URL printed on your notice to access the correct portal. Enter the 10-digit access code from your notice or your notice/letter number plus your name and taxpayer ID. Upload files in JPG, PNG, or PDF format (up to 15 MB per file, up to 40 files). Print or save the confirmation screen as your proof of submission.
- **Fax:** Use the fax number printed on your notice. Always include a cover sheet labeled "Confidential" with your name, taxpayer ID, notice number, and total number of pages. Print the fax confirmation page and keep it as proof of submission.
- **U.S. Mail:** Use the return address printed on the first page of the notice. Send via certified mail with return receipt requested (USPS Form 3811 or electronic return receipt). Keep the tracking confirmation and the green return receipt card as proof of submission.

**Do not send original documents.** Always send copies.

**Step 11: Document your submission in your communication log.**
Record the date, method, and confirmation number or tracking number of your submission. If you spoke with an IRS employee at any point, record their name and employee ID number.

Output: Your response is submitted, confirmed, and documented.

### Phase 4: After You Submit -- Track the Resolution

**Step 12: Allow the IRS processing time and watch for follow-up notices.**
After submitting a response to a CP2000 or correspondence audit, the IRS typically takes several weeks to several months to process your response and issue a determination. Do not assume silence means resolution. Watch the mail.

If you receive a follow-up notice while your response is pending, do not ignore it. Contact the IRS at the number on the notice to confirm your response is in the system and to request an extension if a new deadline appears before the first issue is resolved.

**Step 13: Review the IRS determination letter when it arrives.**
The IRS will send either an acceptance letter (confirming your return as filed or accepting your corrected position) or a new proposed adjustment or 30-day letter. If the IRS accepts your response, retain the acceptance letter with your tax records.

**Step 14: If the IRS proposes additional changes in a 30-day letter (Letter 525), evaluate your options.**
You have three options after receiving a 30-day letter:
- **Agree:** Sign and return Form 4549. Pay any additional tax, penalties, and interest owed.
- **Request manager conference:** Ask for an informal conference with the examiner's manager before the response deadline on the letter.
- **Request Appeals:** Submit a written protest (or Form 12203 for disputes under $25,000) requesting a conference with the IRS Office of Appeals. Do this before the 30-day deadline.

Do not sign Form 4549 if you disagree with any proposed change. Signing is an agreement.

**Step 15: If you owe a balance and cannot pay in full, set up a payment arrangement before the deadline.**
Options (verified against IRS.gov, June 2026):
- **Short-term payment plan (online):** Available for individuals owing less than $100,000 combined (tax, penalties, and interest). No setup fee. Pay within 180 days.
- **Long-term installment agreement (online):** Available for individuals owing $50,000 or less. Setup fee: $69 (online application); $178 (by phone, mail, or in person). With Direct Debit (automatic monthly withdrawal): $22 (online); $107 (phone/mail/in person). Low-income taxpayers may have fees waived.
- **Form 9465 (Installment Agreement Request):** Use this form to apply by mail or to request a payment plan for amounts above the online threshold or for businesses.
- **Online Payment Agreement:** Apply at IRS.gov/OPA for individuals.

Interest (currently 7% per year for individuals, compounded daily, as of Q1-Q2 and Q3 2026; this rate changes quarterly) continues to accrue on the unpaid balance while you are on an installment plan.

**Step 16: If you cannot pay at all and are experiencing financial hardship, contact the Taxpayer Advocate Service.**
Call 1-877-777-4778 or submit Form 911. TAS assistance is free. TAS can help if the IRS's action is causing you an economic hardship (such as inability to pay for housing, food, utilities, or transportation) or if your issue has been pending too long without resolution.

**Step 17: If the IRS assessed tax on a closed matter and you have new information, request Audit Reconsideration.**
If you did not respond to the original notice and the IRS automatically assessed additional tax, or if you have new documentation that was not reviewed, you can write a letter to the IRS requesting audit reconsideration. Include: your name and taxpayer ID, the tax year, a copy of the original IRS notice and Form 4549 if you have them, an explanation of why you are requesting reconsideration, and copies of the supporting documents you want the IRS to review. No special form is required. The IRS typically responds within 30 days but resolution can take several months.

## Timing And Deadlines

The deadlines below are firm. Missing most of them results in loss of appeal rights, automatic assessment of tax, or the IRS proceeding with collection.

| Notice Type | Response Deadline | What Happens If You Miss It |
|---|---|---|
| CP2000 or CP2501 | 30 days from notice date (60 days if outside the U.S.) | IRS sends CP3219A (Notice of Deficiency); 90-day Tax Court window begins |
| Correspondence audit letter | Deadline shown on the letter; typically 30 days | IRS may disallow all questioned items and issue a 30-day letter or Notice of Deficiency |
| Letter 525 / 30-day letter | 30 days from the letter date | IRS issues Notice of Deficiency |
| CP3219A / Letter 3219 (Notice of Deficiency / 90-day letter) | 90 days from the notice date (150 days if outside the U.S.) | IRS assesses the tax; right to petition Tax Court without paying first is lost |
| CP501, CP503 | Date shown on notice | IRS sends next notice in escalation sequence (CP503, then CP504) |
| CP504 (Notice of Intent to Levy state refund) | 30 days from notice date | IRS may levy your state tax refund |
| LT11 / Letter 1058 (Final Notice of Intent to Levy with CDP rights) | 30 days from notice date to file Form 12153 | CDP rights expire; IRS may proceed with levy; right to Tax Court review is lost (Equivalent Hearing remains available for one year) |
| CDP Equivalent Hearing (missed 30-day CDP deadline) | Within one year of the CDP notice date | Levy action may proceed; Tax Court review is not available |
| Audit reconsideration request | No hard deadline, but file as soon as possible | Installment agreements or collection enforcement may proceed while the matter is open |
| Form 843 (refund/abatement claim for amounts already paid) | 3 years from original return filing date or 2 years from payment, whichever is later | Refund claim is time-barred |

**Recurring cadence:**
- As soon as a notice arrives: Triage it, calendar the deadline, and determine whether you need your advisor before doing anything else.
- Within 3 to 5 days of receiving the notice: Gather documents and draft your response so you are not rushing at the deadline.
- After submission: Monitor the mail for follow-up notices. Do not assume resolution until you receive a written acceptance or closing letter.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**S corporation and partnership returns:** If you receive an IRS notice related to your S corporation (Form 1120-S) or partnership (Form 1065), the notice may involve the entity itself, individual shareholders or partners, or both. Partnership audits under the Bipartisan Budget Act (BBA) of 2015 can result in adjustments assessed at the partnership level rather than flowing through to individual partners. If you receive a partnership-level notice, contact your advisor before responding -- the rules for who can represent the partnership and what elections are available are complex.

**Employment taxes:** Notices related to payroll (CP162, CP259, CP261, or notices from the IRS regarding Form 941 filings) involve separate rules. Unpaid payroll taxes can result in the Trust Fund Recovery Penalty, a personal liability assessed against responsible officers or owners for the portion of unpaid employment taxes that represent withheld employee income and FICA taxes. This is one of the most serious IRS enforcement actions available. Stop and contact your advisor immediately if you receive any notice related to employment taxes, payroll deposits, or potential Trust Fund Recovery.

**State income tax audits:** Most states send their own audit notices separately from the IRS. If the IRS adjusts your federal taxable income, you are generally required to report that change to the applicable state within a specified period (the period varies by state). Failure to self-report federal adjustments to the state can result in additional state penalties. Confirm the specific reporting requirement with your advisor if the IRS changes your federal return.

**Scam notices:** The IRS initiates all audits and most collection actions by U.S. mail. The IRS does not initiate contact by email, text message, or social media. The IRS does not demand immediate payment by phone or require payment by gift card, wire transfer, or cryptocurrency. If you receive a phone call or email claiming to be from the IRS and demanding immediate payment, it is almost certainly a scam. Verify any suspicious contact by calling the IRS directly at 1-800-829-1040. If you receive a letter and are unsure it is legitimate, search the notice number at IRS.gov to confirm it corresponds to a real IRS notice type.

**CP2000 and cryptocurrency or digital asset income:** For tax year 2026, custodial brokers and exchanges are required to report gross proceeds on Form 1099-DA. If you receive a CP2000 related to digital asset transactions, the IRS's figures may reflect gross proceeds without accounting for your cost basis. Documenting your original purchase price (cost basis) is essential to disputing the full proposed amount. Locate transaction records, wallet addresses, and exchange history to establish basis.

**1099-K income reporting:** Payment processors (including credit card companies, PayPal, Venmo, Stripe, and similar platforms) report gross payments to the IRS on Form 1099-K. If you receive a CP2000 based on a 1099-K, the IRS may be treating gross payment volume as taxable income when some of it represents business expense reimbursements, personal transactions, or returns. Document the non-taxable portion separately to reconcile the gross figure to your actual taxable income.

**TCJA provisions expiring or changed after 2025:** The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 made significant changes to business tax rules. Some provisions were set to change or expire after 2025. For example, bonus depreciation (the immediate deduction for the cost of qualifying new property placed in service) and other provisions may have been modified by legislation enacted after the August 2025 training cutoff. If a CP2000 or audit notice relates to a deduction tied to a provision that was changing, confirm with your advisor whether the deduction is still fully available for the year at issue.

## Common Mistakes

**Ignoring the notice.** Every IRS notice has a deadline. If you do not respond, the IRS will automatically disallow the disputed items, assess additional tax, and issue escalating collection notices. Ignoring a Notice of Deficiency causes you to permanently lose the right to petition Tax Court without first paying.

**Missing the deadline because you calculated it from the date you received the notice.** The deadline runs from the date printed on the notice. Mail delivery time is not added. Always use the notice date, not the envelope postmark.

**Sending original documents to the IRS.** The IRS can lose mail. Send copies only. Retain the originals in your files.

**Sending documents to the wrong address.** Each IRS notice includes a specific return address or fax number. The address depends on the IRS campus processing your case. Do not use a general IRS address. Use the address printed on your notice.

**Submitting too much information.** Responding to a narrow income-mismatch notice with a complete document dump can inadvertently raise questions about unrelated items and expand the scope of the inquiry. Respond precisely to what the notice asks about.

**Signing Form 4549 when you disagree.** Signing Form 4549 is an agreement to the proposed adjustments. If you disagree with any item, do not sign. Request an Appeals conference or provide additional documentation instead.

**Failing to request a payment plan when you cannot pay.** Receiving a balance-due notice without the ability to pay in full does not justify ignoring it. Setting up an installment agreement stops the escalation sequence (though interest and a reduced failure-to-pay penalty continue). Call the IRS or apply online before the deadline shown on the notice.

**Assuming the CP2000 reflects a bill.** A CP2000 is a proposal, not a bill. You have the right to agree, disagree, or partially disagree. Many CP2000 notices can be resolved by providing documentation that the income was already reported on a different line of your return or that the proposed income was non-taxable.

**Misidentifying the notice type.** Treating an LT11 or Letter 1058 (Final Notice of Intent to Levy) like a routine balance-due notice will cause you to miss the 30-day CDP window -- the only opportunity to stop a levy and preserve Tax Court review rights. Read the notice number carefully.

**Not keeping proof of submission.** If the IRS claims it did not receive your response, you need proof. Certified mail receipts, fax confirmations, and DUT confirmation screens are essential.

**Failing to report federal audit adjustments to the state.** Most states require you to notify them when the IRS changes your federal taxable income. Missing the state reporting deadline can result in additional state penalties even after you resolve the federal matter.

**Calling the IRS without authorizing a representative first.** If your advisor has not yet filed Form 2848, the IRS cannot discuss your account with them. File Form 2848 before your advisor makes the first call on your behalf.

## Advisor Review Gates

Pause and contact your advisor before taking any action in the following situations:

- You receive a Notice of Deficiency (CP3219A or Letter 3219, the 90-day letter). The Tax Court petition deadline is statutory and cannot be extended.
- You receive an LT11 or Letter 1058 (Final Notice of Intent to Levy with CDP rights). The 30-day CDP window closes permanently.
- The proposed adjustment or balance due exceeds $5,000.
- The notice covers more than one tax year.
- The notice relates to employment taxes, payroll tax deposits, or the potential Trust Fund Recovery Penalty.
- The notice relates to an S corporation, partnership, or multi-member LLC.
- You are considering signing Form 4549. Do not sign until your advisor reviews it.
- You are considering requesting an Appeals conference. The decision to appeal and how to frame your protest has strategic implications.
- You receive a lien notice (Notice of Federal Tax Lien). A lien affects your credit, your ability to sell property, and your business relationships.
- The notice mentions a potential fraud or criminal investigation of any kind.
- The notice is from the IRS Criminal Investigation division.
- You want to request an Offer in Compromise (OIC) or Currently Not Collectible (CNC) status instead of a standard installment agreement.
- You have already missed the response deadline and the IRS has assessed tax or begun collection action.
- You are unsure whether the notice is legitimate. Verify before responding.
- You want to authorize a representative to act on your behalf. Use Form 2848 and have your advisor assist with completion and submission.

## Before You Act

Before responding to any IRS notice, signing any IRS form, making any payment or payment arrangement, requesting Appeals, or taking any other action related to an IRS notice, confirm your specific facts with your advisor. The consequences of missing a statutory deadline (such as the 90-day Tax Court petition window or the 30-day CDP hearing window) cannot be undone. The consequences of signing an agreement with the IRS are binding.

The figures, deadlines, and procedures in this playbook are accurate as of the date of publication based on verified IRS sources. Tax law changes. IRS interest rates change quarterly. Deadlines are absolute. Do not act on any statutory deadline without first confirming the specific deadline and your options with your advisor.

If you are experiencing financial hardship as a result of IRS collection action, contact the Taxpayer Advocate Service at 1-877-777-4778 or submit Form 911. The service is free.
