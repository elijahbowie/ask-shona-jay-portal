# Year-End Strategy Review Checklist

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

A year-end tax strategy review is a structured, annual pass through your finances -- typically done in October through December -- to confirm that every legal deduction, election, and planning move is in place before the calendar year closes. Once December 31 passes, most tax-year opportunities are gone permanently. This playbook turns that review into a concrete, step-by-step checklist that a business-owner client can work through independently using the attached worksheet, then bring to their advisor with specific questions rather than a blank page. The goal is to reduce your tax liability for the current year while setting up accurate estimated payments and a clean position heading into the next year.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy applies to you if ALL of the following are true:

- You are a U.S. taxpayer -- individual, sole proprietor, S-corporation owner, partnership member, or LLC owner -- filing a U.S. federal income tax return for tax year 2026.
- You have some level of business income, self-employment income, or investment income that creates planning decisions (rather than a simple W-2-only situation with no other income or deductions to manage).
- You are reading this between approximately October 1 and December 31, 2026 -- or you are doing a retrospective review to understand what you missed and what to do differently next year.

This checklist is designed for business owners who are actively managing their tax picture, not just passively waiting for a CPA to file. You do not need to be a tax expert; this playbook defines every term it uses.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **If the year is already over:** Most year-end moves require action before December 31. If it is January or later and you are filing your return, use this playbook as a planning guide for the coming year instead, and focus your current energy on document gathering and accurate reporting.
- **If you have only W-2 income and no other deductions or entities:** A simple W-2-only situation with the standard deduction often has little year-end flexibility. You can still review withholding accuracy and IRA contributions, but many sections of this playbook will not apply.
- **C-corporation owners managing corporate-level taxes:** A C-corporation (a business that pays income tax at the corporate level, separate from its owners) has different tax-year deadlines and strategies. This checklist focuses on pass-through entities and individuals. C-corp owners should confirm the applicable deadlines with their advisor.
- **Taxpayers with highly complex situations:** If your return involves significant passive activity losses (losses from investments you do not actively manage), foreign income, multi-state operations with conflicting deadlines, or trust and estate issues, this checklist covers the foundations but is not a substitute for specialized guidance from your advisor.

## Key Terms

**Tax year:** The 12-month period for which you compute your taxes. For most individuals and pass-through businesses, the tax year is January 1 through December 31.

**Pass-through entity:** A business structure -- including sole proprietorships, partnerships, LLCs, and S-corporations -- where the business itself does not pay federal income tax. Instead, the income "passes through" to the owners' personal tax returns, and the owners pay tax on it. Contrast with a C-corporation, which pays its own income tax.

**Adjusted Gross Income (AGI):** Your total income from all sources (wages, business income, investment income, etc.) minus certain "above-the-line" deductions such as self-employed health insurance premiums, retirement plan contributions, and half of self-employment tax. AGI is a critical number because many deduction limits and phase-outs are calculated as a percentage of AGI.

**Taxable income:** AGI minus either the standard deduction (a flat amount the IRS allows everyone) or itemized deductions (a list of specific expenses you deduct individually), whichever is greater. Your tax is calculated on your taxable income.

**Standard deduction:** A fixed dollar amount that reduces your taxable income without requiring you to list individual expenses. For 2026: $16,100 for single filers, $32,200 for married filing jointly (MFJ), and $24,150 for heads of household. Source: IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32; IRS.gov 2026 inflation adjustments.

**Itemized deductions:** A list of specific allowable expenses you deduct on Schedule A instead of taking the standard deduction. Examples include mortgage interest, state and local taxes paid (subject to the SALT cap, described below), charitable contributions, and unreimbursed medical expenses above 7.5% of AGI. You choose whichever method (standard or itemized) gives you the larger deduction.

**SALT cap:** The State and Local Tax (SALT) deduction is the combined deduction for state income taxes paid and property taxes paid on Schedule A. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (enacted July 4, 2025), the SALT cap was increased from $10,000 to $40,000 for tax year 2025, indexed to $40,400 for 2026, and continuing to increase by approximately 1% per year through 2029. The increased cap is subject to a phasedown: if your modified AGI exceeds approximately $505,000 in 2026 (the indexed amount; $500,000 in 2025), your deduction is reduced by 30 cents for every dollar above that threshold, but will not fall below $10,000. The cap reverts to $10,000 for tax years beginning in 2030. The married-filing-separately cap is one-half the applicable limit. Source: IRS.gov One Big Beautiful Bill Provisions; IRS Understanding the One Big Beautiful Bill -- Individual Tax Provisions (YouTube transcript).

**Self-employment (SE) tax:** The equivalent of payroll taxes (Social Security and Medicare) paid by self-employed individuals. The rate is 15.3% on net self-employment income up to $184,500 (the Social Security wage base for 2026), then 2.9% on amounts above $184,500. You may deduct half of the SE tax you pay as an above-the-line deduction on your personal return. Source: IRS Topic 751; SSA.gov Contribution and Benefit Base.

**QBI deduction (Section 199A):** The Qualified Business Income deduction allows eligible owners of pass-through businesses to deduct up to 20% of their qualified business income from their taxable income. This deduction is available whether you itemize or take the standard deduction. The deduction is permanent as of 2026 under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. For 2026, the income threshold above which limitations begin is $201,750 for single filers and $403,500 for married filing jointly; the deduction phases out completely at $276,750 (single) and $553,500 (MFJ). Specified Service Trades or Businesses (SSTBs) -- which include fields like law, health, consulting, financial services, and performing arts -- lose the deduction entirely once income is above the upper phase-out limit. Source: Rev. Proc. 2025-32 (IRB 2025-45); IRS.gov 2026 inflation adjustments.

**SSTB (Specified Service Trade or Business):** A business in a field where the principal asset is the reputation or skill of its employees or owners. IRS-listed categories include health, law, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services, and brokerage services. SSTB owners face stricter QBI deduction limits at higher income levels.

**Estimated tax payments:** Quarterly prepayments of federal (and often state) income tax required of taxpayers who expect to owe at least $1,000 in tax after subtracting withholding and credits. Business owners and self-employed individuals typically must make these. The 2026 due dates are: April 15, 2026 (Q1); June 15, 2026 (Q2); September 15, 2026 (Q3); and January 15, 2027 (Q4). Underpayment can trigger a penalty. Source: IRS Publication 505 (2026); IRS.gov estimated tax FAQ.

**Safe harbor (estimated tax):** A rule that protects you from the underpayment penalty even if your actual tax bill is higher than expected. The two most common safe harbors: (1) pay at least 100% of last year's tax liability (110% if your prior-year AGI exceeded $150,000); or (2) pay at least 90% of your current-year tax liability. Satisfying either eliminates the penalty. Source: IRS Publication 505 (2026).

**Retirement plan contribution deadline:** The date by which you must contribute money to a retirement account for it to count as a deduction in the current tax year. For 401(k) elective deferrals, this is December 31 of the tax year. For SEP-IRAs and traditional IRAs, contributions can be made up to the tax-filing deadline (including extensions). For solo 401(k) plans, the plan itself must be established by December 31 of the tax year you want to begin contributing; contributions can then be made through the filing deadline.

**Realized gain or loss:** A gain or loss that has actually been "locked in" by selling an asset. Until you sell, the gain or loss is "unrealized" and does not affect your taxes. Year-end planning focuses heavily on whether to realize gains or losses before December 31.

**Tax-loss harvesting:** The practice of selling investments that have declined in value before year-end to generate a capital loss. Capital losses offset capital gains dollar-for-dollar; if losses exceed gains, up to $3,000 of net capital loss can offset ordinary income each year (any excess carries forward to future years). Source: IRC Section 1211.

**HSA (Health Savings Account):** A tax-advantaged savings account available to individuals enrolled in a High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP). Contributions are deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free -- a triple tax benefit. For 2026, the contribution limit is $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage. Individuals age 55 or older can contribute an additional $1,000 as a catch-up contribution. Source: IRS Publication 969 (2026); Rev. Proc. 2025-19.

**HDHP (High-Deductible Health Plan):** A health insurance plan with a higher annual deductible than traditional plans, required to be eligible to contribute to an HSA. For 2026, an HDHP must have a minimum annual deductible of $1,700 for self-only coverage and $3,400 for family coverage. Source: IRS Publication 969 (2026); Rev. Proc. 2025-19 (IRC sec. 223).

**Health FSA (Flexible Spending Account):** An employer-sponsored account that lets you set aside pre-tax dollars for eligible medical expenses. Unlike an HSA, FSA funds are generally "use it or lose it" within the plan year (with limited carryover). For 2026, the maximum contribution is $3,400 per year. The maximum carryover to the following plan year is $680. Source: IRS.gov 2026 inflation adjustments.

**Section 179 expensing:** A tax provision (Internal Revenue Code Section 179) that allows businesses to deduct the full purchase price of qualifying equipment and property in the year placed in service, rather than depreciating it over several years. For 2026, the maximum Section 179 deduction is $2,560,000, reduced dollar-for-dollar when total qualifying property placed in service exceeds $4,090,000. Source: IRS.gov 2026 Section 179 limits (Rev. Proc. 2025-32).

**Bonus depreciation:** An additional first-year depreciation deduction for qualified property. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, 100% bonus depreciation was reinstated for qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. This means you may be able to deduct the full cost of certain equipment in the year purchased. Source: IRS.gov Additional First Year Depreciation FAQ.

**Reasonable compensation (S-corp):** The wage the IRS expects an S-corporation to pay its owner-employee for services actually performed, before any profit distributions. There is no IRS safe harbor dollar amount; it is a facts-and-circumstances determination. Paying too little triggers IRS reclassification of distributions as wages, with back payroll taxes, interest, and penalties. Source: IRS.gov S Corporation Compensation and Medical Insurance Issues.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A 3.8% surtax on the lesser of net investment income (interest, dividends, capital gains, passive rental income) or the amount by which modified AGI exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (MFJ). Source: IRC Section 1411; IRS Topic 559.

**Form 1040-ES:** The IRS form used to calculate and submit quarterly estimated tax payments for individuals. Available at IRS.gov.

**Form W-4:** The employee withholding form used to tell your employer (or your S-corp, if applicable) how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. Updating your W-4 can adjust withholding going forward for the remainder of the year.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these items before beginning the checklist. You will reference them throughout the review.

1. Your most recent paystub or payroll summary (if you receive W-2 wages from an employer or S-corp).
2. Your prior-year federal tax return (Form 1040) and, if applicable, your business return (Form 1120-S for an S-corp, Form 1065 for a partnership, or Schedule C for a sole proprietor).
3. A year-to-date Profit and Loss (P&L) statement for your business as of the most recent month end. This can be a report from your accounting software (QuickBooks, Xero, etc.) or a spreadsheet.
4. Current bank and brokerage account statements showing year-to-date investment income (dividends, interest), realized gains or losses, and any unsold positions with unrealized gains or losses.
5. Records of all estimated tax payments made so far this year (dates and amounts), plus copies of any IRS or state notices received.
6. A list of all retirement accounts you own and current-year contribution amounts to date.
7. Receipts or a summary of major business expenses paid this year: equipment purchases, home office costs, vehicle use logs, and any large one-time items.
8. Any HSA or FSA account statements showing current contributions and remaining balance.
9. Your current health insurance policy documents, confirming whether you are enrolled in an HDHP (required to contribute to an HSA).
10. Prior-year AGI (found on Line 11 of Form 1040). You need this to calculate your estimated-tax safe harbor.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Work through these steps in order. Steps 1-5 build the information you need for Steps 6-12.

**Step 1: Gather your inputs (October, or as soon as possible)**

Pull every document listed in "Required Client Inputs" above. Do not skip this step. Trying to estimate without real numbers leads to missed opportunities and incorrect payments. Open the attached Year-End Strategy Review Checklist Implementation Worksheet and enter your numbers in Section 1 as you go.

Output: Completed Section 1 of the worksheet with real year-to-date figures.

**Step 2: Project your full-year income and taxable income**

Using your year-to-date P&L and any known upcoming income, estimate your total income for the full calendar year. Add:

- Business net income (after expenses, before owner distributions or salary)
- W-2 wages (yours and any from other employment)
- Investment income: interest, dividends, realized capital gains
- Rental income (if any)
- Any other expected income before year-end

Subtract above-the-line deductions you expect to take:

- Half of self-employment tax (if you are a sole proprietor or single-member LLC)
- Self-employed health insurance premiums paid by you (not an S-corp)
- Retirement plan contributions made so far this year
- HSA contributions made so far this year

The result is your projected AGI. Then subtract your expected standard deduction (or projected itemized deductions if you will itemize -- see Step 5) to get projected taxable income.

Enter projected AGI and taxable income in Section 2 of the worksheet.

Output: Projected AGI and taxable income for 2026.

**Step 3: Compare projected income to key thresholds**

Look up your projected taxable income against these 2026 thresholds and note where you stand. Each threshold triggers a different planning action:

- **37% top bracket:** $640,600 (single) / $768,700 (MFJ). If you are near or over, every deduction you can accelerate saves at 37 cents per dollar.
- **NIIT threshold:** $200,000 (single) / $250,000 (MFJ) in modified AGI. If you are above this, investment income is subject to an additional 3.8% surtax.
- **QBI deduction phase-out begins:** $201,750 (single) / $403,500 (MFJ) in taxable income. If you are in this range, strategies that reduce taxable income may preserve the deduction.
- **QBI deduction eliminated (SSTB owners):** $276,750 (single) / $553,500 (MFJ). Above this, SSTB owners lose the 20% QBI deduction entirely.
- **IRA deductibility phase-out (if covered by a workplace plan):** $81,000-$91,000 (single) / $129,000-$149,000 (MFJ) in AGI.
- **Additional Medicare Tax:** Wages and self-employment income above $200,000 (single) / $250,000 (MFJ) are subject to an additional 0.9% Medicare tax.

Enter which thresholds you are near or over in Section 3 of the worksheet. This drives Steps 6-10.

Output: List of thresholds you are near, over, or under, with approximate dollar distance to each.

**Step 4: Verify your estimated tax payments and safe harbor position**

a. Add up all estimated tax payments made to date in 2026 (from EFTPS records or paper receipts) plus any federal withholding from W-2s.

b. Calculate your prior-year tax liability from Line 24 of your 2025 Form 1040.

c. Calculate the safe harbor amount: if your 2025 AGI was $150,000 or less, you need to pay 100% of your 2025 tax liability; if your 2025 AGI exceeded $150,000, you need 110% of the 2025 tax liability (or 90% of 2026 projected tax, whichever is smaller -- you only need to meet one test).

d. Compare total payments to date to your safe harbor amount. If you have already met the safe harbor, your priority shifts to accuracy rather than penalty avoidance. If you have not met it, use Q3 (due September 15) and Q4 (due January 15, 2027) payments to close the gap.

e. Calculate what you still owe: projected 2026 tax minus payments to date. This is your remaining liability estimate. Divide by remaining quarters to size future payments.

Enter results in Section 4 of the worksheet.

Output: Safe harbor status (met or not met), remaining estimated tax due, Q4 payment target.

**Step 5: Decide between standard deduction and itemizing**

Add up your anticipated itemized deductions:

- Mortgage interest (from Form 1098 you will receive in January; estimate from your mortgage statements)
- State and local taxes paid (income taxes plus property taxes, capped at $40,400 for 2026 under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act -- see SALT cap in Key Terms for the phasedown rules if your MAGI exceeds approximately $505,000)
- Charitable contributions (from your records)
- Medical expenses above 7.5% of your projected AGI
- Any other Schedule A items

If your total itemized deductions are higher than your standard deduction ($16,100 single / $32,200 MFJ / $24,150 head of household), you will itemize. If the standard deduction is higher, you will take the standard deduction.

If you are close to the standard deduction threshold and have upcoming charitable giving planned, consider "bunching" -- making two or three years of donations in a single year to push itemized deductions over the standard deduction threshold this year. You can use a Donor Advised Fund (DAF -- a charitable giving account that lets you take the deduction now but distribute grants to charities later) to bunch a large contribution in one year.

Note: For 2026, even if you take the standard deduction, you may deduct up to $1,000 ($2,000 MFJ) of cash contributions to qualified charitable organizations as a separate above-the-line deduction. Source: IRS.gov New and Enhanced Deductions for Individuals (One Big Beautiful Bill Act provisions).

Also note: For 2026, if you do itemize, charitable contributions are only deductible to the extent they exceed 0.5% of your AGI. Plan accordingly. Source: IRS.gov Charitable Contribution Deductions.

Enter your itemized vs. standard deduction decision in Section 5 of the worksheet.

Output: Decision to itemize or take standard deduction, and any year-end bunching moves to make.

**Step 6: Maximize retirement plan contributions**

a. Determine your contribution limits for each plan you own or are eligible for. The 2026 limits are:

- **401(k) / 403(b) / 457 elective deferral:** $24,500 (under age 50); $32,500 (age 50 or older); $35,750 (ages 60-63, using the SECURE 2.0 enhanced catch-up)
- **Traditional or Roth IRA:** $7,500 (under age 50); $8,600 (age 50 or older, using $1,100 catch-up)
- **SEP-IRA:** The lesser of 25% of net self-employment income (or W-2 compensation from your S-corp) or $72,000
- **SIMPLE IRA:** $17,000 (under age 50); $21,000 (age 50 or older); $22,250 (ages 60-63)
- **Solo 401(k) total (employee deferral + employer profit-sharing):** up to $72,000 (or $80,000 age 50+; $83,250 ages 60-63)

b. Calculate how much you have already contributed year-to-date.

c. Confirm the deadline: 401(k) elective deferrals must be contributed by December 31 of the tax year. SEP-IRA and IRA contributions can be made up to the tax-return filing deadline (typically April 15, plus any extension). Solo 401(k) plans must be established (set up with the plan provider) by December 31 of the first year you want to contribute; once established, contributions can be made through the filing deadline.

d. Contribute the maximum you can afford. Every dollar contributed to a pre-tax plan reduces your AGI and taxable income dollar-for-dollar, potentially also preserving your QBI deduction if your income is near the phase-out thresholds.

Enter your contribution gaps and actions in Section 6 of the worksheet.

Output: Confirmed contribution amounts, pending contributions, and action deadline for each plan.

**Step 7: Review S-corp reasonable compensation (S-corp owners only)**

If you own an S-corporation and work in the business, confirm that you have paid yourself a reasonable salary for the full year. The IRS expects shareholder-employees to receive wages that reflect the fair market value of their services before taking profit distributions.

a. Confirm that your W-2 wages from the S-corp are reasonable for your role, experience, and industry. Common reference sources for comparable wages: Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment Statistics, Robert Half salary guides, industry association surveys.

b. Confirm that payroll was actually run (not just documented on paper): payroll taxes were withheld and deposited, and Forms 941 were filed quarterly.

c. If you have not run payroll this year or your salary appears disproportionately low relative to distributions, discuss a year-end payroll correction with your advisor before December 31. Running payroll at year-end to catch up is possible but draws scrutiny; it is better addressed early in the year.

d. Confirm that Form W-2 will be issued by January 31 of the following year.

Enter salary amount and confirmation status in Section 7 of the worksheet.

Output: Confirmed W-2 amount, payroll compliance confirmation, or a flagged item for your advisor.

**Step 8: Accelerate deductions and defer income where appropriate**

"Accelerating" a deduction means moving it into the current tax year (paying or purchasing before December 31). "Deferring" income means pushing it into next year where legally possible. Both reduce this year's taxable income.

Review each of the following categories and decide whether action before December 31 makes sense for your situation:

**Business expenses to pay before December 31:**
- Prepay legitimate operating expenses (office supplies, software subscriptions, advertising, professional dues) that you would pay anyway.
- Purchase business equipment or software that qualifies for Section 179 expensing or 100% bonus depreciation, as long as it is placed in service (used in the business) before December 31. Maximum Section 179 deduction for 2026: $2,560,000. Bonus depreciation: 100% of qualifying cost.
- Pay any outstanding vendor invoices before year-end.

**Income to consider deferring:**
- If you are on the cash basis of accounting (which most small businesses use: you recognize income when you receive it, not when you earn it), consider delaying invoicing or collection of amounts you do not need until January. Do not defer income if it would compromise client relationships or business cash flow.
- If you have year-end bonuses you control (for example, as the owner of your business), you may be able to delay paying yourself until January.

**Caution:** Do not engage in transactions lacking economic substance solely to reduce taxes. The IRS can disregard transactions that have no business purpose beyond tax avoidance.

Enter acceleration and deferral decisions in Section 8 of the worksheet.

Output: List of expenses to pay before December 31 and income items to defer (with amounts and deadlines).

**Step 9: Review capital gains and losses (investment accounts)**

a. Pull a summary of all realized gains and losses in your taxable brokerage accounts year-to-date.

b. Review unrealized gains and losses in your portfolio.

c. If you have net unrealized losses: consider selling positions before December 31 to realize those losses. Capital losses offset capital gains dollar-for-dollar. If losses exceed gains, up to $3,000 of net capital loss can offset ordinary income this year; any excess carries forward indefinitely.

d. If you have net unrealized gains but are in a low-income year: consider realizing some gains now if you are in the 0% long-term capital gains bracket. For 2026, the 0% rate applies to long-term capital gains if your taxable income is below $49,450 (single) or $98,900 (MFJ). Source: Rev. Proc. 2025-32 (IRB 2025-45).

e. Watch the 30-day wash-sale rule: if you sell a security at a loss and buy the same or a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale, the IRS disallows the loss deduction. Source: IRC Section 1091.

Enter capital gain/loss summary and action plan in Section 9 of the worksheet.

Output: Net realized gain/loss position, any tax-loss harvesting trades to execute, and dates by which trades must settle before year-end.

**Step 10: Confirm HSA and FSA contributions**

a. If you have an HSA: confirm your total contributions for 2026 (employer contributions + your contributions). The 2026 limit is $4,400 (self-only) or $8,750 (family). If you are 55 or older, add $1,000. You have until April 15, 2027 to make 2026 contributions to your HSA. To be eligible, you must be enrolled in an HDHP with a minimum annual deductible of $1,700 (self-only) or $3,400 (family) for 2026.

b. If you have an FSA through your employer: confirm your annual election amount and your remaining balance. Most FSAs operate on a "use it or lose it" basis, with a maximum carryover to next year of $680. If you have unspent FSA funds and the plan year ends December 31, spend the balance before year-end on eligible medical expenses.

c. If you are not yet enrolled in an HSA-eligible HDHP but want to start for next year: open enrollment is typically the window to switch health plans. Check with your employer or marketplace for open enrollment dates.

Enter HSA/FSA status in Section 10 of the worksheet.

Output: Confirmed HSA contribution amount, any HSA catch-up contributions to make, and FSA spend-down plan.

**Step 11: Review home office and vehicle deductions**

**Home office:**
- If you use a portion of your home exclusively and regularly for business (and it is your principal place of business), you are entitled to a home office deduction.
- Simplified method: $5 per square foot of dedicated office space, up to 300 square feet, maximum deduction of $1,500. Requires no documentation of actual expenses.
- Regular method: Deduct the business-use percentage (office square feet divided by total home square feet) of actual home expenses (rent or mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, repairs). Requires documentation of all home expenses.
- Note: Employees working from home (W-2 employees) generally cannot deduct home office expenses under current law. This deduction applies to self-employed individuals and business owners. Source: IRS Topic 509; IRS Publication 587.

**Vehicle:**
- Confirm you have a mileage log showing business miles driven for 2026. For 2026, the standard mileage rate is 72.5 cents per mile for business use. Source: IRS Notice 2026-10 / IR-2025-128.
- Alternatively, you can deduct actual vehicle expenses (gas, insurance, maintenance, depreciation) multiplied by the business-use percentage. You must choose one method; generally you can switch between methods from year to year as long as you did not use MACRS depreciation on the vehicle in a prior year.
- If you purchased a vehicle for business use in 2026, confirm whether it qualifies for Section 179 or bonus depreciation. SUVs have a $32,000 limit on Section 179 for 2026. Source: Rev. Proc. 2025-32.

Enter home office and vehicle deduction method and amounts in Section 11 of the worksheet.

Output: Confirmed deduction method, amount, and verification that mileage log is complete through year-end.

**Step 12: Final review and year-end action list**

Compile everything from Steps 1-11 into a short action list: specific dollar amounts, specific actions, and specific deadlines. Each item should be one of these categories:

- Pay before December 31 (equipment, expenses, HSA contributions if applicable, retirement deferrals, estimated tax payments)
- File or elect before December 31 (solo 401(k) plan establishment if new, S-corp payroll confirmation)
- Defer until January (certain invoices or income, if applicable)
- Complete paperwork by December 31 (mileage log, home office measurement)
- Review with your advisor before acting (see Advisor Review Gates below)

Bring this completed action list and the worksheet to your advisor review meeting. Do not take irreversible actions (large purchases, entity elections, payroll adjustments) without completing the advisor review gate.

Output: Written, dated action list with dollar amounts and deadlines.

**Recurring cadence:** Ideally, you run a lighter version of this review quarterly: check estimated tax payments after each quarter, review your P&L monthly, and keep your mileage log and receipts current throughout the year. The year-end review is most powerful when you are not starting from scratch.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity-specific considerations:**

- **Sole proprietors / Schedule C:** Your retirement plan contributions reduce Schedule C net income (for SE tax purposes through the deduction for half of SE tax) and your AGI. Establish a solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA before year-end if you do not have one. Key deadline: solo 401(k) plan document must be executed by December 31.

- **S-corporation owners:** Your ability to contribute to a solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA through the S-corp depends on your W-2 wages from the corporation. Retirement plan contributions are based on W-2 wages, not S-corp distributions. If your wage is too low, your deductible retirement contribution is also capped low. Review both reasonable compensation and retirement contribution together.

- **Partnership and multi-member LLC members:** Partner compensation is generally in the form of guaranteed payments (reported on Schedule K-1 as ordinary income, subject to SE tax) rather than W-2 wages. Year-end allocations and guaranteed payment timing should be coordinated with your partnership agreement and advisor.

- **C-corporation owners:** If you have C-corp income flowing to you as a dividend, that income is not QBI-eligible and is subject to qualified dividend tax rates (not the 20% QBI deduction). Year-end bonus strategies differ significantly for C-corps; consult your advisor.

**Payroll considerations:**

- Payroll taxes on your S-corp W-2 must be deposited with the IRS on a schedule (monthly or semi-weekly) throughout the year. A large year-end payroll catch-up draws IRS scrutiny and may not fully satisfy the quarterly payroll tax deposit schedule. Address compensation issues before Q4 where possible.
- Form 941 (Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return) is due for each quarter. The Q4 2026 Form 941 is due January 31, 2027.
- W-2 forms must be furnished to employees by January 31, 2027, and filed with the SSA.

**State-specific considerations:**

- This checklist covers federal tax only. States vary widely in income tax rates, retirement plan deductibility, itemized deduction rules, estimated tax deadlines, and QBI treatment. Many states do not conform to the QBI deduction or bonus depreciation. Confirm state-specific rules with your advisor.
- Some states have their own year-end deadlines that differ from federal (for example, some state estimated tax Q4 payments are due January 15 of the following year, others differ).
- If you operate in multiple states, year-end planning must account for each state's apportionment rules.

**One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) 2026 provisions:**

- 100% bonus depreciation is reinstated for qualifying property placed in service after January 19, 2025.
- The QBI deduction is now permanent (no longer expiring after 2025).
- The QBI phase-in range was expanded: $75,000 (single) / $150,000 (MFJ).
- New deductions apply starting in 2026: tips (up to $25,000, all filing statuses), overtime ($12,500 single / $25,000 MFJ), senior ($6,000), and non-itemizer charitable ($1,000 single / $2,000 MFJ). See The Numbers section for details.
- The SALT cap is enacted at $40,400 for 2026 (up from the prior $10,000 cap), with a phasedown for MAGI above approximately $505,000 and a reversion to $10,000 in 2030. This is enacted law, not a proposal. Source: IRS.gov One Big Beautiful Bill Provisions.
- The charitable deduction for itemizers is now subject to a 0.5%-of-AGI floor starting in 2026.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Skipping the income projection (Step 2):** Year-end planning without a real number is guesswork. Most missed opportunities happen because the client did not know their income was near a threshold. Run an actual P&L.

**2. Missing the December 31 retirement deferral deadline:** SEP-IRA and IRA contributions can wait until April 15, but 401(k) elective deferrals cannot. If you wait until tax season to think about this, you have already forfeited the current-year 401(k) deferral.

**3. Not establishing a solo 401(k) before December 31:** If you are self-employed and want to contribute to a solo 401(k) for the first time, the plan must be legally established (executed as a plan document with a custodian) by December 31 of the year you want contributions to count. The dollars can go in later, but the plan must exist.

**4. Confusing S-corp distributions and wages:** Making large distributions and little-to-no payroll is one of the most common and costly S-corp mistakes. The IRS regularly audits S-corps where shareholder wages are disproportionately low relative to distributions.

**5. Ignoring estimated tax safe harbor:** Many business owners either underpay quarterly and face a penalty, or overpay and unnecessarily reduce their cash flow. Check your safe harbor position at the beginning of Q4.

**6. Wash-sale violations:** Selling a losing investment and immediately buying it back (or a substantially identical one) within 30 days disallows the loss. Know the wash-sale window before you trade.

**7. Missing the FSA spend-down deadline:** Unused FSA funds are forfeited at year-end (beyond the $680 carryover maximum). Spend remaining balances on qualified medical, dental, or vision expenses before the plan year closes.

**8. Not getting a mileage log to support vehicle deductions:** The IRS requires contemporaneous records of business miles. A log created after the fact is a weak defense in an audit. Use a mileage tracking app all year.

**9. Assuming bonus depreciation rules from prior years still apply:** The OBBBA reinstated 100% bonus depreciation, but rules change with legislation. Confirm the current rule applies to your specific property type with your advisor before making a large purchase for tax reasons.

**10. Skipping the state layer:** Federal planning without considering your state can produce surprises. Some states conform to the federal QBI deduction; many do not. Some states have lower standard deductions or no standard deduction at all.

## Advisor Review Gates

Before taking any of the following actions, complete a review with your advisor. These are high-stakes, hard-to-reverse decisions where errors are costly:

1. **Adjusting S-corp payroll or W-2 wages mid- or late-year.** Changing reasonable compensation requires documenting the business rationale; a catch-up payroll without documentation creates audit exposure.

2. **Making a large equipment purchase solely for Section 179 or bonus depreciation.** Confirm the property qualifies, that you have enough business income to absorb the deduction, and that your state conforms (many states do not allow 100% bonus depreciation).

3. **Realizing large capital gains intentionally.** If you are considering selling a business asset or large investment position, confirm the tax rate (short-term vs. long-term), the NIIT exposure, and whether the gain pushes you into a higher bracket or over a QBI phase-out threshold.

4. **Converting a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA.** A Roth conversion adds to your ordinary income this year; timing and amount must be coordinated with your projected bracket and QBI position.

5. **Making a charitable contribution above $250 (cash) or any non-cash contribution.** Substantiation rules are strict; contributions over $500 of non-cash property require Form 8283; contributions over $5,000 of non-cash property require a qualified appraisal.

6. **Changing your estimated tax payment amount significantly.** If your income projection changes materially late in the year, recalculate your safe harbor before reducing or skipping a payment.

7. **Opening or changing a retirement plan type.** Different plans have different adoption deadlines, plan document requirements, and funding obligations. Do not switch plans or establish a new plan without understanding the setup requirements.

8. **Applying any new OBBBA deduction (tips, overtime, senior, vehicle loan interest).** These are new provisions for 2026; eligibility rules, income phase-outs, and documentation requirements are still being refined in IRS guidance. Confirm eligibility with your advisor before claiming.

This worksheet is your companion through every step of this playbook. Here is how to use it:

## Before You Act

This checklist teaches you how to identify opportunities and organize your facts. It does not replace a review of your specific situation by a qualified tax professional. Tax law is complex, and the numbers on this page are general; your actual deduction amounts, thresholds, and deadlines may differ based on your entity type, state, prior-year elections, and individual circumstances.

Before filing any tax return, making any retirement plan contribution, completing any large equipment purchase for depreciation purposes, adjusting your S-corp payroll, harvesting capital losses, converting a retirement account, or making any other consequential year-end tax move: confirm the specific facts of your situation with your advisor.

The deadline for most year-end moves is December 31. Plan your advisor review meeting for early to mid-December -- not December 29.
