# Business Intention Framework

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The Business Intention Framework is a system for building and maintaining the documentation that proves your activity is a genuine, profit-seeking business rather than a personal hobby. The IRS draws a hard line between these two categories: business losses can offset other income on your tax return, but hobby losses generally cannot be deducted at all. By operating with clear business intent and keeping contemporaneous records that show that intent, you protect every deduction your business generates and significantly reduce your audit risk.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is relevant to you if any of the following are true:

- You operate a business that generates losses in some years, particularly in the startup phase.
- Your business overlaps with a personal interest (photography, coaching, horses, crafts, consulting in a field you enjoy, real estate, etc.).
- You are a sole proprietor filing Schedule C (Form 1040) or an S corporation owner with pass-through losses.
- You are in the first two to five years of a new business and have not yet shown consistent profits.
- You want to prevent the IRS from reclassifying your legitimate business expenses as personal hobby expenses in an audit.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- Your business already shows consistent profits year over year and has no overlap with personal recreation. In that case, the hobby-loss risk is low and minimal additional documentation is needed beyond normal recordkeeping.
- You have a W-2 job and a side project you truly pursue for fun with no genuine expectation of profit. In that case, you should not claim business deductions for it at all. Discuss the facts with your advisor before deducting any losses.
- You have already received an IRS notice proposing to disallow deductions under IRC Section 183. At that point, stop and contact your advisor immediately before taking any further steps. You are in a different workflow.

## Key Terms

**IRC Section 183 ("hobby loss rule"):** The Internal Revenue Code section that limits deductions when the IRS determines your activity is not engaged in for profit. If Section 183 applies, you must still report all income from the activity, but your deductions are capped at that income (not allowed to produce a loss that offsets other income). As of 2026, hobby expenses are also no longer deductible as miscellaneous itemized deductions (see Entity, Payroll, State, and Current-Year Caveats below). The practical result: hobby income becomes nearly fully taxable with little or no offsetting deduction.

**IRC Section 162 ("ordinary and necessary" test):** The code section that permits deductions for expenses paid or incurred while carrying on a trade or business. An expense qualifies when it is both "ordinary" (common and accepted in your type of business) and "necessary" (appropriate and helpful to your business). This is the standard your expenses must meet to be deductible.

**Profit motive:** Your genuine, honest intent to earn a profit from the activity. The IRS does not require that you actually earn a profit every year. It requires that you entered and operate the activity with the real intention of making money. Profit motive is determined by looking at objective facts and circumstances, not just your self-declaration.

**Contemporaneous record:** A document created at or near the time of the event or expense it describes, rather than reconstructed from memory later. The IRS gives contemporaneous records far greater weight than records created after the fact. "At or near the time" generally means within the same week or immediately after the event.

**Nine-factor test:** The framework from Treasury Regulation 1.183-2(b) that IRS examiners use to weigh whether an activity has a profit motive. No single factor is decisive; all facts and circumstances are considered together. See Step-By-Step Implementation for the full list.

**Profit presumption (safe harbor):** A rule in IRC Section 183(d) that shifts the burden of proof to the IRS. If your activity shows a profit (gross income exceeding deductions) in at least 3 of any 5 consecutive tax years, the IRS presumes you are engaged in the activity for profit. For activities involving the breeding, training, showing, or racing of horses, the threshold is a profit in at least 2 of any 7 consecutive years. Once the presumption applies, the IRS must prove you lack profit motive, not the other way around.

**Form 5213:** An IRS election form ("Election to Postpone Determination As To Whether the Presumption Applies That an Activity is Engaged in for Profit") that asks the IRS to delay a hobby-loss determination until the 5-year (or 7-year) profit-presumption period is complete. Filing it has significant trade-offs; discuss with your advisor before using it.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The IRS form used by sole proprietors and single-member LLCs to report business profit or loss. It is one of the most audited tax forms because losses on it directly reduce your taxable income from wages and other sources.

**Statute of limitations:** The time period within which the IRS can legally audit your return and assess additional tax. The standard period is 3 years from the filing date. It extends to 6 years if you omit more than 25% of gross income. It is unlimited for fraud.

**TCJA:** The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 (Public Law 115-97). Among other changes, it suspended miscellaneous itemized deductions (including hobby expense deductions) for tax years 2018 through 2025.

**One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA):** Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025. It made the TCJA suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions permanent beginning January 1, 2026. Hobby expenses remain nondeductible. See Entity, Payroll, State, and Current-Year Caveats.

**Miscellaneous itemized deduction:** A category of personal deductions (historically subject to a 2% of adjusted gross income floor) that includes hobby expenses. Permanently suspended as of 2026 under the OBBBA.

**Adjusted gross income (AGI):** Your total gross income minus specific "above-the-line" deductions such as self-employment tax, retirement contributions, and health insurance premiums for self-employed individuals. AGI is an important threshold for many tax calculations.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before you begin building or updating your documentation system:

1. **Business description in writing.** One paragraph explaining what your business does, who your customers are, and how it makes (or plans to make) money.
2. **A list of every year the business has operated** and whether it showed a profit or loss each year.
3. **Total gross income and total deductions for each prior year** from your tax returns or bookkeeping software.
4. **List of all personal interests or recreational activities** that overlap with the business (be honest -- this is for your eyes and your advisor's only).
5. **Copies of any prior-year tax returns** on which business losses were claimed.
6. **Any IRS correspondence** about the business activity, including any notices proposing to disallow deductions.
7. **Your current bookkeeping method** (software name, whether books are current, who maintains them).
8. **A list of training, courses, certifications, industry memberships, and subscriptions** related to the business.

## Required Documents And Records

**Ongoing records to create and keep:**

| Document | Purpose | When to Create It |
|---|---|---|
| Business intent statement | Shows the profit motive behind the activity | Before or at the start of each tax year |
| Annual business plan or strategy memo | Shows businesslike operation | At the start of each year |
| Profit-and-loss goal for the year | Shows you track financial performance | At the start of each year |
| Monthly or quarterly P&L review notes | Shows active management | Within the month or quarter reviewed |
| Decision log | Records key business decisions and the business reason behind them | Within the same week as each decision |
| Receipts and invoices | Substantiates each expense | At the time of purchase |
| Bank and credit card statements | Corroborates receipts | Maintained monthly |
| Mileage log (if claiming vehicle) | Required substantiation under IRC Section 274(d) | At the time of each trip |
| Time log | Shows time and effort devoted to the business | Weekly or daily |
| Customer or client contracts and correspondence | Shows you are actively seeking paying customers | At the time of execution |
| Marketing materials and records | Shows efforts to generate revenue | At the time of creation |
| Industry research and training records | Shows expertise factor | As acquired |
| Expert or advisor consultation notes | Shows you sought and acted on expert guidance | Within the same week as the consultation |

**Retention guidance (based on IRS guidance at IRS.gov "How long should I keep records?"):**

- Standard business records: at least 3 years from the date you filed the return (the standard assessment window).
- If your net losses are large relative to gross income, or if you are in the early years of the business: keep records for at least 6 years (to cover the extended assessment period if the IRS claims unreported income exceeded 25% of gross income).
- Best practice recommended by many tax professionals: retain all business-intention documentation for 7 years.
- Property records (equipment, vehicles, improvements): keep until you dispose of the property, plus the applicable statute period.
- Never discard documentation for a year that is currently under IRS examination.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Establish your documented business foundation

**Step 1: Write a dated Business Intention Statement.**

Create a one-to-two-page document that covers:
- What the business does and the specific products or services it sells.
- Who the intended customers are.
- How the business will generate revenue and, eventually, profit.
- Why you (the owner) have the skills and knowledge to make it profitable.
- What your profit target is for the current year and the next one to two years.

Date and sign the document. Save it to your Tax Vault under the current year's business folder. Use the Business Intention Framework Implementation Worksheet (see Using The Download(s)) to structure this document.

**Step 2: Open and maintain a dedicated business bank account.**

Every dollar of business income should go in; every business expense should come out of this account. Mixing personal and business transactions in one account is one of the clearest signals of a hobby to an IRS examiner.

**Step 3: Establish and maintain complete, accurate books.**

Use accounting software (QuickBooks, Wave, FreshBooks, or equivalent) or a spreadsheet maintained monthly. Generate a profit-and-loss statement at least quarterly. The IRS looks at whether you "carry on the activity in a businesslike manner and maintain complete and accurate books and records" (Treasury Reg. 1.183-2(b)(1)) as its first and most heavily weighted factor.

**Step 4: Create an Annual Business Plan at the start of each tax year.**

A one-to-three-page plan is enough. Include:
- Revenue targets.
- Marketing or client-acquisition strategy for the year.
- Major planned expenses and why they are necessary.
- Lessons from the prior year and how you are adjusting.

Date the plan and save it in your Tax Vault.

### Phase 2: Build your nine-factor documentation file

For each of the nine factors in Treasury Reg. 1.183-2(b), keep specific evidence. The worksheet in the download walks through each factor.

**Factor 1: Manner of carrying on the activity (businesslike operation).**
Evidence: separate bank account, complete books, business plan, business email and website or marketing presence, formal contracts with customers. Action: confirm all of these are in place and documented before year-end.

**Factor 2: Expertise of taxpayer or advisors.**
Evidence: certifications, courses, industry memberships, subscriptions to trade publications, records of consulting experts or experienced practitioners. Action: list every training activity and keep the certificates or receipts.

**Factor 3: Time and effort expended.**
Evidence: a time log or calendar showing hours devoted to the business. Action: log business hours weekly. If you also hold a W-2 job, document your part-time business hours specifically.

**Factor 4: Expectation of asset appreciation.**
Evidence: if the business involves appreciating assets (land, equipment, intellectual property, a brand), document the expected appreciation in your business plan. Action: include an asset-value projection in your annual plan if applicable.

**Factor 5: Success in similar or prior activities.**
Evidence: prior businesses you successfully built or turned profitable; relevant professional background. Action: write a brief career and business history section in your Business Intention Statement.

**Factor 6: Income and loss history.**
Evidence: your actual P&L for each year the business has operated. Action: keep all prior-year tax returns and P&L statements. If losses are increasing, document the specific reason in a decision log entry (startup costs, a bad market year, a client who did not pay, etc.).

**Factor 7: Amount of occasional profits.**
Evidence: any year in which the business showed a profit. Action: track cumulative profit years toward the 3-of-5-year safe harbor. If you are two profitable years in, note this in your annual review.

**Factor 8: Financial status of taxpayer.**
Evidence: if you depend on this business as your primary or sole income source, that supports profit motive. If you have substantial W-2 wages and the business generates losses that offset those wages, be prepared to explain why the losses are temporary and business-driven. Action: your Business Intention Statement should note your financial dependence on the business succeeding.

**Factor 9: Elements of personal pleasure or recreation.**
Evidence (and risk): if the activity is inherently recreational or matches a personal hobby, the IRS will scrutinize more heavily. Action: document the commercial aspects aggressively. Emphasize customer relationships, revenue targets, and competitive market analysis. Show that the activity is managed for profit, not fun.

### Phase 3: Maintain your documentation throughout the year

**Step 5: Log each key business decision within the same week it is made.**

Format: date, decision made, business reason for the decision, and what outcome you expect. Example: "March 14 -- Hired a freelance photographer to shoot product images for the website. Business reason: current phone photos are reducing conversion on the sales page. Expected outcome: higher click-to-purchase rate for the spring campaign." Keep this log in a running document in your Tax Vault.

**Step 6: Conduct and document quarterly P&L reviews.**

Pull your actual P&L from your bookkeeping software each quarter. Write a one-paragraph note comparing actual to plan and explaining any significant variances. Save the note with the P&L. This is evidence of active financial management.

**Step 7: Track your path to the profit presumption safe harbor.**

Count the years in your current 5-year window in which gross income exceeded deductions. Note this in your annual review. When you reach 3 profitable years in 5 (or 2 in 7 for horse activities), the burden of proof shifts to the IRS.

**Step 8: Review and update your Business Intention Statement each January.**

The statement should reflect where the business is now, not just where it started. Update the revenue history, update the plan, and re-date it.

### Phase 4: Consult your advisor before acting on edge-case decisions

**Step 9: Discuss Form 5213 with your advisor if the IRS raises the issue.**

Form 5213 (Election to Postpone Determination) lets you ask the IRS to wait until the 5-year (or 7-year) profit-presumption period ends before making a hobby-loss determination. However, filing it: (1) signals to the IRS that you acknowledge a potential hobby-loss issue, (2) automatically extends the statute of limitations for the activity, and (3) is rarely advisable unless the IRS has already notified you of a proposed disallowance. Do not file Form 5213 without first discussing it with your advisor.

**Step 10: Use your advisor to evaluate whether to elect out of the profit presumption period.**

If you have already been in business for the full 5-year window and have not met the 3-of-5-year profit test, your advisor can help you evaluate the strength of your nine-factor documentation versus the risk of an IRS challenge. This is a fact-specific determination.

- Weekly: update time log and decision log.

- Annually: update Business Intention Statement and Business Plan; count profit years toward the safe harbor; conduct year-end documentation review.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**2026 law change -- permanent elimination of hobby expense deductions.**
Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025), the TCJA suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions became permanent beginning January 1, 2026. Hobby expenses were previously deductible as miscellaneous itemized deductions (subject to the 2% AGI floor) before 2018, then suspended 2018 through 2025. As of 2026 and beyond, those deductions are permanently gone. If your activity is reclassified as a hobby, you must still report all hobby income, but you can deduct essentially nothing. This makes the Business Intention Framework more important for 2026 than it has ever been.

**S corporation and partnership owners.**
The nine-factor test applies at the entity level for S corporations. If you own an S-corp and the corporation's activity lacks profit motive, Section 183 can apply at the entity level, not just to you personally. Your entity's operating records (meeting minutes, board resolutions, operating agreements, documented owner-compensation decisions) all contribute to the nine-factor analysis. Keep corporate records current.

**Single-member LLCs (disregarded entities).**
A single-member LLC that has not elected to be treated as a corporation is a disregarded entity for federal tax purposes. Its activity is treated as the owner's Schedule C activity. The Section 183 analysis is the same as for a sole proprietor.

**Multiple states.**
If you conduct business in more than one state, verify with your advisor whether each state follows federal Section 183 treatment. Most do, but state conformity is not guaranteed.

**Schedule C audit risk.**
Repeated losses on Schedule C are among the most common IRS audit triggers for self-employed individuals. This playbook's documentation system is also your audit defense kit. Keep it current throughout the year, not only when you know an audit is coming.

**New businesses.**
Courts and the IRS understand that a new business typically loses money in its early years. Startup losses in years 1 through 3 are expected and do not automatically indicate a hobby. The IRS Audit Technique Guide for Section 183 explicitly acknowledges that "losses continue to be sustained beyond the period which customarily is necessary" is the relevant concern. Losses in year 1 or 2 of a new business, paired with a strong Business Intention Statement and active management documentation, are defensible.

**Activities with strong recreational elements.**
Activities such as photography, horse breeding, travel blogging, coaching in a sport you personally play, or running a side business in any field you enjoy recreationally will face a higher burden because Factor 9 (personal pleasure or recreation) works against you. Compensate with especially strong documentation on Factors 1 (businesslike operation), 2 (expertise), 3 (time and effort), and 5 (prior success).

## Common Mistakes

**1. Treating the Business Intention Framework as a one-time exercise.**
Creating a Business Intention Statement in year one and never updating it is a missed opportunity. The IRS looks at conduct throughout the life of the activity. Update your statement annually.

**2. Reconstructing records at year-end instead of keeping them contemporaneously.**
A mileage log written in December to cover January through November, or a decision log assembled the week before taxes are due, carries little evidentiary weight. The IRS and courts treat contemporaneous records far more favorably.

**3. Mixing personal and business finances.**
Using a personal checking account for business income and expenses removes a foundational element of businesslike operation (Factor 1) and makes it nearly impossible to substantiate deductions cleanly.

**4. Claiming expenses that cannot survive Factor 9 scrutiny.**
If a deduction is for something you would buy regardless of the business -- a sport you play anyway, equipment you use personally, trips to places you vacation -- the business purpose must be clearly documented and genuinely primary. Document the specific business purpose at the time of each purchase, not after the fact.

**5. Ignoring the profit presumption safe harbor.**
Many business owners do not realize this safe harbor exists. If your business is approaching 3 profitable years in the current 5-year window, discuss that milestone with your advisor. It shifts the burden of proof in a meaningful way.

**6. Filing Form 5213 without professional advice.**
This form can alert the IRS to a potential issue and extend the statute of limitations. It is rarely the right move without an IRS notice already in hand. Discuss it with your advisor first.

**7. Treating a hobby as a business just to claim deductions.**
Section 183 exists precisely because some taxpayers do this. If you genuinely do not intend to earn a profit, do not claim business deductions. The risk of audit, reclassification, and penalties is real, and as of 2026, hobby expenses are not deductible at all.

**8. Not seeking or following expert guidance (Factor 2).**
An IRS examiner will ask whether you consulted people who know the industry. If the answer is no, Factor 2 works against you. Consult industry experts or advisors early, document those consultations, and show that you incorporated their advice.

## Advisor Review Gates

Contact your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

1. **Before claiming losses from an activity that overlaps with a personal interest.** Have an explicit conversation about whether the nine-factor facts support a profit motive determination for your specific activity.

2. **If the business has not shown a profit in the first 3 of 5 years.** Discuss the strength of your documentation and whether any adjustments to your business operation or deduction strategy are warranted.

3. **If you receive any IRS correspondence mentioning Section 183, hobby losses, or a proposed disallowance of business deductions.** Do not respond to IRS correspondence about this topic without professional guidance. You have a 60-day window from receipt of the notice to file Form 5213 if that election is appropriate; do not let that window close without a conversation.

4. **Before filing Form 5213.** This election has significant trade-offs and should never be filed without advisor guidance.

5. **If you are adding a new activity that has recreational elements.** Set up the documentation system before the first year, not after. Retroactive documentation is far weaker than prospective documentation.

6. **If you are converting an S corporation or LLC to a new entity structure.** The nine-factor analysis can be affected by entity type and how the business is operated.

7. **Before year-end if you are in a year that could swing the profit-presumption count.** Your advisor can help you evaluate whether a year-end strategy decision (accelerating income, deferring an expense) could push you into a profitable year for presumption purposes.

## Using The Download(s)

**Business Intention Framework Implementation Worksheet**

This worksheet is the core working document for this playbook. Use it in the following sequence:

- **Step 1 (Business Intention Statement, Phase 1 of the playbook):** Open the worksheet and complete the Business Intention Statement section first. Fill in your business description, customer profile, revenue model, profit target, and owner expertise section. Date and save it. This becomes your primary defense document.

- **Nine-Factor Documentation Section:** The worksheet contains a section for each of the nine factors from Treasury Reg. 1.183-2(b). For each factor, record the specific evidence you have and note any gaps. Use this to identify which factors need more documentation before year-end.

- **Annual Review:** Each January, open last year's completed worksheet, duplicate it or create a new one for the current year, and update every section. The date on each annual version matters -- it shows the IRS that you actively reviewed and updated your business posture year over year.

- **Quarterly P&L Review Tab:** Use the worksheet's P&L review section each quarter to record your actual income, actual expenses, variance from plan, and one-paragraph explanation of significant variances.

- **Decision Log:** The worksheet includes a running decision log table. Add an entry for every significant business decision: hiring, major purchases, marketing changes, pricing changes, pivots in business model. Date every entry. Do not batch entries at year-end.

Save each completed worksheet to your Tax Vault under the folder for the applicable tax year. Do not delete prior years' worksheets; they form a chronological record of your ongoing profit motive.

## Before You Act

Before filing a return that includes losses from any activity that overlaps with personal interests, before claiming deductions under IRC Section 162 for a new or unprofitable business, or before responding to any IRS correspondence about a potential hobby-loss issue:

**Confirm your specific facts with your advisor.**

This playbook gives you the framework and the documentation system. It does not substitute for a professional review of your individual situation. Tax law under IRC Section 183 turns on the specific facts of each activity, each taxpayer, and each year. No general playbook can tell you with certainty how the nine factors will be weighed in your case.

Your advisor should review:
- Your completed Business Intention Framework Implementation Worksheet.
- Your profit-and-loss history for the activity.
- The personal-interest overlap, if any.
- Any IRS correspondence you have received.

Do not file, respond to the IRS, file Form 5213, or change your business structure in response to a Section 183 concern without completing that review first.
