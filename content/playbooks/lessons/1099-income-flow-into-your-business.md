# 1099 Income Flow Into Your Business

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

When you earn income as an independent contractor or freelancer, payers report those payments on Form 1099-NEC (Nonemployee Compensation). By default, that income lands on your personal tax return as self-employment income, exposing every dollar to the 15.3% self-employment tax. This playbook shows you how to direct that same income into a properly structured business entity, so the entity receives the payment, claims allowable business deductions against it, and, in some structures, reduces the portion of your earnings subject to self-employment tax. The result is a lower combined tax bill and a more defensible paper trail, provided the structure is set up correctly before income arrives.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is for you if ALL of the following are true:

- You receive or expect to receive Form 1099-NEC (or 1099-MISC for certain payments) reporting income for services you perform independently, freelance, or as a contractor.
- Your total annual 1099 income from self-employment services is at least $10,000. Below that level the administrative cost of a separate entity often exceeds the tax savings.
- You are a U.S. person (citizen, resident alien, or domestic entity) subject to U.S. federal income tax.
- You perform services through which you are the primary earner, not an employee receiving a W-2 from those same payers.
- You have (or are willing to open) a separate business bank account and keep basic financial records.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do NOT use this strategy, or consult your advisor before proceeding, if ANY of the following apply:

- **Your payer insists on issuing the 1099 in your personal Social Security Number (SSN).** Some clients will not update their vendor records to reflect an entity EIN. If the 1099 comes to you personally and the entity cannot establish a clean paper trail showing the income belongs to it, the structure may be challenged.
- **You are a common-law employee misclassified as a contractor.** Routing payroll-type income through an entity does not correct a worker-classification problem; it may worsen it. If your work looks like employment (set hours, one client, employer-provided tools), consult your advisor about reclassification risk before forming an entity.
- **You work in a Specified Service Trade or Business (SSTB) and your taxable income exceeds the QBI phase-out range.** SSTBs include health, law, accounting, financial services, consulting, performing arts, athletics, and certain brokerage services. At higher income levels, key deductions phase out for SSTBs. See "The Numbers" and "Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats."
- **You are not willing or able to run payroll.** An S-corporation (the structure most often recommended to reduce self-employment tax) legally requires you to pay yourself a reasonable salary via payroll. If you cannot commit to quarterly payroll and payroll-tax deposits, do not elect S-corp status.
- **Your net self-employment income is too low to justify an S-corp.** The compliance cost of payroll, an additional state filing, and a separate tax return typically makes an S-corp worthwhile only when net annual 1099 income exceeds roughly $40,000-$50,000. Ask your advisor to run the break-even calculation for your state.
- **You have not yet registered a legal entity in your state.** This playbook assumes the entity either already exists or will be formed before income is earned or directed. Do not backdate entity formation.

## Key Terms

**Form 1099-NEC (Nonemployee Compensation):** An IRS information return that businesses use to report payments of $2,000 or more (for 2026 and later; previously $600) made to non-employees for services. The payer sends a copy to you and a copy to the IRS. You are responsible for reporting and paying tax on this income whether or not a 1099 is issued.

**Self-employment (SE) tax:** The combined Social Security and Medicare tax that self-employed individuals pay. Because you are both the employer and employee, you pay both halves: 12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare, totaling 15.3%. It is calculated on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings. You may deduct half of SE tax paid as an above-the-line deduction on Form 1040.

**Schedule C (Profit or Loss From Business):** The IRS form (attached to Form 1040) on which sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (disregarded entities) report gross business income, subtract business expenses, and arrive at net profit or loss.

**Schedule SE (Self-Employment Tax):** The IRS form used to calculate the SE tax owed on net self-employment earnings of $400 or more.

**Sole proprietor:** A person who operates a business individually and reports all business income and expenses directly on their personal Form 1040. No separate legal entity exists. A single-member LLC that has not elected corporate tax treatment is taxed exactly like a sole proprietor.

**Single-member LLC (SMLLC):** A Limited Liability Company with one owner. For federal income-tax purposes, a SMLLC is a "disregarded entity" by default, meaning the IRS ignores the entity and taxes the owner exactly as a sole proprietor. The LLC provides state-law liability protection but does not reduce self-employment tax on its own.

**S-corporation (S-corp):** A corporation (or LLC that has elected corporate status) that has further elected to be taxed under Subchapter S of the Internal Revenue Code. The business itself does not pay federal income tax; income and losses "pass through" to shareholder returns. The key tax advantage: only the salary you pay yourself as a shareholder-employee is subject to payroll/SE tax. Distributions of remaining profit to shareholders are not subject to payroll tax.

**Reasonable compensation:** The salary the IRS expects an S-corp owner who performs services for the business to pay themselves before taking profit distributions. It must reflect what a comparable business would pay an unrelated employee to do the same work. Paying yourself too little to avoid payroll tax is a known IRS audit trigger.

**EIN (Employer Identification Number):** A nine-digit federal tax ID number assigned to a business entity, analogous to a Social Security Number for individuals. Used on tax returns, payroll filings, and information returns like 1099s. Apply at no cost on IRS.gov using Form SS-4 or the online EIN application.

**Form W-9 (Request for Taxpayer Identification Number and Certification):** The form your business gives to clients before they pay you. It provides them your entity name, EIN, and tax classification so they can correctly file the 1099. If you have an entity, the W-9 should list the entity name and EIN, not your personal name and SSN.

**Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction (Section 199A):** A deduction allowing eligible business owners to deduct up to 20% of their qualified business income from a pass-through entity (sole proprietorship, partnership, S-corp). Made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025). Subject to income thresholds and, for high earners, a W-2-wage limitation. Does not apply to wages you receive as an employee.

**Disregarded entity:** A business entity the IRS treats as non-existent for income-tax purposes, taxing the income directly on the owner's return. A SMLLC without a corporate election is the most common example.

**Pass-through entity:** A business structure where the entity's income, deductions, and credits flow through to the owners' personal returns rather than being taxed at the entity level. S-corps, partnerships, and sole proprietorships are all pass-through entities.

**Form 2553 (Election by a Small Business Corporation):** The IRS form a corporation or eligible LLC files to elect S-corporation tax treatment.

**Form 8832 (Entity Classification Election):** The IRS form a business entity files to choose its federal tax classification (for example, an LLC electing to be taxed as a corporation). A SMLLC that wants S-corp treatment first files Form 8832 (or is treated as a corporation via Form 2553 filing alone) and then Form 2553.

**Net earnings from self-employment:** Gross self-employment income minus allowable business deductions, multiplied by 92.35%. This is the figure on which SE tax is calculated.

**Above-the-line deduction:** A deduction taken on Form 1040 before calculating Adjusted Gross Income (AGI). Reduces taxable income regardless of whether you itemize. Examples: the SE tax deduction, health insurance premiums paid by a self-employed person, and contributions to a SEP-IRA.

**Estimated taxes:** Quarterly tax payments self-employed individuals make directly to the IRS (and usually their state) because no employer is withholding taxes from their pay. Paid using Form 1040-ES.

**Safe harbor (estimated tax):** A calculation method that protects you from the IRS underpayment penalty. If you pay at least 90% of your current-year tax liability, or 100% of last year's tax liability (110% if your prior-year AGI exceeded $150,000), no penalty applies even if you owe tax at filing.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before beginning implementation:

1. **Prior-year tax return (Form 1040 with Schedule C, if applicable):** Shows your existing income level, filing status, and any prior business structure.
2. **Year-to-date 1099-NEC forms or client payment records:** Know how much income is flowing in and from how many payers.
3. **Business expense records (current year):** Receipts and logs for software, equipment, home office, vehicle, professional development, insurance, and other ordinary and necessary expenses.
4. **State of domicile and state where services are performed:** Entity formation and tax obligations vary by state.
5. **Payer W-9 request status:** Know which clients have already issued or will issue a 1099. Identify whether they will update their records to use your entity EIN.
6. **Projected annual net income from 1099 services:** You need a reliable estimate to choose the right structure and calculate estimated tax payments.
7. **Prior-year AGI from Form 1040, Line 11:** Determines whether the 110% safe harbor applies to your estimated tax payments.
8. **Any existing business entity filings:** Articles of organization, EIN confirmation letter (IRS notice CP 575), state registration confirmation.

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect before filing:**

- All Forms 1099-NEC received from each payer (due to you by February 2, 2026, for 2025 payments)
- Bank statements for the business bank account covering the full tax year
- Credit card statements for any business card used
- Mileage log (date, destination, business purpose, miles) if claiming vehicle expenses
- Home office measurement and calculation if claiming the home office deduction
- Receipts for all business purchases over $75 (the IRS may require receipts for amounts under $75 as well; keep all records you have)
- Payroll records and Forms W-2/W-3 if you operated as an S-corp and ran payroll
- Form 2553 acceptance letter from the IRS if you elected S-corp status

**Keep after filing (retention guidance):**

- General tax records and receipts supporting deductions: **3 years** from the date you filed the return (or 2 years from when you paid the tax, whichever is later), per IRS guidance.
- Records related to property (equipment, vehicles) you depreciate: Keep for **as long as you own the property, plus 3 years after the year you dispose of it.**
- Employment tax records (payroll, Forms 941, W-2): **4 years** after the tax is due or paid, whichever is later.
- Records of unreported income exceeding 25% of gross income reported: **6 years** (the IRS has a 6-year statute of limitations in this scenario).
- Entity formation documents, EIN confirmation, Form 2553 acceptance: **Permanently.** These establish your entity's existence and tax elections.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Work through these steps in order. Do not skip to a later step before completing the earlier ones.

**Step 1: Determine your current income-reporting baseline.**
Pull last year's Schedule C (or current-year projections if this is your first year). Calculate your net self-employment income: gross 1099 receipts minus deductible business expenses. This is your starting point and the number you will compare against any new structure's tax cost.

**Step 2: Decide on your business structure.**
Work through this decision with your advisor. The two most common options for 1099 earners are:

- **Single-member LLC (disregarded entity, no corporate election):** Provides legal liability protection and separates your business finances, but does not reduce self-employment tax. Income still flows to Schedule C. Best choice when net 1099 income is relatively low or when the simplicity of no payroll is a priority.
- **LLC or corporation taxed as an S-corp:** Adds payroll requirements but allows distributions of profit beyond your reasonable salary to escape payroll tax. Best choice when net annual income is high enough that SE tax savings exceed the cost of payroll compliance. Your advisor should run a break-even comparison specific to your income level and state.

Do not proceed to form an entity or change your structure without completing this analysis with your advisor.

**Step 3: Form the entity (if not already done).**
File the required formation documents with your state (typically Articles of Organization for an LLC). Pay the state filing fee, which varies by state. Obtain your state business license or registration if your state requires one.

Action: After state approval, apply for an EIN on IRS.gov (search "EIN online application"). The online tool issues your EIN immediately. Print and save the confirmation (IRS Notice CP 575 or the online confirmation page).

**Step 4: Open a dedicated business bank account.**
Use your entity name and EIN to open an account separate from your personal finances. All 1099 income must be deposited into this account. All deductible business expenses must be paid from this account or tracked separately. Commingling personal and business funds is one of the most common audit risk factors and weakens the integrity of your deductions.

**Step 5: Update your W-9 on file with each client.**
Provide each payer with an updated Form W-9 showing:
- Entity legal name (exactly as registered with your state)
- Entity EIN (not your personal SSN)
- Tax classification (check "Limited Liability Company" and enter "S" for S-corp treatment, or the classification matching your structure)

The payer uses this information to file their 1099-NEC with the IRS in your entity's name and EIN for all 2026 payments. Do this as early in the year as possible. If a payer has already issued a 1099 in your personal SSN for the current year, consult your advisor about how to correctly attribute that income to your entity on your return.

**Step 6 (S-corp only): File Form 2553 to elect S-corporation tax treatment.**
If you and your advisor have determined an S-corp election is appropriate:
- A newly formed corporation or LLC must file Form 2553 no more than 2 months and 15 days after the beginning of the tax year (or the date the entity came into existence, whichever is later) for the election to apply to that tax year. This is the statutory standard from IRC Section 1362(b) and the Form 2553 instructions.
- For the 2026 tax year (beginning January 1, 2026): the deadline was March 16, 2026. If you missed this window, the election applies to tax year 2027. Late elections may be granted relief in certain circumstances; ask your advisor about IRS Revenue Procedure 2013-30.
- Keep the IRS acceptance letter confirming S-corp status permanently in your files.

**Step 7 (S-corp only): Establish payroll and pay yourself a reasonable salary.**
An S-corp owner who performs services for the business must receive a salary before taking distributions. Steps:

a. Work with your advisor to determine a reasonable salary for your role, based on what comparable businesses pay for similar work. Document your rationale in writing.

b. Register for payroll tax accounts with the IRS (for federal payroll taxes: EFTPS enrollment, available free at eftps.gov) and your state's tax agency.

c. Run payroll at least quarterly (many advisors recommend monthly or bi-weekly). Issue yourself a paycheck from which federal income tax, the employee half of Social Security (6.2%), and the employee half of Medicare (1.45%) are withheld.

d. The S-corp pays the employer half of Social Security (6.2%) and Medicare (1.45%) and deposits those taxes using EFTPS.

e. File Form 941 (Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return) each quarter to report payroll taxes. File Form W-2 to yourself and Form W-3 to the SSA by February 2 (for 2025 wages). For 2026 wages, the W-2 deadline will follow the standard January 31 rule (adjusted for weekends/holidays).

**Step 8: Claim all allowable business deductions on the entity's books.**
Record every ordinary and necessary business expense in your bookkeeping software or ledger. Common deductible categories include:

- Software and technology subscriptions used for the business
- Professional development, courses, certifications
- Home office (see "The Numbers" for deduction methods and limits)
- Vehicle use for business travel (see "The Numbers" for the 2026 mileage rate)
- Business meals (50% deductible; must document the business relationship and purpose)
- Professional services (legal, accounting, bookkeeping)
- Business insurance
- Equipment and tools (may be eligible for Section 179 expensing or bonus depreciation)
- Cell phone and internet (the business-use percentage is deductible)

These deductions reduce the net income on which you calculate your tax. Deductions taken through your business entity reduce income before the SE tax or S-corp salary is calculated.

**Step 9: Calculate and pay estimated taxes quarterly.**
Because 1099 income has no withholding, you must pay estimated taxes four times per year. Use Form 1040-ES to estimate your tax.

Quarterly due dates for 2026 estimated tax payments:
- Q1 (January 1 to March 31): **April 15, 2026**
- Q2 (April 1 to May 31): **June 15, 2026**
- Q3 (June 1 to August 31): **September 15, 2026**
- Q4 (September 1 to December 31): **January 15, 2027**

Pay electronically at IRS Direct Pay (directpay.irs.gov) or through your EFTPS account. Also pay state estimated taxes on the schedule your state requires.

To avoid the underpayment penalty, pay the lesser of:
- 90% of your current-year tax liability, OR
- 100% of last year's tax liability (110% if your prior-year AGI exceeded $150,000)

**Step 10: File the year-end return.**
- **Sole proprietor / disregarded SMLLC:** File Schedule C attached to your personal Form 1040. Calculate SE tax on Schedule SE.
- **S-corp:** File Form 1120-S (U.S. Income Tax Return for an S Corporation) by March 16 of the year following the tax year. Your distributive share flows to you on Schedule K-1. Report K-1 income on Form 1040 Schedule E. Report your W-2 wages on Form 1040 as wages.

**Step 11: Retain all records.**
File and store all receipts, bank statements, mileage logs, payroll records, and the filed returns per the retention periods in "Required Documents And Records."

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity type matters for SE tax relief.**
A single-member LLC that has not elected corporate treatment provides zero self-employment tax benefit. The liability protection is real, but the tax treatment is identical to operating as a sole proprietor. SE tax relief requires an S-corp election and an actual payroll.

**Reasonable compensation is not optional for S-corp owners.**
If you take distributions from your S-corp without first paying yourself a salary that reflects the market rate for your work, the IRS can and does reclassify distributions as wages, assess payroll taxes, and add penalties and interest. The IRS pays particular attention to shareholder-employees who receive Forms 1099-NEC with high income but W-2s showing little or no salary from their own corporation.

**The S-corp payroll cost offsets some tax savings.**
Running payroll means: payroll-service fees ($50-$150/month is common), employer payroll tax on your salary (7.65% of wages you pay yourself), quarterly Form 941 filings, and an annual corporate return (Form 1120-S). At low income levels these costs can exceed the SE tax savings. Your advisor should model the net benefit for your specific numbers.

**State taxes vary widely and can eliminate federal savings.**
Some states impose a separate entity-level franchise tax or minimum tax on S-corps regardless of income (California's $800 minimum franchise tax is one well-known example). Some states do not recognize the S-corp election and tax the entity as a C-corp. Check with your advisor about your specific state before electing S-corp status.

**Specified Service Trades or Businesses (SSTBs) face additional QBI limits.**
If your business provides services in health, law, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services, brokerage, or certain other professional categories, it is likely an SSTB. For SSTBs, the QBI deduction begins phasing out at $201,750 (single) / $403,500 (MFJ) and is fully eliminated once taxable income exceeds $276,750 (single) or $553,500 (MFJ). Above those upper limits, the 20% QBI deduction is zero for SSTBs. These figures are per IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32, section 4.26. This affects the overall tax math when comparing entity structures; have your advisor calculate the impact before year-end.

**1099 income already received personally before entity formation.**
The IRS generally does not allow you to retroactively assign income that was constructively received by you personally to an entity formed later. If clients paid you directly before your entity existed, that income belongs to you. Consult your advisor about how to handle income earned before and after entity formation in the same tax year.

**The 2026 1099-NEC threshold change does not reduce your reporting obligation.**
Beginning with 2026 payments, payers are not required to issue a 1099-NEC unless payments total $2,000 or more. You must still report every dollar of business income on your return, even if no 1099 is issued. The threshold change affects the payer's filing obligation, not yours.

**Multi-state income creates multi-state filing obligations.**
If you perform services in states other than your home state, you may owe income tax in those states and may be required to file a return there. Entity formation in your home state does not eliminate other states' tax claims on income sourced from activity within their borders.

**TCJA sunset is no longer a concern for QBI.**
The Section 199A QBI deduction was previously set to expire after 2025. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025) made it permanent. No sunset planning is needed for QBI beyond the income-threshold considerations above.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Mixing personal and business funds.**
Depositing 1099 income into a personal account, then trying to reconstruct which expenses were business-related at tax time, is the single most common and costly mistake. Open the business bank account first. Deposit all business income there. Pay all business expenses from there.

**2. Forgetting to update W-9 information with existing clients.**
If clients continue to issue 1099s in your personal SSN after you form an entity, it creates a reporting mismatch. Update your W-9 with every client at the start of a new engagement or when your entity changes.

**3. Paying little or no salary in an S-corp to avoid payroll tax.**
This is a well-documented IRS examination priority. "Zero salary, all distributions" will trigger a payroll-tax audit if your S-corp receives substantial 1099 income. Always document the basis for your salary determination.

**4. Missing estimated tax payments.**
1099 income has no withholding. If you wait until April to pay the full year's tax, you will owe an underpayment penalty. Set aside 25-30% of net income each month (or more for high earners) and pay quarterly using Form 1040-ES.

**5. Claiming a home office deduction without meeting the exclusive-use test.**
The home office deduction requires a space used regularly and exclusively for business. A kitchen table or shared bedroom desk does not qualify. If audited, you must be able to show the space is dedicated to the business.

**6. Deducting 100% of a dual-use expense.**
A cell phone, vehicle, or internet connection used partly for personal purposes is only partially deductible. You must determine and document the business-use percentage.

**7. Not keeping a contemporaneous mileage log.**
The IRS requires a written (or digital) record created near the time of travel, showing date, destination, business purpose, and miles. A year-end reconstruction from memory or a calendar does not satisfy this requirement.

**8. Failing to file Form 2553 on time.**
Missing the S-corp election deadline means your entity is taxed as a C-corporation or sole proprietor for that year. Late-election relief is available in some circumstances, but it is not guaranteed. File Form 2553 as soon as you decide to elect S-corp status.

**9. Assuming a SMLLC provides the same tax benefits as an S-corp.**
It does not. The SMLLC's tax treatment is identical to a sole proprietor. Forming an LLC without the S-corp election reduces legal liability but does not reduce self-employment tax.

**10. Overlooking state-level entity taxes, franchise fees, or registration requirements.**
Not every state is as simple as a federal return. Know your state's obligations before you form the entity.

## Advisor Review Gates

Before you take any of the following actions, discuss them with your advisor. These are decision points where your specific facts change the outcome.

**Gate 1: Choosing your entity structure.**
The choice between operating as a sole proprietor, a disregarded SMLLC, or an S-corp depends on your income level, state of operation, the nature of your services, and your tolerance for payroll administration. Do not form an entity or file an election based solely on general information.

**Gate 2: Filing Form 2553 for S-corp election.**
An incorrect or premature S-corp election is difficult and sometimes impossible to undo. Confirm with your advisor that the election makes financial sense for your income level, that your state recognizes the election, and that you can meet the payroll requirements.

**Gate 3: Determining your reasonable compensation as an S-corp shareholder-employee.**
Your advisor should help you document the basis for your salary (comparable positions, industry surveys, duties performed, hours worked). This documentation is your defense if the IRS questions the compensation.

**Gate 4: Claiming a home office or large vehicle deduction.**
Both are audit-sensitive. Confirm you meet the IRS requirements before claiming and that your records are complete.

**Gate 5: Receiving 1099s in your personal name after forming an entity.**
If payers have already issued or will issue 1099s in your SSN during a year when your entity is supposed to receive the income, consult your advisor before filing. The treatment on the return must be consistent and defensible.

**Gate 6: Income approaching or above the QBI phase-out thresholds.**
Once your taxable income approaches the phase-in range ($201,750 single / $403,500 MFJ for 2026 per IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32, section 4.26), your QBI deduction may be limited or eliminated entirely, especially if you are an SSTB. For SSTBs, the deduction reaches zero at $276,750 (single) / $553,500 (MFJ). Have your advisor run the QBI calculation before year-end so you can take action while there is still time.

**Gate 7: Operating in multiple states.**
Multi-state income creates multi-state compliance obligations. Do not assume your home-state entity filing covers all states where you perform work.

## Before You Act

This playbook gives you the framework and the steps. Before you form an entity, file an S-corp election, update your tax classification with clients, or make any structural change to how your income is reported, confirm your specific facts with your advisor.

The tax savings this strategy can produce are real, but they depend entirely on your income level, state of operation, the nature of your services, and how carefully the structure is implemented. A mistake in entity choice, election timing, or reasonable compensation can cost more than the taxes you were trying to save.

Your advisor should review this playbook in the context of your actual return, current income projections, and state-specific requirements before you act.
