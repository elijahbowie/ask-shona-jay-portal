# Home Office Monthly Reimbursement Cadence

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

---

## Overview

If your business is an S-corporation (or other entity that pays you a W-2 wage), you can reimburse yourself each month for the business portion of your home office costs through a written accountable plan. When done correctly, those monthly reimbursement payments are completely excluded from your taxable wages, free of payroll tax, and fully deductible by the corporation. This playbook explains how to calculate the amount, set up the monthly cadence, and keep the records that protect the deduction.

---

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is designed for:

- **S-corporation owner-employees:** You own shares in an S-corp and receive a W-2 salary from it. This is the most common setup for Beyond Freedom Financial clients and the primary focus of this playbook.
- **C-corporation owner-employees:** The same three-requirement accountable plan rules apply. The corporation deducts the reimbursement and you pay no income or payroll tax on it.
- **Multi-member LLCs taxed as a partnership that have W-2 employees:** Any W-2 employee (not a partner) of the entity can be reimbursed for home office costs under a written accountable plan.

You must already be running payroll and paying yourself (or the covered employee) W-2 wages before a home office reimbursement under an accountable plan can benefit you.

---

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Sole proprietors (Schedule C filers) and single-member LLCs taxed as disregarded entities:** You deduct home office expenses directly on Schedule C using Form 8829 (the actual expense method) or the simplified method ($5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet). An accountable plan requires an employer-employee relationship, which a sole proprietor cannot have with themselves. No plan needed -- deduct directly.
- **Partners in a partnership (for themselves):** Partners are not W-2 employees of their own partnership. Home office costs may be deductible as an Unreimbursed Partnership Expense (UPE) on Schedule E Part II only if the partnership agreement explicitly requires partners to pay these costs themselves. Consult your advisor.
- **The home office space fails the exclusive-use test:** If any part of the space is used for personal activities -- even occasionally -- the deduction is entirely disallowed. There are no partial exceptions for personal use (other than inventory storage and licensed daycare, which are outside the scope of this playbook).
- **The owner takes no W-2 salary from the S-corp:** You must be on payroll. Reimbursements cannot substitute for reasonable compensation (the wage the IRS expects an S-corp owner to pay themselves before taking profit distributions). Replacing salary with reimbursements is a red flag that can cause the IRS to reclassify reimbursements as wages with back payroll taxes and penalties.
- **States that do not conform to the federal accountable plan rules:** Several states compute income differently. California, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania, for example, have additional or differing reimbursement rules. Verify state treatment with your advisor before starting.
- **Year-end catch-up reimbursements only:** Paying one large reimbursement in December for the full year is a significant audit risk. The IRS expects a real, recurring cadence -- ideally monthly. If you cannot commit to the monthly routine, discuss an alternative approach with your advisor.

## Key Terms

**Accountable plan:** A written employer reimbursement arrangement that meets three IRS requirements (business connection, substantiation, and return of excess), causing reimbursements to be excluded from the employee's gross income, not reported on Form W-2, and exempt from payroll taxes. Governed by Treasury Regulation 1.62-2 and IRC Section 62(a)(2)(A).

**Business connection:** The expense must be an ordinary and necessary business expense (deductible under IRC Section 162) that was paid or incurred while performing services as an employee. Personal expenses do not qualify.

**Substantiation:** Providing your employer with records proving the amount, date, description, and business purpose of the expense, within a reasonable period of time after incurring it.

**Return of excess:** Any advance or reimbursement that exceeds the substantiated actual expense must be returned to the employer within a reasonable period of time.

**Nonaccountable plan:** Any reimbursement arrangement that fails one or more of the three accountable plan requirements. All payments under a nonaccountable plan are treated as taxable wages, reported on Form W-2, and subject to federal income tax withholding, FICA, and FUTA.

**FICA:** Federal Insurance Contributions Act taxes -- the combined Social Security tax (6.2% employee plus 6.2% employer, on wages up to the annual wage base) and Medicare tax (1.45% employee plus 1.45% employer, with no wage base cap). Proper accountable plan reimbursements are exempt from both the employee and employer halves of FICA.

**FUTA:** Federal Unemployment Tax Act tax, paid by the employer only (0.6% after the standard state credit, on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages per year). Accountable plan reimbursements are FUTA-exempt.

**W-2 (Form W-2, Wage and Tax Statement):** The annual form an employer gives each employee showing wages paid and taxes withheld. Proper accountable plan reimbursements are not reported as wages in Box 1.

**IRC Section 280A:** The Internal Revenue Code section that establishes the home office deduction rules, including the exclusive-use and regular-use requirements and the tests a space must pass to qualify (principal place of business, client meeting place, or separate structure).

**Exclusive use:** The home office area must be used only for business. Any personal use of the same area -- even a couch used for TV viewing in a corner of the office -- defeats the exclusivity requirement and disqualifies the entire space.

**Regular use:** The office must be used on a consistent, ongoing basis for business, not merely occasionally or incidentally.

**Principal place of business:** Under IRC Section 280A(c)(1)(A), your home qualifies if it is the primary location where you conduct your most important business activities or where you perform substantial administrative and management activities and have no other fixed location for those activities.

**Business use percentage:** The fraction of your home used for business, calculated as: office square footage divided by total home square footage. This percentage is applied to indirect home expenses to determine the reimbursable amount.

**Direct expenses:** Costs that benefit only the home office space (for example, painting only the office, or a dedicated office-only phone line). These are 100% reimbursable.

**Indirect expenses:** Costs that benefit the entire home (utilities, homeowners insurance, mortgage interest, property taxes, repairs to the whole house). Only the business use percentage of each indirect expense is reimbursable.

**Depreciation (home):** A non-cash deduction representing the gradual use and wear of the residential structure (not the land). For home office purposes, depreciation is calculated on the business-use percentage of the home's cost basis (excluding land value), using the straight-line method over 39 years (residential rental property rules do not apply here; the IRS uses the 39-year period for home office depreciation). Note: including depreciation in the reimbursement creates a depreciation recapture obligation when the home is sold (see Caveats below). Consult your advisor before including depreciation.

**Straight-line depreciation:** A method that spreads the depreciable cost of property equally over its useful life, producing the same deduction amount each year.

**Treasury Regulation 1.62-2:** The IRS regulation that defines the three accountable plan requirements in detail and specifies the IRS timing safe harbors.

**Safe harbor (timing):** IRS-specified windows that automatically satisfy the "reasonable period of time" requirement: (1) substantiate expenses within 60 days after paying or incurring them; (2) return any excess reimbursement within 120 days after paying or incurring the expense. Alternatively, if the employer issues a periodic statement at least quarterly listing unsubstantiated amounts and requests return or substantiation within 120 days, that satisfies the requirement.

**Form 1120-S:** The S-corporation annual income tax return. Home office reimbursements paid under a valid accountable plan are reported as a deductible business expense on Form 1120-S, typically on the "Other Deductions" line.

**Corporate resolution:** A formal written record of a decision made by a corporation's board of directors (even if there is only one director, who is also the sole shareholder). Adopting an accountable plan should be recorded in a corporate resolution and kept with corporate records.

**Depreciation recapture:** The IRS rule under IRC Section 1250 requiring you to recognize gain (as ordinary income subject to a special maximum rate) when you sell property for which you previously claimed depreciation deductions. For home office depreciation taken after May 6, 1997, that gain is "unrecaptured Section 1250 gain" and is subject to a maximum federal tax rate of 25%, taxed on Form 4797.

**Schedule A (Form 1040):** The individual tax form for itemized deductions, including mortgage interest and real estate taxes. If those expenses are reimbursed through the accountable plan, the reimbursed portion must be excluded from Schedule A to avoid a double deduction.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before computing your monthly reimbursement:

1. **Total square footage of your home.** Measure all finished living space. Keep the measurement and a simple floor plan sketch.
2. **Square footage of the home office only.** Measure the dedicated office space. The area does not need to be walled off, but it must be clearly identifiable and used exclusively for business.
3. **Monthly amounts for each indirect home expense:**
   - Mortgage interest (from your monthly lender statement)
   - Real estate/property taxes (monthly accrual: annual bill divided by 12)
   - Homeowners or renters insurance premium (monthly accrual)
   - Utilities: electricity, gas, water (monthly bills)
   - Internet service (the portion not already reimbursed separately)
   - Trash collection (monthly or quarterly bill converted to a monthly amount)
   - General repairs and maintenance costs that benefit the whole home
   - HOA (homeowners association) dues if applicable
4. **Cost basis of your home (structure only, not land) and the date you placed the home office in service**, if you plan to include depreciation. Your advisor will help you compute the annual depreciation amount and convert it to a monthly figure.
5. **A copy of your written accountable plan document.** If one does not exist yet, you must create it before the first reimbursement. See Step 1 in the implementation steps below.
6. **The entity type and tax classification of your business.** Confirm that your entity pays you a W-2 wage.

## Required Documents And Records

**Documents to create and keep permanently (as long as the plan is active):**

- Written accountable plan document, signed and dated, kept with corporate records.
- Corporate resolution adopting the accountable plan (for corporations). For single-shareholder S-corps, a written statement or resolution by the sole director is sufficient.

**Documents to create and keep each month:**

- Monthly expense report submitted to the corporation listing: the period covered, each expense category, the total indirect home expenses, the business use percentage, and the reimbursable amount.
- Receipts or statements supporting each line item (utility bills, mortgage statement, insurance statement, property tax bill). PDFs stored in a dedicated folder organized by month and year are acceptable.
- Proof of payment from the corporation to you (bank statement or check image showing the transfer from the corporate account to your personal account).

**Retention guidance:**

- Keep all home office reimbursement records for at least 3 years after the filing date (or due date, whichever is later) of the tax return on which the deduction is claimed. This is the standard IRS statute of limitations for audits.
- If your return omits more than 25% of gross income, the statute extends to 6 years.
- Employment tax records (including payroll records that support your W-2) must be kept for at least 4 years after the date the tax becomes due or is paid, whichever is later.
- Keep records related to the home itself (purchase price, improvements, depreciation schedules) for as long as you own the property plus the applicable statute of limitations period after you sell it, because those records determine your cost basis and any depreciation recapture calculation.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Step 1: Adopt a Written Accountable Plan (one-time setup)

**What to do:** Draft a short written document establishing the accountable plan. It must state: (a) that the corporation will reimburse employees for ordinary and necessary business expenses incurred on behalf of the corporation; (b) that employees must submit substantiation (receipts and an expense report) within a reasonable time; (c) that any excess reimbursement must be returned within a reasonable time; and (d) the specific categories of eligible expenses (home office is one category).

**Input:** A description of your business and the expense categories you expect to reimburse (home office, mileage, cell phone, etc.).

**Output:** A signed, dated written accountable plan document. For an S-corp, pass a corporate resolution adopting the plan. Keep both documents with your corporate minute book.

**Verify before proceeding:** The plan must exist in writing before the first reimbursement is issued. Retroactive plans are not valid.

### Step 2: Measure and Document Your Home Office Space

**What to do:** Measure the square footage of your home office and the total finished square footage of your home. Divide office square footage by total home square footage to get your business use percentage.

**Example:** Office is 200 square feet. Home is 2,000 square feet total. Business use percentage = 200 / 2,000 = 10%.

**Input:** A tape measure and a simple sketch or floor plan.

**Output:** A written note recording the two measurements, the calculated percentage, the date measured, and your signature. Attach a floor plan sketch if possible. Keep this with corporate records.

**Verify before proceeding:** The office must be used exclusively and regularly for business. Walk through the space and confirm there is no personal furniture, TV, guest sleeping area, or other personal use that would disqualify it.

### Step 3: Identify and Categorize Your Monthly Home Expenses

**What to do:** List every recurring indirect home expense. For expenses billed annually or quarterly (like property taxes or insurance), divide by 12 to get a monthly accrual. For expenses that vary month to month (like utilities), use the actual bill amount for each month.

**Input:** One month of bills and statements for each expense category.

**Output:** A running spreadsheet or table (the Attached Worksheet supports this) with columns for: expense category, monthly amount, business use percentage applied, and reimbursable amount.

**Note on mortgage interest and property taxes:** If you itemize deductions on Schedule A of your personal return, you must reduce your Schedule A deduction for mortgage interest and real estate taxes by the amount reimbursed through the accountable plan. You cannot deduct the same dollar on both your personal return and through the corporation. Consult your advisor when preparing your personal return.

### Step 4: Decide Whether to Include Depreciation

**What to do:** Discuss with your advisor whether to include the business-use percentage of home depreciation in the monthly reimbursement.

**Why it matters:** Depreciation increases the monthly reimbursement amount and the corporation's deduction. However, depreciation claimed on the home office creates an "unrecaptured Section 1250 gain" obligation when you sell the home. That recaptured gain is taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25% and cannot be sheltered by the IRC Section 121 home-sale exclusion (the exclusion that allows most homeowners to exclude up to $250,000/$500,000 of gain on a principal residence sale). The trade-off between current deductions and future recapture is fact-specific. Do not include depreciation in the reimbursement without your advisor's input.

**Input:** Your home's cost basis (purchase price plus capital improvements, excluding land value), the date the office was first placed in service, and the current depreciation schedule if one already exists.

**Output:** An annual depreciation amount for the business-use portion of the home, divided by 12 for the monthly figure. Your advisor should prepare or confirm this calculation.

### Step 5: Prepare the Monthly Expense Report

**What to do:** At the end of each calendar month (or at the beginning of the next month), complete the Monthly Expense Report section of the attached worksheet. List every indirect expense for that month, apply the business use percentage to each, total the reimbursable amount, and sign the report.

**Input:** That month's utility bills, mortgage statement, insurance and property tax accruals, and any direct office expenses.

**Output:** A completed, signed monthly expense report with supporting receipts attached (PDFs are fine). The report should show:
- Reporting period (e.g., May 1 -- May 31, 2026)
- Your name and the corporation's name
- Each expense category, the total amount, the business use percentage, and the reimbursable amount
- Total reimbursement requested
- Your signature and date

### Step 6: Submit the Expense Report to the Corporation

**What to do:** Submit the signed expense report and attached receipts to the corporation. For a single-owner S-corp, this means filing it in the corporate records folder (digital or physical) and noting the submission date.

**Timing:** You must submit (substantiate) within 60 days after the end of the month in which the expenses were incurred to remain within the IRS safe harbor. Submitting monthly, within 30 days of month end, keeps you comfortably within the window.

**Output:** A dated copy of the submitted report, stored in the corporation's records.

### Step 7: The Corporation Issues the Reimbursement Payment

**What to do:** The corporation pays you the reimbursement amount from the corporate bank account. This is a separate transfer from your payroll direct deposit.

**Input:** The approved monthly expense report total.

**Output:** A check or bank transfer from the corporate account to your personal account, dated the same month or the following month (staying within the 60-day substantiation window). The bank statement showing the transfer is your proof of payment. Label the memo line "Home Office Reimbursement -- [Month/Year]."

**Critical:** The payment must come from the corporate account. Paying yourself from a commingled personal-business account eliminates the paper trail and blurs the corporate structure.

### Step 8: Record the Reimbursement in the Corporation's Books

**What to do:** Enter the reimbursement payment in the corporation's accounting records as a business expense (commonly labeled "Home Office Expense" or "Employee Reimbursement -- Home Office").

**Input:** The dollar amount and the supporting expense report.

**Output:** A bookkeeping entry in the corporate books reducing income by the reimbursement amount. This expense flows to the "Other Deductions" line on Form 1120-S (the S-corporation income tax return) when your accountant prepares the return.

### Step 9: File the Record Package Each Month

**What to do:** Place the completed expense report, all receipts, and the bank statement showing payment into a folder organized by month and year.

**Suggested folder structure:**
```
Home Office Reimbursements /
  2026 /
    01 - January /
      Expense Report - January 2026.pdf
      Utility Bill - January 2026.pdf
      Mortgage Statement - January 2026.pdf
      [other receipts]
      Bank Transfer Confirmation - January 2026.pdf
    02 - February /
      ...
```

### Step 10: Annual Year-End Review

**What to do:** In December (or when preparing corporate tax documents in January/February), confirm that all 12 months of expense reports are complete and filed, that total reimbursements match the amount recorded in the corporate books, and that the business use percentage is still accurate (if you moved, renovated, or changed the office space during the year, recalculate).

**Input:** The full year's monthly expense reports and corporate bookkeeping records.

**Output:** A year-end summary reconciling total reimbursements paid versus total amounts recorded in the books, plus a note confirming the business use percentage used. Provide this summary to your accountant when they prepare Form 1120-S.

## Timing And Deadlines

| Event | Deadline / Cadence |
|---|---|
| Complete monthly expense report | By the last day of the month or within 5 business days of month end |
| Submit expense report to corporation (substantiation) | Within 60 days of incurring the expenses (IRS safe harbor under Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g)) |
| Corporation issues reimbursement payment | Promptly after receiving the substantiated report; ideally within the same calendar month |
| Return any over-reimbursement to corporation | Within 120 days of incurring the expense (IRS safe harbor under Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g)) |
| Year-end reconciliation | By January 31 of the following year (before corporate books are closed for tax preparation) |
| Corporate tax return (Form 1120-S) due date | March 15 of the year following the tax year; a 6-month extension (to September 15) is available |
| Record retention begins | From the later of the return filing date or the return due date |

**Why monthly matters:** The IRS views year-end reimbursements as a sign the arrangement is being reverse-engineered from tax returns rather than reflecting a genuine, standing business obligation. Monthly reimbursements demonstrate the plan operates as a real, recurring business expense policy throughout the year.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

### Entity-specific notes

**S-corporations:** This is the primary use case. The owner-employee submits expense reports to the corporation, and the corporation reimburses from the corporate account. The reimbursement is deducted on Form 1120-S. The shareholder's W-2 does not include the reimbursement. There is no income or FICA tax to the shareholder on the reimbursement.

**C-corporations:** The same three accountable plan rules apply. The deduction is taken on Form 1120 rather than Form 1120-S.

**Partnerships (for employees, not partners):** Partners who are not employees cannot receive accountable plan reimbursements for themselves under the same rules. If you are a partner with a home office, consult your advisor about the Unreimbursed Partnership Expense (UPE) treatment on Schedule E.

**Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (disregarded entities):** Do not use an accountable plan for the owner. Deduct home office costs on Schedule C using Form 8829 (actual expenses) or the simplified method. There is no employer-employee relationship.

### W-2 employees (non-owner) reminder

Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed July 4, 2025, the suspension of miscellaneous itemized deductions (including unreimbursed employee business expenses) that was enacted under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act was made permanent. As of tax year 2026 and going forward, ordinary W-2 employees cannot deduct home office expenses on their personal returns. The accountable plan is now the only federal mechanism for an employee to receive tax benefit for home office costs.

### Depreciation recapture on home sale

If you include depreciation in the reimbursement, you must track all depreciation claimed on the home office. When you sell the home, the cumulative depreciation becomes "unrecaptured Section 1250 gain," taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25%, reported on Form 4797. This gain is not sheltered by the IRC Section 121 principal-residence exclusion (the rule allowing most homeowners to exclude up to $250,000 of gain if single, or $500,000 if married filing jointly, from a primary home sale). Many advisors recommend excluding depreciation from the monthly reimbursement specifically to avoid this future recapture obligation. Discuss this trade-off with your advisor before including depreciation.

### Mortgage interest and property tax double-deduction prohibition

If you receive reimbursement for the business-use portion of mortgage interest and property taxes, you cannot also deduct those same amounts on Schedule A of your personal Form 1040. When your personal return is prepared, the amounts reimbursed through the accountable plan must be subtracted from the Schedule A entries for mortgage interest and real estate taxes. Failure to adjust creates a disallowed double deduction and triggers a correction if discovered on audit.

### State conformity

State income tax treatment of accountable plan reimbursements varies. Most states follow the federal rules, but some do not. Notable examples:

- **California:** Generally conforms to federal accountable plan rules for income tax exclusion purposes, but California Labor Code Section 2802 independently requires employers to reimburse employees for all necessary business expenditures, regardless of whether a formal accountable plan exists. This creates an additional mandatory reimbursement obligation for California-based employees.
- **Other states:** [confirm your state's conformity with your advisor -- could not verify all 50 states against a public source for 2026]

### Current-year caveats

The simplified method ($5 per square foot, 300 sq ft maximum) is available only to sole proprietors and Schedule C filers. S-corp shareholders reimbursed under an accountable plan must use the actual expense method (percentage of real costs), not the simplified method. The simplified method is a Schedule C mechanism, not an accountable plan mechanism.

## Common Mistakes

1. **No written plan.** Paying reimbursements without a written accountable plan document in place before the first payment converts every reimbursement to taxable wages. Create the document first.

2. **Personal use in the office.** A guest bedroom that doubles as a home office, a couch used for personal TV viewing, or personal files stored in the space all defeat the exclusive-use test. The entire deduction is lost, not just the personal-use portion.

3. **One annual payment in December.** A single year-end reimbursement is a red flag that the arrangement is not a genuine, standing business policy. The IRS expects a consistent monthly or quarterly cadence.

4. **Using the simplified method for S-corp reimbursements.** The $5-per-square-foot simplified method is a Schedule C tool. S-corp accountable plan reimbursements must use actual expenses multiplied by the business use percentage.

5. **Not reducing Schedule A for reimbursed mortgage interest and property taxes.** If you itemize, the reimbursed portion of mortgage interest and real estate taxes must come off Schedule A. Overlooking this creates a double deduction that the IRS can and does catch.

6. **Mixing personal and business bank accounts.** The reimbursement must flow from the corporate account to your personal account. A transfer from a commingled account undermines the corporate structure and eliminates the paper trail.

7. **Forgetting to file receipts contemporaneously.** Reconstructing receipts months later for an audit is difficult and unreliable. File each month's receipts at the same time you prepare the expense report.

8. **Claiming depreciation without tracking it.** If you include depreciation in the reimbursement and later sell the home, uncalculated or untracked depreciation creates errors on Form 4797 and a potential understatement of gain.

9. **Not adjusting when the home changes.** If you move, renovate to expand or shrink the office, or begin using the office for personal purposes, you must recalculate the business use percentage for the months following the change. Using a stale percentage after a change is inaccurate.

10. **Omitting the corporate resolution.** For an S-corp or C-corp, the accountable plan should be adopted by a formal corporate resolution kept in the minute book. Without this, the plan's existence can be harder to prove.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and confirm with your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

1. **Before issuing the first reimbursement:** Your advisor should review the written accountable plan, the business use percentage calculation, the list of expense categories included, and whether depreciation will be included. A one-time setup review prevents systematic errors that repeat for years.

2. **If you are considering including home depreciation in the reimbursement:** The depreciation recapture trade-off is fact-specific and depends on your home's value, your income tax bracket, and your expected holding period. Do not include depreciation without this analysis.

3. **If you work in California, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, or any other state that may not conform to federal accountable plan treatment:** Verify state income tax treatment before filing.

4. **If the IRS issues a notice or inquiry about your reimbursements:** Do not respond without your advisor's involvement. An accountable plan audit requires presenting organized records in a specific sequence.

5. **If your business entity type changes** (for example, if you convert from a sole proprietorship to an S-corp, or dissolve the corporation): The accountable plan treatment changes with the entity. Review the treatment after any entity conversion.

6. **If you sell your home:** Calculate the depreciation recapture obligation before closing. The recapture amount affects your net sale proceeds and must be reported on Form 4797.

7. **If you begin using the home office for personal purposes, or if the office space changes materially:** Recalculate the business use percentage and document the change. Confirm whether the exclusive-use test is still satisfied.

## Before You Act

This playbook teaches a real, IRS-recognized strategy. However, the details of your specific situation -- the square footage of your space, your entity type, your state of residence, whether you include depreciation, how the deduction interacts with your Schedule A, and whether the exclusive-use test is genuinely met -- must be confirmed with your advisor before you issue your first reimbursement. A single misconfigured element (no written plan, personal use in the office, payment from the wrong account, or failure to adjust Schedule A) can convert the entire arrangement into taxable wages. Review this playbook with your advisor, get the written plan in place, and then begin the monthly cadence.
