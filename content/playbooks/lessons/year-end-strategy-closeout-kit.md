# Year-End Strategy Closeout Kit

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Every tax strategy you have deployed during the year -- retirement contributions, accountable plan reimbursements, vehicle deductions, entity elections, and more -- requires a specific set of close-out actions before December 31. Missing a deadline or skipping a required document can convert a valid deduction into an audit exposure. This playbook walks you through a systematic, end-of-year sweep that confirms every open strategy is properly funded, documented, and closed before the calendar flips to January 1, 2027. It pairs directly with the attached Year-End Closeout Checklist, which you work through one section at a time alongside these instructions.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook applies to any business owner who filed (or plans to file) as a sole proprietor, single-member LLC (taxed as a disregarded entity or S-corporation), partnership, or S-corporation for tax year 2026 and who has one or more active tax strategies in place -- such as retirement plan contributions, an accountable reimbursement plan, a Section 179 or bonus depreciation purchase, an HSA (Health Savings Account), or an S-corporation salary arrangement.

It is especially important if you:

- Have not yet made your full retirement plan contribution for 2026
- Have unreimbursed business expenses sitting in your personal records
- Purchased business equipment or a vehicle late in the year
- Received distributions from your S-corporation or LLC throughout the year
- Are self-employed and making quarterly estimated tax payments
- Used your personal home for business meetings under the Augusta Rule (IRC Section 280A(g))
- Have a SIMPLE IRA that requires employer matching by year-end

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **W-2 employees with no side business:** If your only income is from an employer who handles all withholding, payroll, and benefits, most of the strategies this kit addresses do not apply to you.
- **Businesses with a non-calendar fiscal year:** All dates in this playbook assume your tax year ends December 31, 2026. If your entity uses a fiscal year ending on a different date, recalculate every deadline accordingly.
- **Strategies not yet put in place during 2026:** You cannot retroactively adopt a strategy that required action during the year (for example, you cannot claim the Augusta Rule for a meeting you did not actually hold). If a strategy is not active, skip that section of the checklist.
- **Taxpayers with exclusively passive income or investment-only portfolios:** While some items (estimated tax, IRA contributions) may apply, the business-entity sections require active business income.

## Key Terms

**Tax year 2026:** The 12-month period from January 1 through December 31, 2026, for which you will file a return (Form 1040, 1120-S, 1065, or 1120) in 2027.

**Calendar year entity:** A business or individual whose tax year runs January 1 through December 31. Most small businesses are calendar year entities by default.

**Elective deferral:** The portion of your salary or self-employment income that you choose to contribute to a qualified retirement plan such as a 401(k) or SIMPLE IRA before taxes are applied. Subject to annual IRS limits.

**Employer profit-sharing contribution:** A contribution made by the business (not the employee) to a qualified retirement plan such as a SEP-IRA or the employer side of a Solo 401(k). Often deductible on the business return.

**SEP-IRA (Simplified Employee Pension Individual Retirement Account):** A retirement plan that any self-employed person or small business can establish, funded entirely by employer contributions up to 25% of compensation (capped at $72,000 for 2026). Established using IRS Form 5305-SEP.

**Solo 401(k) (also called an Individual 401(k) or one-participant 401(k)):** A retirement plan for self-employed individuals with no common-law employees other than a spouse. Allows both an employee elective deferral (up to $24,500 for 2026) and an employer profit-sharing contribution (up to 25% of compensation), with a combined maximum of $72,000 for 2026.

**SIMPLE IRA (Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees):** A retirement plan available to employers with 100 or fewer employees, allowing both employee salary deferrals and required employer matching or non-elective contributions. Must be established by October 1 to be effective for a calendar year.

**HSA (Health Savings Account):** A tax-advantaged savings account you can use to pay qualified medical expenses. Available only if you are enrolled in a qualifying high-deductible health plan (HDHP). Contributions are deductible, earnings grow tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free -- a triple tax benefit.

**HDHP (High-Deductible Health Plan):** A health insurance plan with a minimum annual deductible required by the IRS to qualify for HSA contributions. For 2026, the minimum deductible is $1,700 for self-only coverage and $3,400 for family coverage.

**Accountable plan:** An employer-adopted reimbursement arrangement that meets three IRS requirements: (1) expenses have a genuine business connection, (2) the employee substantiates the expense with documentation within 60 days of incurring it, and (3) any excess reimbursement is returned within a reasonable time. Reimbursements under a qualifying accountable plan are tax-free to the employee and deductible to the business, with no payroll tax owed on either side. Governed by Treasury Regulation 1.62-2.

**Section 179 expensing:** An election under Internal Revenue Code Section 179 that allows a business to deduct the full cost of qualifying equipment or software in the year it is placed in service rather than depreciating it over multiple years. For 2026, the maximum deduction is $2,560,000, with a phase-out beginning when total qualifying purchases exceed $4,090,000. (IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 §4.24; IRS Pub. 946.)

**Bonus depreciation (first-year expensing):** A tax deduction under IRC Section 168(k) that allows businesses to deduct a large percentage of the cost of qualifying property in the year it is placed in service. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025) permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for eligible property acquired after January 19, 2025, including property placed in service in 2026.

**QBI deduction (Section 199A deduction):** A deduction of up to 20% of qualified business income (the net profit of a pass-through business such as a sole proprietorship, S-corporation, or partnership) allowed on the owner's individual return. Made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. For 2026, the deduction is unrestricted below $403,500 of taxable income (married filing jointly) or $201,750 (all other filers). Described more fully in "The Numbers" section.

**Pass-through entity:** A business structure -- such as a sole proprietorship, partnership, S-corporation, or LLC taxed as one of those -- where income and loss flow through to the owner's personal return rather than being taxed at the entity level.

**Reasonable compensation:** For S-corporation shareholder-employees who perform services for the corporation, the IRS requires the shareholder to receive a salary (reported on Form W-2) that is comparable to what another employer would pay for the same services. Profit distributions taken above that salary are not subject to payroll tax, but the salary itself must be reasonable or the IRS may reclassify distributions as wages. Governed by IRC Section 3121.

**FICA taxes (Federal Insurance Contributions Act taxes):** Payroll taxes consisting of Social Security tax (6.2% employer plus 6.2% employee, on wages up to the $184,500 Social Security wage base for 2026) and Medicare tax (1.45% employer plus 1.45% employee, on all wages with no cap). Self-employed individuals pay a combined self-employment tax of 15.3% that covers both sides.

**Augusta Rule (IRC Section 280A(g)):** A provision of the tax code that allows a homeowner to rent their personal residence to their own business for up to 14 days per year and exclude all of that rental income from federal taxable income. The business deducts the rent it pays. Detailed in a separate playbook; referenced here for the year-end close-out step only.

**Estimated tax safe harbor:** A specific payment threshold set by the IRS that, if met, guarantees no underpayment penalty regardless of the final tax bill. Two tests apply: (1) pay at least 90% of your current-year tax liability, or (2) pay 100% of your prior-year tax liability (110% if your prior-year adjusted gross income exceeded $150,000).

**W-2 wages (for QBI purposes):** Total wages paid by the business to employees and reported on Form W-2, including owner-officer compensation in an S-corporation. Above the QBI income phase-in threshold, your QBI deduction is capped at 50% of W-2 wages paid.

**SALT deduction:** The itemized deduction for state and local taxes (income taxes or sales taxes, plus property taxes) claimed on Schedule A of Form 1040. For 2026, the cap is $40,400 (increased from $10,000 by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act), subject to a phase-out for modified adjusted gross income above $505,000.

**Distribution:** A payment from an S-corporation or partnership to a shareholder or partner that is not salary. Distributions are generally not subject to payroll taxes, but they are not deductible to the entity.

**Basis:** Your after-tax investment in an asset or entity. In an S-corporation or partnership context, basis determines how much loss you can deduct and how much of a distribution is tax-free. Tracking basis is essential and is covered in a separate playbook.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather all of the following before working through the Closeout Checklist. Missing inputs will leave gaps in the checklist.

1. **Year-to-date profit and loss (P&L) statement** for each business entity you own, updated through the most recent month-end. Your bookkeeper or accounting software should generate this in minutes.
2. **Your 2025 federal tax return (Form 1040)** and the returns for each entity (1120-S, 1065, or Schedule C). You need: prior-year total tax (line 24 of Form 1040), prior-year adjusted gross income (line 11 of Form 1040), and prior-year W-2 wages paid by each entity.
3. **Retirement plan account statements** for any plan you currently contribute to: 401(k), SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, IRA. Shows balances and contributions made so far in 2026.
4. **Payroll records (Form W-2 drafts or year-to-date payroll reports):** Shows your W-2 wages paid by each entity through the current date, and the projected year-end W-2 total.
5. **Accountable plan reimbursement log:** Your running record of business expenses incurred personally that have not yet been submitted for reimbursement.
6. **Equipment, vehicle, and asset purchase records:** Invoices and placed-in-service dates for any qualifying property purchased in 2026 for which you want to claim Section 179 or bonus depreciation.
7. **HSA account statement:** Shows current 2026 contributions and confirms your HDHP enrollment dates.
8. **Augusta Rule documentation (if applicable):** Written rental agreement, comparable market rate support (at least three written rate quotes from comparable venues), and cancelled check or bank transfer showing payment.
9. **Estimated tax payment history:** Dates and amounts of all quarterly payments made in 2026 (you can verify at IRS.gov under "View Your Account").
10. **Business credit card and bank statements** for October and November 2026 to catch any late-year deductible expenses not yet entered in bookkeeping.

## Required Documents And Records

The following records must exist -- not just be planned -- before December 31, 2026, for the deduction or strategy to hold up to IRS scrutiny. Keep all records for at least seven years from the return due date (including extensions), which is consistent with the period during which the IRS can assess additional tax for substantial understatement of income.

| Record | What It Must Show | Retention |
|---|---|---|
| Retirement plan contribution receipt or account statement | Amount, date, and plan type (SEP-IRA, 401(k), SIMPLE IRA) | 7 years |
| Solo 401(k) plan adoption agreement (if establishing a new plan in 2026) | Signed plan document and effective date | Life of plan + 7 years |
| Accountable plan policy document | Written employer policy meeting all three IRS requirements | Life of plan + 7 years |
| Expense reports with receipts | Amount, date, place, business purpose, and who was present (for meals) | 7 years |
| Augusta Rule rental agreement and payment record | Rental dates, rate, payment method; rate support from comparable venues | 7 years |
| Equipment/vehicle purchase invoices and placed-in-service documentation | Date purchased, date placed in service, cost, business use percentage | Life of asset + 7 years |
| Vehicle mileage log | Date, destination, business purpose, odometer start and end for each trip | 7 years |
| HSA contribution records and HDHP enrollment confirmation | Contribution date and amount; insurance card or EOB confirming HDHP | 7 years |
| S-corporation W-2 and payroll tax returns (Forms 941, 940) | Wages paid each quarter, payroll taxes withheld and deposited | 7 years |
| Year-end balance sheet | Ending balances on all asset, liability, and equity accounts | 7 years |
| Minutes or written resolutions (for corporations) | Any year-end decisions: bonus, salary change, distribution amount | Life of entity + 7 years |

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Work through each numbered step in order. Steps 1 through 4 must be completed before December 31, 2026. Steps 5 through 9 have later deadlines but depend on information gathered in the earlier steps.

---

**Step 1: Pull your year-to-date financials and project year-end income**

1a. Log in to your accounting software (QuickBooks, Wave, FreshBooks, or similar) and generate a profit and loss statement from January 1, 2026, through the most recent month-end. Print or save as a PDF.

1b. Project your December income and expenses based on what you expect to collect and pay. Add the projected December figures to the year-to-date totals to estimate your full-year net profit for each entity.

1c. If you own an S-corporation, separate your W-2 wage income from your projected year-end pass-through profit. Your W-2 wages are already set; the remaining profit is what will flow through to your Form 1040 as S-corp income.

1d. If you own a partnership or multi-member LLC, use your projected K-1 (Schedule K-1, Form 1065) allocable share of income as your pass-through amount.

1e. Write your projected figures on Section 1 of the attached Year-End Closeout Checklist.

*Output: A single-page projected P&L for each entity with a clear "estimated year-end net profit" figure.*

---

**Step 2: Confirm your estimated tax payments are on track**

2a. Log in to your IRS online account at irs.gov/account to confirm the four quarterly payments you have already made for 2026 are recorded. (The four 2026 due dates are April 15, 2026; June 15, 2026; September 15, 2026; and January 15, 2027 for the fourth quarter.)

2b. From your 2025 Form 1040, find line 24 (total tax). This is your prior-year tax liability for safe harbor purposes.

2c. If your 2025 adjusted gross income (Form 1040, line 11) was $150,000 or less, you need to have paid (or withheld) at least 100% of your 2025 tax liability by the end of the year to avoid an underpayment penalty. If your 2025 AGI exceeded $150,000, the threshold rises to 110% of your 2025 tax liability.

2d. Alternatively, calculate 90% of your projected 2026 tax liability. If your prepayments (withholding plus estimated payments) will meet either the 90% current-year test or the 100%/110% prior-year safe harbor, you are protected from the underpayment penalty.

2e. If you are short, consider whether to: (i) increase December withholding if you have a W-2 job (withholding is treated as paid evenly throughout the year, which can retroactively cure Q1-Q3 shortfalls), or (ii) make an additional estimated payment before January 15, 2027.

2f. Mark Section 2 of the Closeout Checklist complete with the payment amounts confirmed.

*Output: Confirmation that total 2026 prepayments meet at least one safe harbor test, or a specific dollar amount to pay by January 15, 2027.*

---

**Step 3: Close out your retirement plan contributions**

This step differs by plan type. Identify your plan type(s) and follow the relevant sub-steps.

**If you have a SIMPLE IRA:**

3a. SIMPLE IRA employee deferrals must be deposited within 30 days after the end of the month in which the amounts were withheld. For December 2026 deferrals, this means they must be deposited by January 30, 2027.

3b. The employer matching contribution (typically 3% of compensation) must be deposited by the employer's tax return due date, including extensions (April 15, 2027 for sole proprietors; March 15, 2027 for S-corporations and partnerships, or September 15, 2027 with an extension).

3c. Verify that your total 2026 employee deferrals do not exceed the applicable limit. For standard SIMPLE IRA plans (larger employers not offering the enhanced contribution), the employee deferral limit is $17,000 ($21,000 if you are age 50 through 59 or age 64 or older; $22,250 if you are age 60 through 63). For enhanced SIMPLE IRA plans (employers with 25 or fewer employees, or larger employers who make the higher company contribution), the employee deferral limit is $18,100 ($21,950 if you are age 50 through 59 or age 64 or older, using the $3,850 applicable-plan catch-up; $23,350 if you are age 60 through 63). Confirm which limit applies to your plan with your plan administrator. Note: the age-50 catch-up differs between plan types ($4,000 for standard plans vs. $3,850 for applicable/enhanced plans) due to a quirk in how the tax code indexes these limits -- confirm your specific catch-up amount with your plan administrator.

3d. Check the SIMPLE IRA establishment date. A SIMPLE IRA must have been established on or before October 1, 2026, to be effective for 2026. If yours was established after October 1, it will not be valid until 2027.

**If you have a SEP-IRA:**

3e. SEP-IRA contributions do not have to be made by December 31. The deadline is your business tax return due date, including extensions. For a sole proprietor filing Schedule C, that is April 15, 2027, or October 15, 2027 if you file Form 4868 for an extension.

3f. The maximum contribution for 2026 is the lesser of 25% of your net self-employment earnings (after the deduction for half of self-employment tax) or $72,000. The compensation used in the calculation cannot exceed $360,000.

3g. If you do not have a SEP-IRA yet, you can still establish one and fund it up to your tax return due date (including extensions).

**If you have a Solo 401(k) (one-participant 401(k)):**

3h. The employee elective deferral portion (up to $24,500; $32,500 if age 50 through 59 or age 64 or older; $35,750 if age 60 through 63) must generally be elected before December 31, 2026, if your plan was established in a prior year.

3i. Under SECURE 2.0, sole proprietors who file Schedule C and have no common-law employees may adopt a new Solo 401(k) plan and make the elective deferral election as late as their personal return due date (April 15, 2027, without extension). Confirm whether this late-adoption exception applies to your situation with your advisor.

3j. The employer profit-sharing contribution (up to 25% of W-2 compensation for S-corp owners, or a modified calculation for self-employed individuals, up to the overall $72,000 combined limit) can be made up to your tax return due date, including extensions.

3k. If the combined contributions across all of your retirement plans could reach or exceed $72,000, work through the calculation with your advisor before making additional contributions to avoid excess contribution penalties.

**If you have a Traditional IRA or Roth IRA:**

3l. Traditional and Roth IRA contributions for 2026 are not required by December 31. The deadline is April 15, 2027 (extensions do not extend the IRA contribution deadline). However, making the contribution before year-end simplifies your record-keeping.

3m. The 2026 IRA contribution limit is $7,500 per person ($8,600 if age 50 or older). Roth IRA eligibility phases out at modified AGI of $150,000 to $165,000 (single) or $236,000 to $246,000 (married filing jointly) -- confirm current figures with your advisor as these are indexed for inflation [confirm exact 2026 Roth IRA phase-out figures with your advisor].

3n. Mark Section 3 of the Closeout Checklist with the plan type, contribution amount, and deadline confirmed.

*Output: Confirmed maximum allowable contribution for each plan and a deposit plan with a specific dollar amount and date.*

---

**Step 4: Submit all outstanding accountable plan reimbursements**

4a. Gather your accountable plan expense log and all receipts for business expenses paid out of pocket (or on a personal card) from January 1 through November 30, 2026. Expenses incurred in December can be submitted in January 2027 as long as substantiation is submitted within 60 days of incurring each expense.

4b. For each expense, confirm you have: the dollar amount, the date, the business purpose, and (for meals) the names of everyone present. Mileage entries need date, starting address, ending address, odometer readings, and business purpose for each trip.

4c. Prepare a formal expense report. Submit it to your business (even if you are the sole owner) as required by your accountable plan policy document.

4d. Have the business reimburse you from the business bank account by check or electronic transfer. Do not reimburse yourself with a cash withdrawal.

4e. Mileage reimbursements at or below the 2026 IRS standard business mileage rate of 72.5 cents per mile are fully tax-free to you and deductible to the business with no payroll taxes on either side.

4f. Mark Section 4 of the Closeout Checklist with the total reimbursed and the date the payment was issued.

*Output: A completed expense report submitted to the business, a reimbursement payment from the business account to you, and all supporting receipts filed.*

---

**Step 5: Confirm all equipment and vehicle purchases are placed in service**

5a. List every piece of equipment, technology, furniture, or vehicle purchased for business use in 2026 for which you intend to claim Section 179 or bonus depreciation.

5b. For each item, confirm the "placed in service" date -- the date the property was first available for business use, not merely ordered or delivered. An item you purchased in December but did not actually use until January 2027 cannot be deducted in 2026.

5c. For Section 179, the item must be placed in service by December 31, 2026, and the Section 179 election is made on Form 4562 (Depreciation and Amortization), which is filed with your tax return. Section 179 cannot create a business loss; it is limited to the business's taxable income for the year.

5d. For 100% bonus depreciation (property acquired after January 19, 2025, and placed in service in 2026), there is no business income limitation. Bonus depreciation can create or increase a net operating loss.

5e. If you are purchasing a vehicle primarily for business, confirm the business-use percentage you will claim. Vehicles with a gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR -- the manufacturer's maximum loaded weight) above 6,000 pounds used more than 50% for business qualify for Section 179 and/or bonus depreciation without the passenger vehicle luxury limits.

5f. Save the invoice, proof of payment, and a note of the date the item was first used for business.

5g. Mark Section 5 of the Closeout Checklist with each asset name, cost, placed-in-service date, and planned depreciation method.

*Output: A complete asset list with placed-in-service dates and depreciation elections confirmed before December 31.*

---

**Step 6: Confirm your HSA is fully funded**

6a. Log in to your HSA account and check your 2026 year-to-date contributions.

6b. For 2026, the maximum contribution is $4,400 for self-only HDHP coverage or $8,750 for family HDHP coverage. If you are age 55 or older, you may contribute an additional $1,000 as a catch-up contribution.

6c. Confirm you were enrolled in a qualifying HDHP for the months you are claiming HSA contributions. You can only contribute to an HSA for months in which you were enrolled in an HDHP (with certain exceptions for the last-month rule -- ask your advisor if you switched plans mid-year).

6d. HSA contributions for 2026 can be made up to April 15, 2027. However, if your employer processes payroll HSA contributions, the last payroll date before year-end is effectively your cutoff.

6e. If you have qualified medical expenses you paid out of pocket during 2026 and have not reimbursed yourself from the HSA yet, you may reimburse yourself at any time (there is no time limit to reimburse from an HSA for past expenses, as long as the expense occurred after the HSA was established and while you were enrolled in an HDHP).

6f. Mark Section 6 of the Closeout Checklist with the current HSA balance, the maximum for your coverage level, and the gap to contribute by April 15, 2027.

*Output: Confirmation of the remaining allowable HSA contribution for 2026 and a date to make the final deposit.*

---

**Step 7: Review your S-corporation salary for reasonableness**

7a. If you are a shareholder-employee of an S-corporation, compare your 2026 W-2 wages (from your year-to-date payroll report) to the total compensation you received, including W-2 wages plus distributions.

7b. The IRS requires that your W-2 wages represent "reasonable compensation" -- what an unrelated employer would pay someone in a comparable role in your industry and geographic market. There is no single safe percentage, but a salary that is significantly below industry norms or that represents a small fraction of total compensation draws IRS scrutiny.

7c. If your W-2 wages are below what you and your advisor have agreed is a reasonable amount, run an additional payroll before December 31, 2026, to bring total wages up to the appropriate level.

7d. Note that W-2 wages paid by the S-corporation also affect your QBI deduction. Above the income threshold, the QBI deduction is capped at 50% of W-2 wages paid by the business. Higher reasonable wages increase your QBI deduction ceiling.

7e. Confirm the year-end payroll is processed and taxes are deposited on time (federal payroll tax deposits are due on the next business day after a large payroll, or by the 15th of the following month for smaller payroll schedules -- confirm your deposit schedule with your payroll provider).

7f. Mark Section 7 of the Closeout Checklist with the final W-2 wage total and the date of the last payroll run.

*Output: Year-end payroll processed, total 2026 W-2 wages confirmed, and payroll taxes deposited.*

---

**Step 8: Close out the Augusta Rule rental (if you used it in 2026)**

8a. Confirm the total number of days you rented your home to your business in 2026. The day count must not exceed 14.

8b. Confirm that each rental event is documented with a written rental agreement or meeting confirmation showing the date(s), the business purpose, the rental rate, and the parties.

8c. Confirm you have written market-rate support -- typically three written rate quotes from comparable local venues (hotel conference rooms, private event spaces) -- showing that the rate your business paid was at or below market.

8d. Confirm the business paid you by check or bank transfer (personal-to-business cash transactions are not adequate documentation).

8e. Confirm the income was not reported on your personal return. Under IRC Section 280A(g), rentals of 14 days or fewer are excluded from your gross income at the federal level and do not need to be reported.

8f. Check whether your state conforms to the federal Augusta Rule exclusion. Some states do not. Note this for your advisor.

8g. Mark Section 8 of the Closeout Checklist with the number of days rented, the total rent paid, and confirmation that documentation is in the tax file.

*Output: Documentation folder complete with rental agreements, payment records, and rate comparables.*

---

**Step 9: Run a final QBI deduction projection and confirm entity structure is still optimal**

9a. Using your projected year-end net profit from Step 1, calculate the preliminary QBI deduction: 20% of projected qualified business income.

9b. If your projected 2026 taxable income (before the QBI deduction) will exceed $403,500 (married filing jointly) or $201,750 (all other filers), the deduction may be limited by the W-2 wage test or the UBIA (unadjusted basis immediately after acquisition) of qualified property. Confirm with your advisor which limitation applies and whether additional W-2 wages (from Step 7) can increase the deduction ceiling. (Source: IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 §4.26.)

9c. If your projected 2026 taxable income is below the phase-in threshold ($403,500 MFJ or $201,750 other), the deduction is 20% of QBI with no W-2 wage limitation. The minimum QBI deduction of $400 applies if you have at least $1,000 of QBI from a business in which you materially participate.

9d. If you operate a specified service trade or business (SSTB -- which includes law, accounting, health, consulting, financial services, and similar fields), your QBI deduction begins to phase out once taxable income exceeds the threshold. The deduction is eliminated when taxable income exceeds the phase-out ceiling ($553,500 MFJ or $276,750 other for 2026, per IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 §4.26).

9e. If your entity structure review (S-corp vs. sole proprietor vs. partnership) raises a concern, note it for your advisor. Entity elections generally cannot be changed retroactively for 2026 at this stage; plan any restructuring for 2027.

9f. Mark Section 9 of the Closeout Checklist with the preliminary QBI deduction estimate.

*Output: A preliminary QBI deduction figure to share with your tax preparer, flagging any W-2 wage or SSTB limitation.*

---

**Step 10: Do a final sweep of deductible expenses before December 31**

10a. Review your business credit card and bank statements for October and November 2026 to confirm all deductible expenses are recorded in your bookkeeping system. Reconcile accounts if they are not current.

10b. Consider whether you have any deductible business expenses you can pay before December 31 to accelerate the deduction into 2026 (for example, prepaying a software subscription, purchasing supplies, or making a charitable contribution through your business if allowable under your entity type).

10c. Confirm any business charitable contributions are made to qualifying organizations and documented with a written acknowledgment from the recipient if the contribution is $250 or more (required under IRC Section 170(f)(8)).

10d. If you are an itemizer on your personal return, review whether paying a state or local tax bill before December 31 would be beneficial, keeping in mind the $40,400 SALT deduction cap for 2026 and the phase-out for modified AGI above $505,000.

10e. Review any outstanding receivables. If a customer owes you money and the debt is genuinely uncollectible, you may be able to claim a bad debt deduction (only available for accrual-basis taxpayers -- not cash-basis taxpayers).

10f. Mark Section 10 of the Closeout Checklist with a note that books are reconciled and final expenses are recorded.

*Output: Bookkeeping current through November 30, all deductible expenses recorded, and final December expenses scheduled.*

---

**Step 11: Assemble your year-end file and hand off to your tax preparer**

11a. Compile a single digital folder (or physical binder) containing all the records identified in the "Required Documents And Records" section above.

11b. Prepare a brief summary memo for your tax preparer that lists: (1) each strategy in place for 2026, (2) the status of each (funded, partially funded, or pending), (3) any open questions, and (4) any new assets purchased or entity changes made in 2026.

11c. Send the completed Year-End Closeout Checklist to your tax preparer as a cover document. It gives your preparer the information needed to correctly prepare your return without having to chase missing data.

11d. If your entity is an S-corporation or partnership, Schedule K-1 forms (which report each owner's share of income, deduction, and credit) must be prepared and delivered to shareholders or partners no later than March 15, 2027 (the S-corp and partnership return due date), or September 15, 2027 with a valid extension.

11e. Mark the final section of the Closeout Checklist complete and note the date your file was sent to your tax preparer.

*Output: A complete year-end tax file delivered to your preparer with the Closeout Checklist as a cover document.*

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity type:**

- **Sole proprietors (Schedule C):** Self-employment tax of 15.3% applies to all net earnings (reduced to 2.9% above the $184,500 Social Security wage base). You can deduct one-half of SE tax on Form 1040. Retirement plan contributions reduce net income but do not reduce the SE tax base directly.
- **S-corporations:** Payroll for shareholder-employees must be current. Distributions cannot substitute for reasonable compensation. All employees (not just shareholders) must be offered the same retirement plan, unless exclusions apply.
- **Partnerships and multi-member LLCs taxed as partnerships:** Each partner's guaranteed payments may be subject to SE tax. Retirement plan contributions at the entity level follow different rules than at the individual level.
- **C-corporations:** C-corporation tax planning follows a different calendar and structure. This playbook focuses on pass-through entities and sole proprietors. C-corp owners should consult their advisor for entity-specific closeout steps.

**Payroll:**

- Year-end payroll must be processed by December 31, 2026, to have wages counted on 2026 Forms W-2.
- If your S-corporation has not been paying payroll during 2026 and you intend to take distributions, you must run payroll before year-end to ensure reasonable compensation is paid. Distributions paid without any corresponding W-2 wages expose the entity to IRS reclassification.
- Form 941 (Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return) for Q4 2026 is due January 31, 2027.

**State taxes:**

- State income tax rules vary significantly. Many states do not conform to federal bonus depreciation amounts, Section 179 limits, or the QBI deduction. Your state taxable income may be materially different from your federal taxable income.
- Some states impose franchise taxes, minimum taxes, or excise taxes on S-corporations. Confirm any state-level year-end obligations with your advisor.
- The Augusta Rule federal income exclusion does not apply in all states. Ask your advisor whether your state taxes home rental income regardless of the federal exclusion.
- SALT deduction cap: The $40,400 limit applies to federal itemized deductions. It does not affect your state return (states have their own rules). The $505,000 phase-out threshold is a federal modified AGI threshold.

**SECURE 2.0 and current-year changes:**

- Beginning in 2026, catch-up contributions for employees age 50 or older who had prior-year Social Security wages exceeding $150,000 must be made as Roth (after-tax) contributions. If this applies to your Solo 401(k) or SIMPLE IRA, confirm the Roth option is available in your plan. This is a new 2026 rule that may require plan amendments.
- The OBBBA made several permanent changes effective for 2025 and later tax years. If you received guidance about strategies that were affected by the 2025 "TCJA sunset," those sunset provisions no longer apply.

**Charitable contributions (2026 OBBBA change):**

- Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, a new floor applies for 2026: only the portion of charitable contributions that exceeds 0.5% of your adjusted gross income is deductible. The existing AGI percentage caps (60% for cash to public charities, 30% for appreciated long-term capital gain property) continue to apply. The carryover period remains five years for excess contributions. Note: for taxpayers in the 37% bracket, OBBBA also caps the value of itemized deductions at 35%; confirm with your advisor how this interacts with your overall itemized deduction planning.

**Augusta Rule state conformity:**

- As noted, several states do not follow the federal Section 280A(g) exclusion. The playbook for the Augusta Rule (separate lesson) contains the state-by-state note. If you used the Augusta Rule in 2026, confirm your state's treatment before filing.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Missing the SIMPLE IRA establishment deadline.** A SIMPLE IRA must be set up by October 1 of the year it is to be effective. Business owners who set one up in November or December assume it covers 2026; it will not. The IRS is firm on this date.

**2. Treating the Section 179 election as something done at filing.** Section 179 is elected on Form 4562 when you file, but the property must actually be placed in service before December 31, 2026. Purchasing equipment in December and using it on January 2, 2027 disqualifies it for 2026.

**3. Taking S-corp distributions without running payroll.** If you received distributions from your S-corporation during 2026 but paid yourself little or no W-2 salary, you are exposed to IRS reclassification of those distributions as wages and assessment of unpaid payroll taxes plus penalties.

**4. Over-contributing to a retirement plan.** If you have more than one plan (for example, a day job 401(k) and a Solo 401(k) for a side business), the $24,500 employee elective deferral limit is per person, not per plan. Exceeding the combined limit across all plans results in an excess contribution that must be corrected to avoid a 6% excise tax.

**5. No documentation for Augusta Rule rentals.** The Augusta Rule exclusion depends entirely on documentation: a written rental agreement for each event, a payment record, and comparable market rate support. An undocumented Augusta Rule rental is indefensible in an audit.

**6. Forgetting to substantiate accountable plan expenses within 60 days.** If you incurred a business expense in March 2026 and do not submit it for reimbursement until after June 2026, it may be treated as a non-accountable reimbursement and become taxable income to you.

**7. Confusing the filing extension with the payment extension.** Form 4868 gives you six extra months to file Form 1040. It does not give you six extra months to pay. Taxes owed are still due April 15, 2027; interest and late-payment penalties accrue on unpaid balances from that date.

**8. Failing to reconcile books before December 31.** If your bookkeeping is behind, you cannot accurately calculate your net profit, retirement plan contribution, or QBI deduction. Run a reconciliation for all accounts through November before the end of December.

**9. Ignoring the charitable contribution 0.5% floor.** For 2026, charitable contributions below 0.5% of your AGI are not deductible. Taxpayers who make small recurring donations may find those donations no longer provide a tax benefit.

**10. Missing the SALT cap increase.** Many taxpayers in high-tax states who have not itemized since 2018 may now benefit from itemizing in 2026 due to the $40,400 SALT cap. If your state and local taxes plus mortgage interest and charitable contributions exceed the standard deduction ($32,200 MFJ), itemizing may now produce a larger deduction.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and confirm with your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

1. **Your total retirement plan contributions across all plans will exceed $72,000 in 2026.** Excess contributions carry a 6% annual excise tax. Have your advisor calculate the combined limit across all plans you participate in.

2. **Your S-corporation has paid you little or no W-2 wages during 2026 and you are receiving distributions.** Do not close out the year without confirming a year-end payroll plan. Running payroll after December 31 cannot fix the problem for 2026.

3. **Your projected 2026 taxable income is above $403,500 (MFJ) or $201,750 (single)** and you operate a specified service trade or business (law, accounting, health, consulting, financial services). Your QBI deduction may be partially or fully phased out and the W-2 wage limitation is critical. (QBI phase-out is complete at $553,500 MFJ / $276,750 other per IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 §4.26.)

4. **You want to establish a new retirement plan for 2026 and have not yet done so.** SEP-IRAs can still be established before April 15, 2027. Solo 401(k) establishment deadlines depend on your entity type and whether you have common-law employees. Do not set up a new plan without confirming the establishment rules for your entity type.

5. **You purchased equipment under the 100% bonus depreciation rule and this will create or increase a net operating loss (NOL).** Confirm whether the loss passes through to your personal return, whether the excess business loss limitation (under IRC Section 461(l)) applies, and how the NOL carryforward rules affect future years.

6. **Your modified AGI is near the $505,000 SALT phase-out threshold.** The benefit of the increased $40,400 SALT cap phases out for modified AGI above $505,000 (30 cents per dollar of excess MAGI, until the cap floors at $10,000). If you are in that range, the deduction benefit requires a precise calculation.

7. **You are making a year-end Roth conversion.** A Roth conversion increases your taxable income for 2026, which can affect your QBI deduction ceiling, SALT phase-out, estimated tax calculations, and more. Coordinate with your advisor before executing the conversion.

8. **You are in a state that does not conform to federal bonus depreciation or the Augusta Rule exclusion.** Your state tax liability may be materially higher than expected.
