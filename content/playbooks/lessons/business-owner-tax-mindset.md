# Business Owner Tax Mindset

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The Business Owner Tax Mindset is not a single deduction or election; it is the operating posture that makes every other tax strategy work. Employees receive a paycheck after taxes have already been withheld. Business owners receive revenue before taxes, which means they have the opportunity to pay expenses, fund retirement accounts, shift income, and claim deductions before the tax bill is calculated. Understanding that sequence -- and building a system around it -- is the foundation for every strategy in this portal. This playbook walks you through the mindset shift and the concrete habits that turn tax awareness into lower tax bills.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is relevant to you if any of the following are true:

- You are a self-employed individual, sole proprietor, freelancer, or independent contractor filing Schedule C (Form 1040) with net earnings of $400 or more.
- You own or operate a single-member LLC, a multi-member LLC, an S corporation, or a partnership.
- You are a W-2 employee who also has a side business generating self-employment income.
- You have just started a business or are newly self-employed and want to understand how taxes work differently for business owners.
- You feel like you are constantly surprised by your tax bill and want to take a proactive approach.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- You are a W-2 employee with no self-employment income and no plans to start a business. The strategies in this playbook require business income to apply. The mindset section is still educational, but the action steps will not produce tax savings without business income.
- You are already running a fully optimized tax strategy with your advisor and simply need a reference for a specific deduction or election. Use the specific lesson in this portal for that topic instead.
- You expect your net business income to be below $400 for the year. Federal self-employment tax does not apply below that threshold, and most deduction-planning strategies do not produce meaningful savings at that level. Confirm with your advisor whether state taxes still apply.

## Key Terms

**Self-employment tax (SE tax):** The Social Security and Medicare taxes owed by self-employed individuals. Employees split these taxes with their employer (each paying 7.65%). When you are self-employed, you pay both sides, making the total rate 15.3% (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare) on net self-employment earnings up to the Social Security wage base (the annual dollar ceiling above which the Social Security portion of SE tax no longer applies; $184,500 for 2026 -- see The Numbers), plus 2.9% Medicare on any amount above that base. SE tax is calculated on Schedule SE (Form 1040) and added to your income tax.

**Net self-employment earnings:** Your gross business income minus your allowable business deductions. SE tax applies to 92.35% of net self-employment earnings (not 100%), because the IRS allows you to reduce SE income by the employer-equivalent share of SE tax before the rate is applied.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The IRS form that sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (disregarded entities) use to report business income and deductions. Net profit from Schedule C flows to your Form 1040 and is subject to both income tax and SE tax.

**Schedule SE (Form 1040):** The IRS form used to calculate your SE tax. It is filed with your Form 1040 each year.

**Above-the-line deductions:** Deductions you can take even if you do not itemize. Self-employed individuals can deduct several things "above the line," meaning before adjusted gross income (AGI) is calculated. The most important for business owners are the deduction for half of SE tax, the self-employed health insurance deduction, and contributions to self-employed retirement plans.

**Adjusted gross income (AGI):** Your total gross income minus your above-the-line deductions. AGI is the starting point for calculating many phase-outs, credits, and limitations throughout your tax return. Lowering AGI can unlock additional tax benefits.

**Taxable income:** AGI minus either the standard deduction or your itemized deductions. Your tax bracket is applied to taxable income, not gross income.

**Ordinary income:** Income taxed at your regular income tax rates (10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, or 37% depending on your bracket). Most business income and wages are ordinary income.

**Tax bracket:** The percentage rate that applies to each layer of your taxable income. The U.S. uses a progressive system, meaning only the income in each bracket is taxed at that rate. A business owner in the 24% bracket does not pay 24% on all income -- only on the income that falls within that bracket.

**Marginal rate:** The tax rate that applies to the next dollar of taxable income you earn. This is the rate that matters most for deduction planning. A deduction saves you your marginal rate in income tax, plus the SE tax rate if it also reduces SE income.

**Estimated tax payments:** Quarterly prepayments of income tax and SE tax made directly to the IRS (and often your state). Because business owners do not have an employer withholding taxes from a paycheck, they are generally required to estimate and pay tax four times per year. Payments are made using Form 1040-ES.

**Safe harbor:** A rule that protects you from an underpayment penalty even if you end up owing tax at filing. You meet the federal safe harbor if you pay at least (a) 90% of your current-year tax, or (b) 100% of last year's tax (110% if your prior-year AGI exceeded $150,000 -- see The Numbers).

**Deductible business expense:** Any ordinary (common and accepted in your industry) and necessary (appropriate and helpful to your business) expense paid or incurred in operating your trade or business. Authority: IRC Section 162.

**Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction:** A deduction of up to 20% of your net income from a qualified trade or business, available to sole proprietors, S corporation shareholders, and partners. Claimed on Form 8995 or Form 8995-A. Made permanent beginning January 1, 2026, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025). Previously, it was a temporary TCJA provision scheduled to expire after 2025.

**SSTB (Specified Service Trade or Business):** A category of businesses whose QBI deduction phases out above certain income thresholds. SSTBs include health, law, accounting, consulting, financial services, performing arts, athletics, brokerage services, and any business whose principal asset is the reputation or skill of one or more owners or employees. Non-SSTBs are not subject to the same phase-out.

**Entity type:** The legal structure of your business. Common types include sole proprietorship (no separate entity, all income on Schedule C), single-member LLC (treated as a sole proprietorship unless an election is made), S corporation (a corporation that passes income and loss to shareholders, avoiding corporate-level income tax), C corporation (taxed at the corporate level at a flat 21%), and partnership or multi-member LLC (income passes to partners). Entity type affects your tax rates, self-employment tax exposure, and available strategies.

**Reasonable compensation:** The wage an S corporation must pay its owner-employee before taking profit distributions. The IRS requires S corporation owner-employees who provide substantial services to pay themselves a wage that reflects what a similarly situated employee would earn in the market. Distributions above that wage are not subject to SE tax. If reasonable compensation is too low, the IRS can reclassify distributions as wages and assess back payroll taxes, penalties, and interest.

**Section 179 deduction:** A provision under IRC Section 179 that allows a business to deduct the full cost of qualifying property (equipment, machinery, certain vehicles, software) in the year it is placed in service, rather than depreciating it over time. Subject to annual dollar limits and income limits.

**Bonus depreciation:** An additional first-year depreciation deduction for qualifying business assets placed in service during the year. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21), 100% bonus depreciation is restored for qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025.

**SEP IRA (Simplified Employee Pension Individual Retirement Account):** A retirement account available to self-employed individuals and small business owners. Contributions are deductible above the line and reduce both income tax and, for sole proprietors, can reduce SE tax through the calculation chain.

**Solo 401(k) (Individual 401(k)):** A retirement plan designed for self-employed individuals with no employees (other than a spouse). Allows both employee and employer contributions, producing larger potential deductions than a SEP IRA for some income levels.

**Home office deduction:** A deduction for the portion of your home used regularly and exclusively for business. Available using the simplified method ($5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet, maximum $1,500) or the actual expense method (Form 8829, which allocates a percentage of actual home costs). Only available when the home office is the principal place of business or a place where clients regularly meet.

**Tax Vault:** Beyond Freedom Financial's document storage system. Keep your tax records, worksheets, and completed forms there for easy retrieval at tax time and in the event of an IRS inquiry.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before working through this playbook:

1. **Prior-year tax return (Form 1040 with all schedules).** This tells you your prior-year AGI, the tax you paid, which schedules applied, and your effective and marginal tax rates.
2. **Year-to-date profit-and-loss statement** from your accounting software. You need your current-year net business income to estimate tax owed.
3. **Current-year estimated tax payments made so far.** Note the date and amount of each payment.
4. **Entity type and formation documents.** Know whether you are operating as a sole proprietor, LLC, S corporation, or other structure.
5. **List of your business expense categories.** Think through major categories: equipment, home office, vehicle, travel, meals, software, subscriptions, professional services, marketing, insurance, retirement.
6. **Health insurance premium amounts** if you pay for your own coverage (not through a spouse's employer plan).
7. **Retirement plan type and current contribution levels.** Know whether you have a SEP IRA, Solo 401(k), SIMPLE IRA, or no retirement plan yet.
8. **State of business operation.** State income tax treatment varies. Know which states you operate in.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Understand your true tax exposure as a business owner

**Step 1: Calculate your two tax bills, not one.**

As a business owner, you owe two separate taxes on business profit:

- **Income tax:** Calculated at your marginal bracket rate on your taxable income.
- **SE tax:** 15.3% on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings up to the Social Security wage base (the annual ceiling above which the 12.4% Social Security portion no longer applies; $184,500 for 2026), plus 2.9% on any amount above that base (no ceiling on the Medicare portion).

Add these together to get your true effective tax rate on each additional dollar of business profit. For a business owner in the 22% income tax bracket with $80,000 of net self-employment earnings, the combined federal rate on that income is approximately 22% + 14.13% (the effective SE rate after the 92.35% adjustment) = approximately 36%. That is before any state income tax.

Use your prior-year Schedule SE and Form 1040 to find the actual numbers for your situation. Write them on the Business Owner Tax Mindset Implementation Worksheet.

**Step 2: Identify which above-the-line deductions you are currently claiming.**

Check your prior-year Form 1040, Schedule 1 for these deductions. If you are not taking them, you may be overpaying:

- Half of SE tax (automatically reduces AGI for income tax purposes; does not reduce SE tax itself). This is calculated on Schedule SE and entered on Schedule 1.
- Self-employed health insurance premiums (deductible above the line if you are not eligible to participate in an employer-subsidized plan through a spouse).
- Retirement plan contributions (SEP IRA, Solo 401(k), or SIMPLE IRA contributions are deductible above the line).

**Step 3: Find your marginal tax rate.**

Look at your most recent Form 1040. Find your taxable income and compare it to the 2026 tax brackets (listed in The Numbers section). The bracket your last dollar of income falls in is your marginal rate. This is the rate you save on every dollar of additional deductions you create.

**Step 4: Estimate your current-year tax liability.**

Use your current-year profit-and-loss statement to project your full-year net business income. Then calculate:

- SE tax: (Net business income x 0.9235) x 0.153 (for earnings up to the SS wage base)
- AGI reduction for half of SE tax: divide SE tax by 2
- Estimated AGI: gross income minus above-the-line deductions
- Estimated taxable income: estimated AGI minus standard deduction (or itemized deductions if higher)
- Estimated income tax: apply the 2026 tax brackets to estimated taxable income
- Total tax owed: SE tax plus income tax

Compare total estimated tax to estimated tax payments already made. The difference is what you will owe at filing (or the overpayment you will receive as a refund).

Use the Business Owner Tax Mindset Implementation Worksheet to run this calculation.

### Phase 2: Build your estimated tax payment system

**Step 5: Set up quarterly estimated tax payments.**

Because no employer withholds tax from business income, you must make estimated tax payments four times per year. The 2026 due dates are:

- April 15, 2026 (for income earned January 1 through March 31)
- June 15, 2026 (for income earned April 1 through May 31)
- September 15, 2026 (for income earned June 1 through August 31)
- January 15, 2027 (for income earned September 1 through December 31)

Use Form 1040-ES to make payments. The IRS strongly prefers electronic payment through IRS Direct Pay (irs.gov/directpay) or the Electronic Federal Tax Payment System (EFTPS; eftps.gov). Keep every payment confirmation.

**Step 6: Choose your safe harbor method.**

You avoid the underpayment penalty if your total estimated payments (plus any withholding from a W-2 job) equal at least one of these:

- **90% of your 2026 actual tax** (requires estimating accurately throughout the year), OR
- **100% of your 2025 actual tax** (easier -- simply divide last year's total tax by 4 and pay that amount each quarter), OR
- **110% of your 2025 actual tax** if your 2025 AGI exceeded $150,000 (or $75,000 if married filing separately) -- this higher threshold applies to higher-income taxpayers.

For most business owners with variable income, the 100%/110% of prior-year tax method is the simplest way to guarantee penalty protection.

**Step 7: Adjust payments when income changes significantly.**

If your business income increases substantially from the prior year, the prior-year safe harbor prevents the penalty but does not eliminate the cash due at filing. Increase your payments in the quarter when income jumps to avoid a large April bill.

### Phase 3: Organize your deduction categories

**Step 8: Set up a dedicated business bank account and credit card.**

Every dollar of business income should deposit into this account; every business expense should come from it. Mixed personal and business accounts make it nearly impossible to substantiate deductions cleanly and raise audit risk.

**Step 9: Create a deduction category checklist.**

Walk through the following categories and confirm you are capturing all legitimate expenses:

- **Home office:** Do you use a portion of your home regularly and exclusively for business? If yes, measure the space, calculate the percentage of total home square footage, and choose between the simplified method ($5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet) and the actual expense method (Form 8829). The home office deduction also unlocks the ability to deduct a pro-rata share of utilities, repairs, and depreciation under the actual method.
- **Vehicle:** Do you use a vehicle for business? If yes, track every business mile (date, destination, purpose, miles) in a contemporaneous mileage log. Alternatively, use the actual expense method. You cannot use the standard mileage rate if you previously used MACRS depreciation on the vehicle. See the Vehicle Deduction lesson in this portal.
- **Equipment and technology:** Computers, phones, cameras, tools, software, and subscriptions used for business are deductible. Under Section 179 or bonus depreciation, qualifying equipment may be fully deducted in the year purchased rather than depreciated over multiple years.
- **Professional development:** Books, courses, certifications, industry memberships, and conferences that maintain or improve skills required in your current business are deductible.
- **Professional services:** Accounting fees, legal fees, consulting fees, and payroll service fees paid for business purposes are deductible.
- **Marketing and advertising:** Website hosting, domain names, social media advertising, print materials, and business cards are deductible.
- **Business insurance:** General liability, professional liability, and other business insurance premiums are deductible.
- **Retirement contributions:** Contributions to a SEP IRA, Solo 401(k), or SIMPLE IRA are deductible above the line and reduce both income tax and effectively lower the SE tax calculation chain.
- **Health insurance premiums:** If you pay your own health insurance and are not eligible for coverage through a spouse's employer, premiums are deductible above the line under IRC Section 162(l).
- **Meals with a business purpose:** 50% of the cost of business meals (with a client, prospect, or business colleague where business is discussed) is deductible. The business purpose must be documented. See the Meals Deduction lesson.
- **Business travel:** Flights, hotels, and ground transportation for travel that is primarily for business are deductible. Personal days embedded in a business trip reduce the deductible portion. See the Business Travel Deduction lesson.

**Step 10: Close the deduction gap before year-end.**

Review your year-to-date P&L against your deduction checklist by October 31 each year. If you identify legitimate expenses you have not yet incurred but plan to, consider whether making those purchases before December 31 makes sense. Do not manufacture expenses; only make purchases you were already planning.

### Phase 4: Understand entity-level tax savings

**Step 11: Know the S corporation self-employment tax savings opportunity.**

As a sole proprietor or single-member LLC, 100% of your net business profit is subject to SE tax. As an S corporation, only the portion of income paid to you as wages (reasonable compensation) is subject to payroll tax (the same 15.3% split between employer and employee sides). Profit distributions above your reasonable compensation are not subject to payroll taxes.

Example (simplified): A business earns $150,000 in net profit. As a sole proprietor, all $150,000 is subject to SE tax. As an S corporation with $80,000 in reasonable compensation, only the $80,000 is subject to payroll taxes, and the remaining $70,000 is taken as a distribution not subject to those taxes. The payroll tax savings on the $70,000 can be significant, but S corporation compliance costs (payroll processing, corporate return filing, state fees) must be weighed against the savings. Discuss this with your advisor. See the LLC to S-Corp Conversion and S-Corp Owner Payroll / Reasonable Compensation lessons in this portal.

**Step 12: Understand QBI deduction eligibility.**

If your business is a sole proprietorship, S corporation, or partnership, you may be eligible for the QBI deduction: up to 20% of your net qualified business income. This is in addition to your other deductions and can significantly reduce your effective tax rate on business income. The deduction is calculated on Form 8995 (for simpler cases) or Form 8995-A (for higher-income or SSTB situations). See The Numbers section for 2026 income thresholds. See the QBI Deduction Basics and 2026 Watchpoint lesson for a full breakdown.

### Phase 5: Build your recurring tax management system

**Step 13: Set up a monthly tax review habit.**

Once a month, do these four things:

1. Pull your P&L from your accounting software. Confirm that income and expenses are categorized correctly.
2. Confirm your estimated tax payment for the current quarter is scheduled and funded.
3. Note any large anticipated income or expense changes coming in the next 60 days.
4. Save bank statements and any major receipts to your Tax Vault.

**Step 14: Conduct a year-end tax planning review with your advisor.**

Schedule a meeting in October or November (before December 31). Bring your year-to-date P&L, your projected full-year income, and your list of estimated tax payments made. Your advisor can identify year-end moves: accelerating deductible expenses, deferring income if appropriate, making retirement contributions, and confirming your entity strategy is optimized for the current year's income level.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**S corporation payroll compliance.**
If you operate as an S corporation, you must run actual payroll for your wages, withhold payroll taxes, make employer payroll tax deposits, and file quarterly payroll returns (Form 941). Taking only distributions and no salary is one of the most heavily audited S corporation issues. The payroll compliance cost must be weighed against the SE tax savings when deciding whether to elect S corporation status.

**State income taxes and SE taxes.**
This playbook covers federal tax only. Many states impose their own income taxes; some also impose their own version of self-employment or business taxes. A few states (Florida, Texas, Nevada, South Dakota, Wyoming, Washington, Alaska) have no personal income tax, but business activity taxes may still apply. Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor.

**State conformity to QBI deduction.**
Not all states allow the Section 199A QBI deduction. Some states that conformed to TCJA did not adopt the QBI deduction, and some may not conform to the One Big Beautiful Bill Act's permanent extension. Confirm with your advisor whether your state allows the deduction.

**SSTB classification.**
If your business involves health, law, accounting, financial services, consulting, performing arts, athletics, brokerage, or any field where the business's primary asset is the owner's reputation or skill, you may be classified as an SSTB. For SSTB owners whose taxable income is above the phase-out thresholds, the QBI deduction is reduced or eliminated entirely. Discuss your classification with your advisor before projecting QBI savings.

**Additional Medicare Tax.**
If your combined net self-employment income and wages exceed $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), you owe an additional 0.9% Medicare tax on the amount above the threshold. This is not split with an employer -- you pay the full 0.9% yourself. It is calculated on Form 8959 and added to your Form 1040 tax. The governing statutes are IRC Section 1401(b)(2) for self-employment income and IRC Section 3101(b)(2) for wages.

**Excess business loss limitation.**
Non-corporate taxpayers cannot deduct net business losses exceeding $256,000 (single or other non-joint filers) or $512,000 (married filing jointly) for 2026. These amounts decreased from the 2025 limits ($313,000 / $626,000) because the One Big Beautiful Bill Act made the Section 461(l) limitation permanent and reset the inflation-indexing methodology to the original TCJA base amounts. Excess losses become a net operating loss carryforward. Source: Rev. Proc. 2025-32; IRC Section 461(l).

**Estimated tax for states.**
Most states that have an income tax also require quarterly estimated tax payments, often on a schedule similar to the federal one. Failure to pay state estimated taxes can generate state underpayment penalties separately from the federal penalty. Confirm your state's requirements with your advisor.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT).**
This 3.8% tax applies to passive income (investment income, rental income in some cases) above the same thresholds as the Additional Medicare Tax. It does not apply to active business income subject to SE tax. If you have both active business income and passive investment or rental income, the interaction requires careful planning.

**Retirement plan setup deadlines.**
A Solo 401(k) must be established by December 31 of the tax year you want to contribute for. A SEP IRA can be established up to the return due date (including extensions). Confirm exact deadlines with your plan custodian and your advisor before the end of each year.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Treating SE tax as a surprise every year.**
Many business owners know their income tax bracket but forget SE tax. The result is a large unexpected April bill. Estimate both taxes from day one using the method in Step 4.

**2. Skipping estimated tax payments and paying everything in April.**
This generates an underpayment penalty from the IRS and often from the state. Even if the total you owe at filing is modest, you still owe the penalty for the quarters you underpaid.

**3. Commingling personal and business finances.**
Depositing business income into your personal account and paying expenses from the same account makes it impossible to substantiate deductions and signals poor business records to an IRS examiner.

**4. Not tracking mileage in real time.**
The IRS requires a contemporaneous mileage log under IRC Section 274(d). A log reconstructed at year-end from memory is not considered adequate. Log each trip on the day it happens.

**5. Missing the home office deduction.**
Many self-employed individuals qualify for the home office deduction but do not claim it out of fear it will trigger an audit. The IRS data does not support the idea that the home office deduction is a disproportionate audit trigger for legitimate businesses. If you have a dedicated space used only for business, claim it.

**6. Overlooking retirement contributions as a deduction tool.**
Contributing to a SEP IRA or Solo 401(k) reduces your AGI dollar for dollar and may also reduce your SE tax base depending on the plan type. A business owner in the 22% bracket who contributes $20,000 to a SEP IRA saves approximately $4,400 in income tax alone, plus potential SE tax savings through the AGI-reduction chain.

**7. Not updating estimated tax payments when income spikes.**
The prior-year safe harbor prevents a penalty, but if income doubles in the current year, the prior-year safe harbor only protects you from the penalty; it does not reduce what you actually owe. If you hit a large contract or sale mid-year, adjust your remaining quarterly payments upward to avoid a cash-flow shock in April.

**8. Electing S corporation status without running payroll correctly.**
Some business owners elect S corporation status to save SE taxes, then fail to run payroll, pay themselves only distributions, or pay unreasonably low wages. The IRS can reclassify distributions as wages and assess back payroll taxes, penalties, and interest. The S corporation strategy requires proper payroll compliance to be legal and beneficial.

**9. Waiting until April to think about taxes.**
Tax reduction is a year-round activity. Most strategies (retirement contributions, equipment purchases, S corp payroll structure, estimated payments) must be in place before December 31. An April review can only help you file correctly; it cannot undo a missed opportunity.

**10. Assuming last year's strategy still applies.**
Tax law changes, income changes, and business structure changes affect which strategies apply each year. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) made significant permanent changes to many TCJA provisions, including the QBI deduction and bonus depreciation. Review your strategy with your advisor every year.

## Advisor Review Gates

Contact your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

1. **Before electing S corporation status.** The SE tax savings must outweigh the compliance costs (payroll, corporate return, state fees). This calculation depends on your net income level and your state. Your advisor should model both scenarios before you elect.

2. **Before setting your S corporation reasonable compensation.** Setting wages too low is an audit risk; setting them too high eliminates the distribution benefit. Your advisor should help you establish a defensible compensation figure based on comparable wages in your industry and geography.

3. **Before making a large equipment purchase to capture Section 179 or bonus depreciation.** These deductions are powerful but they require the equipment to be placed in service (actually used in the business) before December 31. They also create recapture risk if you sell or stop using the equipment. Your advisor can confirm eligibility and timing.

4. **Before changing your entity type.** Converting from a sole proprietorship to an LLC, from an LLC to an S corporation, or from an S corporation to a C corporation has tax, legal, and administrative consequences that may not be reversible without cost. Plan the conversion with your advisor before filing any state or IRS documents.

5. **Before taking profit distributions from an S corporation.** Each distribution reduces your basis in the S corporation. Taking distributions in excess of basis creates a taxable gain. Your advisor should confirm your current basis before you take a large distribution.

6. **If your income varies significantly from prior years.** A large swing up or down affects estimated tax payments, QBI deduction eligibility, safe harbor calculations, and retirement contribution limits. Recalculate all of these with your advisor mid-year if income changes materially.

7. **If you operate in more than one state or travel to other states for business.** Multi-state activity can create tax filing obligations in states where you work, even if you are not based there. Confirm your state filing requirements.

8. **Before year-end if you have not yet made retirement contributions.** Some plan types have December 31 setup and contribution deadlines. Do not miss them.

## Before You Act

Before changing your entity structure, electing S corporation treatment, setting your own compensation, adjusting your estimated tax payments, making large equipment purchases, opening a retirement plan, or taking a profit distribution:

**Confirm your specific facts with your advisor.**

This playbook teaches the framework that applies to most business owners. It does not account for your individual income level, filing status, state, industry, existing entity structure, or prior-year tax history. The numbers in this playbook are verified for 2026 federal law; your situation may involve state taxes, phase-outs, limitations, or interactions with other strategies that change the outcome materially.

Your advisor should review:
- Your completed Business Owner Tax Mindset Implementation Worksheet.
- Your current-year projected income and deductions.
- Your entity type and whether a change is warranted.
- Your estimated tax payment schedule and whether it meets the safe harbor for your income level.
- Your retirement plan options and contribution limits.

Do not file an S corporation election (Form 2553), change your payroll, take a large distribution, make a major equipment purchase, or set your reasonable compensation without completing that review first.
