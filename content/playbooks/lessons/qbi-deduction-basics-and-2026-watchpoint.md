# QBI Deduction Basics and 2026 Watchpoint

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction -- also called the Section 199A deduction -- lets eligible business owners deduct up to 20 percent of their qualifying business profit directly from taxable income, without requiring them to spend a dollar to earn it. For a business owner in the 32 percent federal tax bracket, that deduction can reduce their effective rate on business income to roughly 26 percent. The deduction was originally set to expire after tax year 2025, but the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (signed July 4, 2025) made it permanent and added new provisions effective starting with tax year 2026. This playbook explains who qualifies, how to calculate and claim the deduction, and what changed for 2026.

## Who This Applies To

You are likely a good candidate for this deduction if all of the following are true:

- You own a business structured as a sole proprietorship (a business you run under your own name or a "doing business as" trade name, reported on Schedule C of your personal tax return), a single-member LLC taxed as a sole proprietorship, a partnership, a multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership, or an S corporation (a corporation that passes its income and losses through to the owners' personal returns).
- You have net profit from that business (not a loss) during the tax year.
- Your total taxable income (all income from all sources before claiming this deduction) is above zero.
- You are filing a U.S. federal tax return on Form 1040, 1040-SR, or 1040-NR.

C corporations (corporations that file their own separate tax return and pay the 21 percent corporate income tax rate directly) do NOT qualify -- the deduction was designed specifically for pass-through businesses (businesses whose income passes through to the owner's personal return).

Real estate investors may qualify if their rental activity rises to the level of a "trade or business" -- see "Entity, Payroll, State, and Current-Year Caveats" below.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not expect this deduction to apply in full if any of the following describe you:

- **Your business is a Specified Service Trade or Business (SSTB) AND your income is above the phase-in range.** An SSTB is defined by law as any business primarily providing services in health, law, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services, brokerage services, investing, investment management, or trading in securities, commodities, or partnership interests. Also included is any business where the principal asset is the reputation or skill of its employees or owners. If your business falls into one of these categories and your taxable income exceeds the upper phase-out threshold for your filing status, your QBI deduction from that business is zero.
- **Your business had a net loss this year.** A loss from one business can offset QBI from another. If total combined QBI is zero or negative, there is nothing to deduct.
- **You operate exclusively as an employee.** W-2 wages paid to you as an employee are not QBI, even if you also own the business paying them.
- **You are a C corporation owner only.** The deduction passes through to you only if you own a pass-through entity.
- **You are in a very low bracket.** If your taxable income after the standard deduction is very small, the tax savings may be minimal -- but the calculation still applies; it never hurts.

## Key Terms

**Qualified Business Income (QBI):** The net amount of income, gain, deduction, and loss from a qualified domestic trade or business. Excludes: wages you pay yourself as an S corp officer, capital gains and losses, dividends, interest income not related to the business, and certain other investment-type items.

**Section 199A:** The section of the Internal Revenue Code (the body of U.S. federal tax law) that created the QBI deduction as part of the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017.

**Pass-through business:** A business structure in which the business's income and deductions "pass through" to the owner's personal tax return rather than being taxed at the entity level. Includes sole proprietorships, partnerships, LLCs taxed as sole props or partnerships, and S corporations.

**Sole proprietorship:** A one-owner business that is not a separate legal entity from its owner; income and expenses are reported on Schedule C of the owner's Form 1040.

**S corporation:** A type of corporation that has made a special election with the IRS (using Form 2553) to have its profits and losses flow through to its shareholders' personal tax returns, avoiding corporate-level federal income tax.

**Partnership:** A business owned by two or more people. Each partner receives a Schedule K-1 showing their share of the partnership's income and deductions, which they report on their personal return.

**LLC (Limited Liability Company):** A business structure created under state law. For federal tax purposes, an LLC is classified based on how many members it has and whether it has elected to be taxed as a corporation -- it does not have its own tax classification by default.

**Taxable income:** Your total gross income minus all allowed deductions (including the standard deduction or itemized deductions) but BEFORE subtracting the QBI deduction itself. This is the number used to test whether income-based QBI limitations apply to you.

**Specified Service Trade or Business (SSTB):** A category of businesses, listed in Section 199A(d)(1) of the tax code, that face an additional restriction: the QBI deduction for SSTB income is phased out completely for high earners. See the full list in "When This Is Not A Fit" above.

**W-2 wages:** The total wages, tips, and other compensation paid to employees and reported on W-2 forms for a tax year. For S corporation owners, your officer salary appears on a W-2. Important: wages you pay your employees (or yourself as an S corp officer) count toward this figure; guaranteed payments from a partnership do not.

**UBIA (Unadjusted Basis Immediately After Acquisition):** The original purchase price (cost basis) of depreciable property owned by your business, not reduced by depreciation taken over the years. Used in the alternative W-2 plus property wage test.

**Phase-in range:** The range of taxable income between two dollar thresholds within which the QBI deduction limitations are partially applied. Below the lower threshold, no limitations apply. Above the upper threshold, full limitations (or, for SSTBs, full elimination) apply. Between the two, the limitations are prorated.

**Taxable income before QBI deduction:** The figure on your tax return after all other deductions, but before subtracting the QBI deduction. This is the trigger number for determining which limitation rules apply.

**Form 8995:** The IRS form used to calculate the QBI deduction for taxpayers whose taxable income is at or below the lower phase-in threshold. It is a shorter, simpler form.

**Form 8995-A:** The IRS form used to calculate the QBI deduction for taxpayers whose taxable income exceeds the lower phase-in threshold, taxpayers with multiple businesses, taxpayers in SSTBs, and anyone subject to the W-2 wage limitations.

**One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA):** Legislation signed into law on July 4, 2025 (Public Law 119-21) that, among many other provisions, made the Section 199A QBI deduction permanent and added a new minimum deduction and wider phase-in windows, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025 (i.e., starting with calendar-year 2026 returns).

**Material participation:** An IRS standard for how actively involved you are in a business. For the new 2026 minimum deduction, you must materially participate in the trade or business. Generally, this means you worked in the business for more than 500 hours during the year, or it was your only business activity, or you met one of several other IRS tests (see Treasury Regulation 1.469-5T for the complete list).

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before starting your QBI calculation:

1. Your total taxable income for the year (before the QBI deduction) -- this is the gating number for which form and which limitations apply.
2. The net profit (or loss) from each qualified business you own: Schedule C net income, Schedule E net income from partnerships or S corps (from your K-1s), or Schedule F net income.
3. W-2 wage totals for each business: the total wages shown on W-2s issued by each business entity (including your own officer W-2 from your S corp, if applicable).
4. UBIA of qualified property for each business: the original purchase cost (before depreciation) of depreciable tangible property (equipment, machinery, buildings) used in the business. Your bookkeeper or CPA should have this from your depreciation schedules.
5. Your filing status (Married Filing Jointly, Single, Head of Household, etc.) -- this determines which income thresholds apply.
6. Whether any of your businesses fall under the SSTB definition (listed above in "When This Is Not A Fit").
7. Prior-year QBI loss carryforwards, if any. If a prior year resulted in negative combined QBI, that loss carries forward and reduces current-year QBI.
8. Any qualified REIT dividends or qualified publicly traded partnership (PTP) income received during the year (shown on brokerage statements or Schedule K-1s).

## Required Documents And Records

**Before filing, collect and retain:**

- Schedule C (profit/loss from sole proprietorship or single-member LLC)
- Schedule K-1 forms from each partnership or S corporation in which you have an ownership interest, showing your share of income, deductions, and W-2 wages
- W-2 forms issued by each business entity (especially your officer W-2 from your S corp)
- Business depreciation schedules showing the UBIA (original cost before depreciation) of all depreciable property in each business
- Prior-year tax returns, specifically the QBI loss carryforward amount from the previous year's Form 8995 or 8995-A
- Brokerage statements showing any qualified REIT dividends or qualified PTP income
- Completed Form 8995 or Form 8995-A (whichever applies) for the current year

**Retention guidance:** Keep these records for at least three years after the filing due date of the return on which the deduction was claimed (consistent with the standard IRS statute of limitations for assessment). If you have a QBI loss carryforward, retain records until three years after the carryforward is fully used. Property-related records supporting UBIA should be kept as long as you own the property, plus three years after you sell it and claim the final depreciation deduction.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Work through each step in order. Stop at any step where your facts create uncertainty and consult your advisor before continuing.

**Step 1: Determine your taxable income before the QBI deduction.**

Pull up your draft or completed Form 1040. Find the line labeled "Taxable income" (line 15 on Form 1040 as of 2025). Add back the QBI deduction if it has already been entered on line 13 of Schedule A to Form 1040 (or the draft placeholder). This "taxable income before QBI" is the threshold test number.

**Step 2: Identify which businesses produce QBI.**

List every pass-through business you own. For each one:
- Calculate the net profit (or loss) from that business using Schedule C, Schedule E, or Schedule F.
- Exclude any income that is not QBI: capital gains or losses, dividends, interest not tied to the business, W-2 wages paid to you as an S corp officer, guaranteed payments from a partnership.
- Mark whether each business is an SSTB. If you are unsure, check the list in "Key Terms" and "When This Is Not A Fit."

**Step 3: Net your QBI across all businesses.**

Add together the QBI amounts from all your businesses. If some businesses have positive QBI and others have a loss, the losses offset the gains. If you have a prior-year QBI loss carryforward (from your prior-year Form 8995 or 8995-A), subtract that as well. The result is your combined net QBI. If this number is zero or negative, stop -- there is no deduction available this year, and you may have a new carryforward.

**Step 4: Check whether you are below, within, or above the phase-in range.**

Compare your taxable income before QBI (from Step 1) to the thresholds for your filing status (see "The Numbers" section below for 2026 amounts).

- **Below the lower threshold:** Go directly to Step 5A (simple calculation). No W-2 wage or SSTB limitations apply.
- **Within the phase-in range:** Go to Step 5B (partial limitation). The limitations phase in gradually over this range.
- **Above the upper threshold:** Go to Step 5C (full limitation). The W-2 wage limitation applies in full, and SSTB income is fully excluded.

**Step 5A: Simple calculation (income below the lower threshold).**

Your deduction is the lesser of:
- 20 percent of your combined net QBI (from Step 3), OR
- 20 percent of your taxable income before the QBI deduction minus any net capital gain.

This is the cap that prevents the deduction from creating a refund or reducing taxes on investment income. Use Form 8995 to perform this calculation.

**Step 5B: Partial limitation calculation (income within the phase-in range).**

You are in the transition zone. Your QBI deduction is calculated as in Step 5A, but then partially reduced based on where your income falls within the range. The reduction is proportional: if you are exactly halfway through the range, half of the W-2 wage limitation applies.

For SSTB owners, the applicable percentage of your SSTB income that counts as QBI shrinks proportionally as income climbs through the range. Above the upper threshold, it reaches zero.

You must use Form 8995-A for this calculation. Your tax preparation software will handle the math if you enter all the inputs correctly.

**Step 5C: Full limitation calculation (income above the upper threshold).**

The W-2 wage limitation applies in full. Your deduction from each business is the lesser of:
- 20 percent of QBI from that business, OR
- The greater of: (a) 50 percent of the W-2 wages paid by that business, OR (b) 25 percent of the W-2 wages paid by that business PLUS 2.5 percent of the UBIA of qualified property used in that business.

If the business has no W-2 wages (for example, a solo S corp owner who has not yet set up payroll, or a sole proprietor with no employees), the wage limitation may reduce the deduction significantly or to zero. This is a major planning point -- see "Common Mistakes."

If your business is an SSTB and your income is above the upper threshold, the deduction from that SSTB income is zero.

You must use Form 8995-A for this calculation.

**Step 6: Apply the taxable income cap.**

Regardless of which path you took above, the total QBI deduction cannot exceed 20 percent of your taxable income minus net capital gain. If your business QBI calculation from Steps 5A, 5B, or 5C produces a number larger than this cap, use the cap instead.

**Step 7: Check the new 2026 minimum deduction.**

If your taxable income calculation results in a deduction that would otherwise be less than $400, and you have at least $1,000 of QBI from a trade or business in which you materially participate, you are entitled to a minimum deduction of $400 (this amount is adjusted for inflation for years after 2026). This is a new provision effective starting with 2026 tax year returns.

**Step 8: Add any REIT dividend and PTP component.**

Separately calculate 20 percent of any qualified REIT dividends and qualified PTP income you received. This component is not subject to the W-2 wage or SSTB limitations. Add this amount to your QBI component (from the steps above) to arrive at your total QBI deduction.

**Step 9: Report the deduction on your return.**

Enter the total QBI deduction on Form 1040 (or 1040-SR or 1040-NR), line 13a. Attach the completed Form 8995 or Form 8995-A to your return.

**Step 10: Note any QBI loss carryforward.**

If your combined net QBI from Step 3 was negative (a loss), you cannot deduct a loss in the current year, but you carry that negative amount forward to reduce QBI in future years. Note the carryforward amount on your Form 8995 or 8995-A and keep it with your records for next year.

## Timing And Deadlines

**Tax year:** The QBI deduction is claimed on your annual income tax return for the year in which the business income was earned. For calendar-year taxpayers, that is January 1 through December 31 of the tax year.

**Filing deadline:** For most individual taxpayers, the Form 1040 (with attached Form 8995 or 8995-A) is due April 15 of the year following the tax year. For tax year 2026, that means April 15, 2027. An automatic six-month extension (Form 4868) moves the filing deadline to October 15, 2027, but does NOT extend the time to pay any taxes owed.

**No separate election needed:** You do not file any special form or election to claim the QBI deduction. You simply complete the calculation and enter the result on your return. You can claim it whether you take the standard deduction or itemize.

**Planning deadlines that affect the QBI deduction:**

- **S corp officer salary (W-2 wages):** To get credit for W-2 wages in a tax year, the wages must actually be paid and a W-2 issued during that year. For calendar-year S corps, the last payroll run that counts toward the current year's W-2 wage total must be completed on or before December 31 of the tax year.
- **Retirement plan contributions:** Contributions to a solo 401(k) or SEP-IRA reduce your QBI because they reduce net self-employment income. Employer contributions can be made up until the tax return due date (including extensions). If reducing QBI is your goal, be aware of this timing effect.
- **Entity structure changes:** If your advisor recommends changing entity structure to optimize the QBI deduction (for example, converting to an S corp to establish a W-2 wage base), those changes typically need to happen before the tax year begins or very early in the tax year. Mid-year S corp elections are possible but add complexity.

**Annual rhythm:** Review the QBI deduction with your advisor as part of your year-end tax planning meeting, ideally in October or November of each tax year. At that point you can still adjust W-2 wages, retirement contributions, and entity-level decisions before December 31.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity type matters significantly:**

- **Sole proprietorships and single-member LLCs taxed as sole props:** QBI equals net profit from Schedule C. There are no W-2 wages from this business unless you actually have employees. Solo operators with no employees get no benefit from the W-2 wage tests and face a hard limit if income is above the phase-in threshold.
- **S corporations:** S corp owners must pay themselves reasonable compensation (a wage the IRS considers fair market pay for the work they do), reported on a W-2. That W-2 wage counts as W-2 wages for the QBI wage limitation test. QBI for the S corp owner equals their share of the S corp's pass-through profit (not the officer salary). An S corp with a W-2 wage base generally produces a larger available deduction for high-income owners than a sole prop, which is a major reason business owners consider converting to S corp status.
- **Partnerships and multi-member LLCs:** Guaranteed payments to partners are excluded from QBI. The partner's distributive share of the partnership's income (net of guaranteed payments) is QBI. W-2 wages reported by the partnership entity count for the limitation test.
- **Rental real estate:** Rental income from passive activities generally does not qualify as QBI from a "trade or business." However, under IRS Revenue Procedure 2019-38, rental real estate enterprises that meet certain requirements (including maintaining separate books and records and at least 250 hours of services per year in most cases) may use a safe harbor to treat the activity as a trade or business eligible for the QBI deduction. Real estate professionals (those who spend more than 750 hours per year in real estate activities) may also qualify through a different route. Discuss your rental situation with your advisor.

**Payroll and W-2 wages:**

For business owners above the phase-in threshold, the W-2 wage test is often the single biggest determinant of how large the deduction is. If your business has no payroll -- for example, a sole proprietor consultant or an S corp owner who has not set up payroll -- the 50 percent W-2 wage test produces zero, which eliminates the deduction entirely for that business (except for the UBIA-based alternative, which also requires some W-2 wages or significant tangible property). This is a critical planning point. If your income is likely to exceed the threshold, discuss payroll setup with your advisor well before year-end.

**State tax treatment varies:**

Not all states conform to the federal QBI deduction. Some states that previously did not conform are gradually moving to conform, but state conformity is not automatic. The deduction appears on your federal return; it does not automatically reduce state taxable income. Confirm your state's conformity status with your advisor.

**Current-year changes from OBBBA:**

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025) made these changes to Section 199A:
1. Made the deduction permanent (no more sunset risk after 2025).
2. Expanded the phase-in window from $100,000 to $150,000 for married filers and from $50,000 to $75,000 for others.
3. Added the $400 minimum deduction for active business owners with at least $1,000 of QBI.
4. Did NOT change the SSTB exclusion list.
5. Did NOT change the 20 percent deduction rate.
6. Did NOT change the W-2 wage tests (50 percent and 25 percent + 2.5 percent UBIA).

**QBI deduction does not affect self-employment tax:** The QBI deduction reduces your federal income tax but does NOT reduce self-employment tax (the 15.3 percent or 2.9 percent Medicare-only tax on sole proprietor net profit). Do not confuse the two.

**AMT interaction:** The QBI deduction is allowed for purposes of the Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) and reduces AMT income as well as regular taxable income.

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Counting officer salary as QBI.**
S corporation owners who pay themselves a salary sometimes count the entire S corp revenue as QBI. The W-2 salary paid to the owner is NOT QBI -- it is excluded under Section 199A(c)(4)(B)(i). Only the pass-through profit (after the salary) is QBI.

**Mistake 2: Ignoring the W-2 wage limitation because it looks like extra paperwork.**
High-income business owners sometimes skip the W-2 wage analysis and assume they get the full 20 percent deduction. Above the phase-in threshold, the wage limitation can reduce or eliminate the deduction entirely, especially for service-based businesses with no employees.

**Mistake 3: Not setting up payroll in an S corp, then discovering the deduction is zero.**
An S corp owner who has not set up payroll has no W-2 wages. Above the income threshold, the deduction for a zero-wage S corp with no significant equipment is $0. Payroll must be established during the tax year to create a W-2 wage base for that year.

**Mistake 4: Assuming SSTB status kills the deduction at all income levels.**
The SSTB restriction only applies when your taxable income exceeds the lower threshold. If your income is below the lower phase-in threshold, your SSTB income qualifies just like any other business income and you get the full 20 percent deduction. Many service business owners at lower income levels unnecessarily assume they do not qualify.

**Mistake 5: Forgetting to carry forward a QBI loss.**
If combined QBI is negative in a given year, that loss must be carried forward and reduces QBI in the next year. Forgetting to apply the carryforward understates the limitation in future years.

**Mistake 6: Treating the QBI deduction as applying to gross revenue.**
The deduction is based on net qualified business income -- that is, revenue minus all allowed business expenses. If expenses exceed revenue, QBI is zero or negative.

**Mistake 7: Counting passive rental income that has not been tested against the safe harbor.**
Not all rental income qualifies as QBI. If you claim the deduction on rental income without documenting that you meet the safe harbor requirements (Rev Proc 2019-38) or qualify as a real estate professional, the deduction may be disallowed on audit.

**Mistake 8: Missing the new $400 minimum deduction for 2026.**
Starting with tax year 2026, if your deduction calculation produces less than $400 but you have at least $1,000 of QBI from an active business you materially participate in, you are entitled to $400. Taxpayers (or their software) may miss this if they stop at the standard calculation and do not check the minimum.

## Advisor Review Gates

You must review the following decisions with your advisor before acting. Do not proceed based on this page alone.

1. **SSTB determination:** If there is any question about whether your business qualifies as an SSTB, do not assume either way. The regulations under Section 199A include detailed definitions that are not always obvious. Get a confirmed determination before planning around the deduction.

2. **S corp conversion to establish W-2 wages:** If your advisor suggests converting to or forming an S corp to create a W-2 wage base and increase the QBI deduction, that structural change has many non-QBI implications (payroll tax, state registration, reasonable compensation requirement, costs). Do not make this change without a full analysis.

3. **Officer compensation amount:** The IRS requires S corp owner-employees to pay themselves "reasonable compensation" before taking profit distributions. Too little salary increases QBI but invites an audit reclassification. The right number depends on your industry, role, and other factors -- set it with advisor input.

4. **Rental real estate safe harbor eligibility:** If you want to claim QBI on rental income, have your advisor confirm you have properly documented at least 250 hours of services (or met another qualifying path) and are maintaining the required separate books and records.

5. **State conformity:** Before assuming the federal deduction reduces your state taxes as well, ask your advisor to confirm your state's current conformity status with Section 199A.

6. **Any prior-year loss carryforwards:** Confirm the correct carryforward amount from your prior-year return before starting the current-year calculation.

7. **UBIA of qualified property:** If you plan to use the 25 percent W-2 plus 2.5 percent UBIA alternative wage test, the UBIA figures must come from your depreciation schedules. Ask your advisor or bookkeeper to pull these numbers before your tax appointment.

8. **2026 final thresholds:** The income thresholds for 2026 cited in this playbook are projections. Confirm the final IRS-announced figures with your advisor before finalizing your return.

## Before You Act

This playbook gives you the framework and the numbers to understand and implement the QBI deduction. It is not personalized tax advice tailored to your specific business structure, income, filing status, state of residence, or personal circumstances.

Before you file your return, claim the deduction, make any entity changes, set or change your officer salary, make retirement plan contributions intended to affect QBI, or take any action based on the information in this playbook, confirm the specific facts of your situation with your advisor. A calculation error or misidentification of your business as qualifying (or not qualifying) can result in either an overstated deduction that draws IRS scrutiny or an understated deduction that costs you money.

If your situation involves multiple businesses, SSTB questions, high income near the phase-in thresholds, rental real estate, or any planned entity changes, the analysis is complex enough that working through it with your advisor before filing is especially important.

   Supports: 20 percent deduction rate; two-component structure (QBI + REIT/PTP); form references; taxable income cap; exclusion of capital gains, wages, and guaranteed payments from QBI; OBBBA permanence.

2. **IRS Instructions for Form 8995 (2025 tax year)**
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8995
   Supports: 2025 income thresholds ($394,600 MFJ / $197,300 others for phase-in start; $494,600 MFJ / $247,300 others for phase-in end); Form 8995 vs. Form 8995-A usage rules; SSTB list; Form 1040 line 13a entry.

3. **IRS Instructions for Form 8995-A (2025 tax year)**
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8995a
   Supports: W-2 wage limitation tests (50 percent and 25 percent + 2.5 percent UBIA); how W-2 wage limitations apply above the income threshold; treatment of losses within the phase-in range.

4. **One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) -- Section 70105 amending Section 199A**
   Referenced via: IRS.gov OBBBA provisions page (https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/one-big-beautiful-bill-provisions); RSM US analysis (https://rsmus.com/insights/services/business-tax/obbba-tax-qbi-deduction.html); Foster.com Larry's Tax Law analysis (https://www.foster.com/larry-s-tax-law/one-big-beautiful-bill-act-part-4-qualified-business-income-deduction-code-section-199a); Warren Averett analysis (https://warrenaverett.com/insights/one-big-beautiful-bill-breakdown-qualified-business-income/)
   Supports: Permanence of deduction effective for tax years after December 31, 2025; expanded phase-in windows ($75,000 / $150,000); new $400 minimum deduction for taxpayers with $1,000+ active QBI; effective date; no change to SSTB list or 20 percent rate.

5. **IRS Revenue Procedure 2019-38 (Rental Real Estate Safe Harbor)**
   URL: https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-19-38.pdf
   Supports: Safe harbor requirements for rental real estate to qualify as a trade or business for QBI purposes.

6. **26 CFR Section 1.199A-1 through 1.199A-6 (Treasury Regulations under Section 199A)**
   Referenced in IRS SSTB guidance and Form 8995-A instructions
   Supports: SSTB definitions; material participation standards; W-2 wage determination rules.

7. **GYF (Gelman, Rosenberg and Freedman CPAs) -- "Tax Planning Strategies: Section 199A (QBI) Deduction" (April 2026)**
   URL: https://gyf.com/2026/04/tax-planning-strategies-section-199a-qbi-deduction/
   Supports: Projected 2026 inflation-adjusted thresholds ($403,500/$553,500 MFJ; $201,750/$276,750 others) -- these are professional projections and should be confirmed against final IRS guidance.

**Note on 2026 thresholds:** The IRS publishes final inflation-adjusted figures for Section 199A annually in a Revenue Procedure (historically released in late October or November of the preceding year). At the time of writing, the 2026 thresholds referenced in source 7 ($403,500 and $553,500 for MFJ) had not been independently verified against a readable version of the final official IRS Rev Proc for 2026. Confirm these specific figures with your advisor before relying on them for planning or filing.
