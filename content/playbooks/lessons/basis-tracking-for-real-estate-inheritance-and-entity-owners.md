# Basis Tracking for Real Estate, Inheritance, and Entity Owners

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Your tax basis (the IRS's term for your "cost" in a property or investment) is the starting line for calculating every gain or loss when you sell. For real estate, inherited assets, and entity interests, the rules that set and adjust basis are specific, cumulative, and easy to get wrong. Tracking basis correctly from the day you acquire a property through the day you sell it can mean the difference between paying the right amount of tax and overpaying by tens of thousands of dollars, or facing penalties for understating a gain. This playbook walks you through every scenario step by step so you can build and maintain accurate records.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is for you if any of the following describe your situation:

- You own, or are purchasing, residential rental property, commercial real estate, or land.
- You have inherited real estate or a financial interest in an entity (such as a share of a partnership or S-corporation) from a deceased family member or business partner.
- You received real estate or an entity interest as a gift.
- You are a partner in a partnership (including an LLC taxed as a partnership) or a shareholder in an S-corporation that owns real estate or other depreciable property.
- You have claimed depreciation deductions on property you own and need to understand how depreciation affects your taxable gain when you sell.
- You are planning to sell or transfer real estate and want to minimize your tax bill by confirming your correct basis before the transaction closes.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not use this playbook as your only guidance if any of the following apply:

- **C-corporation shareholders.** C-corporation stock basis follows different rules (no pass-through income or loss adjustments). Consult your advisor and IRS Publication 542.
- **Qualified Opportunity Zone investments.** Basis in Opportunity Zone fund interests is deferred and follows specialized rules under IRC Section 1400Z-2.
- **Properties held in a foreign country.** International basis and reporting rules (FBAR, FATCA, Form 8938) add layers not covered here.
- **Property received in a 1031 like-kind exchange.** Exchanged property carries over a "substituted basis" under IRC Section 1031(d) that requires a separate chain of calculations. This playbook covers original-purchase and inherited basis only; flag any 1031 exchange to your advisor before proceeding.
- **Property held in a revocable living trust.** During your lifetime, basis in a revocable trust is the same as your individual basis. However, distribution rules on death can vary. Confirm with your advisor.
- **Estates that did not file Form 706.** If the estate was below the filing threshold, stepped-up basis still applies under IRC Section 1014, but there is no Form 8971 to rely on for the exact figure. You and your advisor must reconstruct fair market value at the date of death using appraisals or comparable sales data.

## Key Terms

**Basis (also called "cost basis" or "tax basis"):** The amount the IRS uses as your starting investment in a property. When you sell, your taxable gain equals selling price minus basis. A higher basis means a smaller taxable gain.

**Adjusted basis:** Your original basis after all required increases (improvements, capital expenditures) and decreases (depreciation, casualty losses, certain credits) have been applied over the life of ownership.

**Stepped-up basis:** When you inherit property, the IRS resets your basis to the property's fair market value on the date of the original owner's death -- even if the original owner paid far less. The "step up" erases the gain that built up during the owner's lifetime.

**Stepped-down basis:** The same rule that creates a step-up can also create a step-down if the property declined in value before death. Your inherited basis would then be lower than the decedent's cost.

**Fair market value (FMV):** The price at which a property would change hands between a willing buyer and a willing seller, neither under compulsion to buy or sell, and both having reasonable knowledge of the relevant facts. This is the standard used to set inherited basis.

**Depreciation:** A tax deduction that spreads the cost of a building (not land) over its IRS-assigned useful life. Residential rental buildings are depreciated over 27.5 years; commercial (nonresidential) buildings over 39 years. Both use the straight-line method under MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System). Depreciation reduces your adjusted basis each year.

**MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System):** The IRS depreciation system that applies to most property placed in service after 1986. It assigns a recovery period and depreciation method to each type of property.

**Depreciation recapture:** When you sell a property on which you claimed depreciation, the IRS taxes some of that gain as ordinary income instead of capital gain, "recapturing" the benefit of the deductions. For real estate held more than one year, this is called "unrecaptured Section 1250 gain" and is taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25%.

**Section 1250 property:** IRS shorthand for depreciable real property (buildings and structural components). Most rental and commercial real estate falls here.

**Section 1245 property:** Depreciable personal property and certain other non-real-property assets (equipment, machinery, some improvements). Depreciation recapture on Section 1245 property is taxed as ordinary income at your marginal rate.

**Capital improvements:** Expenditures that add value to the property, extend its useful life, or adapt it to a new use. A new roof, an addition, new HVAC, and a deck are capital improvements. Routine repairs (fixing a leaky faucet, painting) are not -- they are expensed and do not increase basis.

**Outside basis (partnerships):** A partner's tax basis in their partnership interest, tracked at the individual level. Outside basis increases when you contribute money or property or your share of partnership income increases, and decreases when you receive distributions or your share of losses is allocated to you.

**Inside basis (partnerships):** The partnership's tax basis in its own assets. Inside basis and outside basis are related but are tracked separately. This playbook focuses on outside basis, which is what you need to determine your deductible losses.

**S-corporation stock basis:** An S-corporation shareholder's tax basis in their shares, which works similarly to partnership outside basis. It increases with income pass-throughs and decreases with loss pass-throughs and distributions.

**Debt basis (S-corporations):** When an S-corporation shareholder personally lends money directly to the corporation, that loan creates an additional layer of basis called debt basis, which can absorb losses beyond the stock basis amount.

**Form 4797 (Sales of Business Property):** The IRS form used to report the sale or disposition of real property used in a trade or business. Most rental and commercial property sales flow through Form 4797 to your individual return.

**Form 8949 (Sales and Other Dispositions of Capital Assets):** The IRS form used to report individual capital asset sales, including personal-use real property. Feeds into Schedule D.

**Schedule D (Capital Gains and Losses):** The IRS schedule on Form 1040 that summarizes capital gains and losses for the year.

**Form 7203 (S Corporation Shareholder Stock and Debt Basis Limitations):** The IRS form S-corporation shareholders use to calculate and report stock and debt basis, and to determine deductible losses.

**Form 8971 (Information Regarding Beneficiaries Acquiring Property from a Decedent):** The form an estate executor sends to each beneficiary when a Form 706 (estate tax return) has been filed. Schedule A (attached to Form 8971) tells you the final estate-tax value of the property you inherited -- which becomes your basis.

**Form 706 (United States Estate and Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax Return):** The estate tax return filed for large estates. For 2026, estates with a gross value at or below $15,000,000 generally do not need to file Form 706. When Form 706 is filed, the estate tax values become the baseline for consistent-basis reporting.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A 3.8% additional federal tax on the lesser of (1) your net investment income or (2) the amount by which your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). Gains from selling real estate can trigger the NIIT. The NIIT threshold is not inflation-adjusted.

**Recourse vs. nonrecourse debt (partnerships):** Recourse debt is debt for which you are personally liable. Nonrecourse debt is secured only by the property. The distinction matters because it determines how partnership liabilities are allocated among partners and, therefore, how much basis each partner receives from the partnership's borrowing.

**Alternate valuation date:** An option available to the executor of a large estate. Instead of valuing inherited assets at the date of death, the executor may elect to value them six months after death if doing so reduces both the gross estate value and the estate tax owed. This election affects the beneficiary's inherited basis.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather all of the following before starting your basis calculation. Do not begin tracking without these.

**For purchased real estate:**
- Closing disclosure or HUD-1 Settlement Statement from the original purchase
- Mortgage or deed of trust (to confirm purchase price and any assumed debt)
- Records of all capital improvements since purchase (invoices, contractor receipts, permits)
- All prior-year depreciation schedules (from tax returns or your accountant's workpapers), or the property's prior tax returns (Schedule E)
- Records of any partial dispositions (e.g., you replaced the roof and the old roof was separately depreciated)
- Any insurance reimbursements or casualty loss deductions taken
- Records of any easements granted or sold

**For inherited real estate:**
- Date of the decedent's death
- Schedule A (Form 8971) from the estate, if a Form 706 was filed
- If no Form 706 was filed: a qualified appraisal of the property as of the date of death, or comparable sales data documenting FMV at that date
- Copy of any alternate valuation date election made by the estate executor
- Name of the state where the decedent resided (community property vs. common law state distinction matters)

**For gifted real estate:**
- The donor's adjusted basis at the time of the gift
- The FMV of the property at the time of the gift
- Any gift tax paid (Form 709 filed by the donor), to determine if a basis addition for gift tax applies
- Date of the gift

**For partnership interests:**
- All Schedule K-1 (Form 1065) forms from every year you have held the interest
- Your original contribution amount (cash or property, and if property, the adjusted basis of that property)
- Any cash or property distributions received each year
- The partnership's allocation of recourse and nonrecourse liabilities (shown on Schedule K-1, Box 20 or the supplemental basis worksheet)

**For S-corporation stock:**
- All Schedule K-1 (Form 1120-S) forms from every year you have held the shares
- Your original stock purchase price or contributed amount
- Any loans you personally made directly to the S-corporation (with promissory notes documenting them)
- Any prior Form 7203 worksheets

**Retention rule:** The IRS generally has three years from the date you file your return to audit it, and six years if it believes you understated income by more than 25%. For basis records, the IRS can challenge your basis as long as the property is still open to audit -- which means basis records for a property you have owned for 20 years need to survive for all 20 years plus the audit window after sale. Keep everything.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Part 1: Setting Your Starting Basis

**Step 1. Identify how you acquired the property.**

The rule for your starting basis depends entirely on how you came to own the property. Identify which of the four acquisition methods below applies:

- A. You **purchased** the property.
- B. You **inherited** the property from someone who died.
- C. You **received** the property as a **gift** (no payment, or payment less than FMV).
- D. You **contributed** the property to a **partnership or S-corporation**, or you acquired a **partnership interest or S-corporation shares** for cash or property.

**Step 2A (Purchased property). Determine your cost basis.**

Under IRC Section 1012, your starting basis equals everything you paid to acquire the property, including:

- The purchase price stated on the closing disclosure
- Closing costs you paid that are capitalized (not deducted): abstract fees, legal fees for title examination, recording fees, surveys, transfer taxes, owner's title insurance premium
- Assumed seller obligations: if you assumed the seller's mortgage or back property taxes, include those amounts
- The full mortgage amount: if you financed $400,000 of an $500,000 purchase, your starting basis is $500,000 -- not $100,000 cash paid

Do NOT include in your starting basis: loan origination fees (points), mortgage insurance premiums, prepaid interest, appraisal fees for the lender, fire insurance premiums, rent paid before closing, or utility charges before closing.

Action: Add up every eligible line from your closing disclosure. Record this number as "Original Cost Basis -- [Property Address] -- [Acquisition Date]."

**Step 2B (Inherited property). Determine your stepped-up (or stepped-down) basis.**

Under IRC Section 1014, your basis in inherited property is generally the fair market value of the property on the date of the decedent's death.

Sub-step 2B-1. Check whether the estate filed Form 706. If yes, locate Schedule A (Form 8971) sent to you by the executor. The value in Part II, column (h) of that Schedule A is your basis. You are legally required to use a basis no higher than that figure (IRC Section 1014(f) and Section 6662 penalty rules apply).

Sub-step 2B-2. If no Form 706 was filed (the estate was below the filing threshold): obtain a qualified appraisal dated as of the date of death, or compile at least three comparable sales in the same market around the same date to document the FMV. Your basis is that FMV. Keep the appraisal permanently.

Sub-step 2B-3. If the executor elected the alternate valuation date (six months after death): your basis is the FMV on the alternate valuation date, not the date of death. Check the Form 706 or ask the executor.

Sub-step 2B-4. Community property rule: If the deceased was your spouse and the property was community property in a community property state (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, or Wisconsin), both halves of the community property generally receive a stepped-up basis to FMV at death -- even the half you already owned. Confirm this with your advisor and document the FMV for both halves.

Sub-step 2B-5. Exception -- one-year lookback: If you or your spouse gave the property to the decedent within one year before the decedent's death and the decedent left it back to you, your basis is NOT stepped up. Instead, your basis equals the decedent's adjusted basis just before death. This prevents a circular step-up maneuver.

Action: Record "Inherited Basis -- [Property Address] -- [Date of Death of Decedent] -- Basis = $[FMV]." Attach the Form 8971 Schedule A or appraisal.

**Step 2C (Gifted property). Determine your carryover or split basis.**

The rules for gifted property depend on whether the property had built-in gain or built-in loss at the time of the gift, under IRC Section 1015:

- If the FMV at the time of the gift was EQUAL TO OR GREATER THAN the donor's adjusted basis: your basis is the donor's adjusted basis (carryover basis). You inherit their low basis and their potential gain.
- If the FMV at the time of the gift was LESS THAN the donor's adjusted basis (a "built-in loss" gift): the rules split:
  - For calculating GAIN on a future sale: your basis is the donor's adjusted basis.
  - For calculating LOSS on a future sale: your basis is the lower FMV at the time of gift.
  - If your eventual sale price falls between the two figures, you recognize neither gain nor loss.

Gift tax addition: If the gift occurred after 1976 and the donor paid gift tax on it, you may add a portion of that gift tax to your basis. The formula is: (Gift tax paid) x (Net appreciation in the gift / Taxable gift amount). Your advisor and the donor's Form 709 are needed to calculate this.

Action: Obtain the donor's adjusted basis from the donor or from the donor's tax advisor. Record "Gifted Property Basis -- [Property Address] -- [Date of Gift] -- Donor's Basis = $[X] -- FMV at Gift = $[Y]."

**Step 2D (Partnership interest or S-corporation shares). Determine your initial outside basis or stock basis.**

Partnership interest (outside basis): Under IRC Section 722, your starting outside basis equals the money you contributed plus the adjusted basis of any property you contributed. If you were allocated a share of the partnership's liabilities at admission, that increases your basis further.

S-corporation stock: Your starting stock basis equals what you paid for the shares (purchase price or the amount you contributed to capitalize the entity).

Action: Record the initial contribution or purchase amount. This is your Year 1 starting outside basis or stock basis.

### Part 2: Adjusting Basis Over Time

**Step 3. Apply annual adjustments to your basis.**

Basis is not static. You must update it every year. The adjustments below apply for each year you own the property.

**Increases to adjusted basis (real property):**

- Cost of capital improvements (additions, new roof, HVAC replacement, landscaping projects that add value, new driveway, built-in appliances). Keep every receipt.
- Assessments paid for local improvements that benefit the property (new sidewalk, water main connection, street paving) if the assessment is capitalized to the property rather than expensed.
- Legal fees to defend or perfect title to the property.
- Amounts spent to restore property after a casualty loss that exceed insurance reimbursements (the excess becomes new basis).
- Zoning costs to change the classification of the property.

**Decreases to adjusted basis (real property):**

- Depreciation you took (or could have taken) on the property. This is the most common and most significant basis reduction. Use the depreciation schedule from your tax return each year.
- Insurance or other reimbursements you received for casualty or theft losses.
- Energy credits taken at the property level that required a basis reduction.
- Proceeds from selling an easement (e.g., you let a utility company run lines across your property).
- Any Section 179 deduction or special depreciation allowance (bonus depreciation) taken on property components.

**Step 4. Track depreciation meticulously -- it reduces basis whether you take it or not.**

The IRS reduces your adjusted basis by depreciation "taken or allowable" -- meaning if you forgot to claim depreciation in a prior year, the IRS still treats your basis as if you did. If you have missed depreciation deductions, file Form 3115 (Application for Change in Accounting Method) to catch up and restore the correct basis record. Do not simply add back missed depreciation without that form -- your advisor must handle this.

Key depreciation periods to know:
- Residential rental buildings: 27.5 years, straight-line method, mid-month convention
- Nonresidential (commercial) buildings: 39 years, straight-line method, mid-month convention
- Land: never depreciated (basis stays constant until sale)
- Certain improvements (land improvements such as parking lots, fences, landscaping): 15 years
- Appliances and personal property placed in rental units: 5 or 7 years under MACRS (check with your advisor for the specific category)

**Step 5. Adjust outside basis or stock basis annually for entity interests.**

**Partnership outside basis:** Each year, after you receive your Schedule K-1 (Form 1065):

Increase for: your share of ordinary income, separately stated income items (capital gains, interest, etc.), tax-exempt income, and any increase in your share of partnership liabilities.

Decrease for: your share of ordinary losses, separately stated loss items, nondeductible expenses, cash or property distributions, and any decrease in your share of partnership liabilities. Basis cannot go below zero.

If allocated losses exceed your outside basis in a given year, those losses are suspended and carried forward until you have sufficient basis to absorb them (IRC Section 704(d)).

**S-corporation stock basis:** Each year, after you receive your Schedule K-1 (Form 1120-S), apply adjustments in this required order (IRC Section 1367):

1. Increase for all income items (including tax-exempt income and excess depletion).
2. Decrease for non-dividend distributions.
3. Decrease for nondeductible, non-capital expenses.
4. Decrease for loss and deduction items.

If losses exceed your stock basis, check your debt basis (personal loans you made to the corporation). If you still have debt basis, losses can absorb that too. Losses exceeding both stock and debt basis are suspended and carry forward.

Use Form 7203 to document the calculation each year, even in years when filing is not technically required.

### Part 3: Selling or Disposing of the Property

**Step 6. Calculate your adjusted basis at the time of sale.**

Pull your basis ledger (see Using The Download(s) below). Confirm:
- Original basis (from Step 2)
- Plus all increases recorded in Step 3
- Minus all depreciation taken or allowable through the date of sale (Step 3 and Step 4)

This is your adjusted basis.

**Step 7. Identify how gain will be categorized.**

When you sell real estate held for more than one year and used in a trade or business:

- Gain up to the amount of straight-line depreciation previously taken is "unrecaptured Section 1250 gain" -- taxed at a maximum 25% federal rate.
- Gain above the total depreciation taken and above your original cost basis is long-term capital gain -- taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your taxable income (see The Numbers section).
- If the property had any appreciation in excess of straight-line depreciation (uncommon for real property placed in service after 1986 since straight-line is required), that excess is ordinary income.

The mechanics of reporting: You report the sale on Form 4797 (Sales of Business Property) for rental or business real estate. Your advisor or tax preparer will flow the results from Form 4797 to Schedule D (Form 1040).

For personal-use property (your primary home or vacation home): report on Form 8949 and Schedule D.

**Step 8. Check for the Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT).**

If your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), your gain from the real estate sale may also be subject to the 3.8% NIIT. Your advisor will calculate this on Form 8960. Budget for it when estimating your total tax liability from a sale.

**Step 9. If you are selling a partnership interest or S-corporation shares, run a final basis calculation before closing.**

Your gain or loss on a sale of a partnership interest or S-corporation shares depends on your final adjusted outside basis or stock basis at the time of sale. A common mistake is selling without updating the basis for the current tax year's K-1 income and distributions. Wait for (or estimate) the current-year K-1 before finalizing your basis and your tax exposure.

---

### Part 4: Post-Sale and Ongoing

**Step 10. File required forms and archive records after the sale.**

- Report the disposition on Form 4797 or Form 8949 (or both if the property has mixed-use components).
- Retain all basis records -- closing documents, depreciation schedules, improvement receipts -- for at least three years after the return is filed, but ideally for seven years given the six-year statute of limitations for substantial omissions.
- If you inherited the property and received a Form 8971 Schedule A, retain that document indefinitely.

**Step 11. Establish a basis tracking ledger for any new property you acquire.**

Start your ledger on the date of acquisition. Record the starting basis, add every capital improvement as it occurs, and subtract depreciation at the end of every tax year. Do not wait until you are selling to reconstruct this history. See Using The Download(s) for the worksheet.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**State tax:** Most states follow federal basis rules for real property, but several states have their own rules for depreciation, bonus depreciation, and Section 179 that create differences between your federal and state adjusted basis. California, for example, does not conform to federal bonus depreciation. If you own property in a non-conforming state, you may have separate state basis records. Confirm with your advisor which states are affected.

**Community property states:** If you live in Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, or Wisconsin, the community property rules can give your spouse's half of jointly owned property a stepped-up basis at your death (and vice versa), which can be a significant estate planning opportunity. This rule is explicitly preserved under IRC Section 1014(b)(6). Confirm with your advisor whether your property qualifies as community property under your state's law.

**One Big, Beautiful Bill impact (2026):** The One Big, Beautiful Bill, signed July 4, 2025 (Pub. L. 119-21), increased the federal estate tax exclusion to $15,000,000 beginning in 2026. This means fewer estates will file Form 706, which in turn means fewer beneficiaries will receive a formal Schedule A (Form 8971). If you inherit property in 2026 or later and no Form 706 is filed, you must document fair market value at death through a qualified appraisal -- do not skip this step.

**S-corporation at-risk rules:** In addition to the stock and debt basis rules, S-corporation shareholders must also pass the "at-risk" rules under IRC Section 465 before losses are deductible. Basis and at-risk amounts are related but not identical. Your Form 7203 worksheet handles both, but consult your advisor if you have non-recourse debt in the S-corp picture.

**Partnership liability allocations and basis:** In partnerships with complex debt structures (construction loans, preferred equity, tiered partnerships), the allocation of recourse and nonrecourse liabilities among partners can be highly technical and can shift basis dramatically year to year. Do not rely on a rough estimate -- insist that the partnership provide a formal outside basis schedule with each K-1.

**Passive activity losses and basis interaction:** Basis limits and passive activity loss limits are separate tests. You need sufficient basis AND your loss must not be passive (or you must have sufficient passive income) before you can deduct it. Losses that fail the basis test are suspended; losses that fail the passive activity test are also suspended but for a different reason. When you sell a property or entity interest, all suspended passive losses from that activity are released. Track these separately on Form 8582 (Passive Activity Loss Limitations).

**Depreciation on partial-year acquisitions:** In the year of acquisition, the mid-month convention for real property means you get a partial year of depreciation. Your depreciation schedule will show a smaller deduction in Year 1 and Year N (the year of sale) than in full years. Make sure your basis calculation accounts for this.

**Inherited property in a trust:** If inherited property passes through a trust before reaching you as a beneficiary, the stepped-up basis rules still apply, but the trustee's timing of distribution and accounting of income between principal and income can affect your basis records. Get a complete accounting from the trustee.

**No like-kind exchange coverage:** If you have ever done a 1031 like-kind exchange involving a property in your portfolio, that property carries a "substituted basis" (the basis of the relinquished property is rolled into the replacement property). This can result in a much lower basis than purchase price suggests. Flag any property you acquired through a 1031 exchange for a separate basis analysis before selling.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Using purchase price as basis when you've claimed years of depreciation.**
The most costly mistake. A property purchased for $400,000 with $100,000 in cumulative depreciation has an adjusted basis of $300,000, not $400,000. Using the wrong number understates your gain and triggers penalties.

**2. Forgetting to include closing costs in original basis.**
Legal fees, transfer taxes, title insurance, and recording fees paid at closing are all part of your original cost basis. Missing them means your basis is too low from Day 1.

**3. Treating repairs as capital improvements (or vice versa).**
Routine maintenance (fixing a broken window, repainting walls) is an expense, not a basis increase. Only improvements that add value, extend life, or adapt the property to a new use are capitalized. Over-capitalizing inflates your records; under-capitalizing understates your basis.

**4. Not tracking basis for entity interests year by year.**
Many S-corp shareholders and partners go years without updating their outside basis or stock basis, then are surprised to learn that suspended losses they thought were deductible have no basis to absorb them, or that a distribution was actually taxable income because basis was already zero.

**5. Assuming inherited basis equals the decedent's original cost.**
Stepped-up basis is the fair market value at death, not what the decedent originally paid. A property bought for $50,000 in 1975 might have a stepped-up basis of $850,000 in 2026. Using the wrong number vastly overstates the gain.

**6. Missing the one-year lookback rule on gifts-to-decedent-back-to-donor.**
If you gave property to someone and they died and left it back to you within one year, the step-up does not apply. The basis is the decedent's adjusted basis. Advisors and clients often overlook this rule.

**7. Not obtaining a qualified appraisal at the date of death when no Form 706 is filed.**
With the 2026 estate tax exclusion at $15,000,000, most estates will not file Form 706. That means no official Schedule A (Form 8971). Without a contemporaneous appraisal, you will be reconstructing FMV years later when data is stale and comparable sales are harder to find. Get the appraisal promptly after the date of death.

**8. Failing to account for suspended losses when selling an entity interest.**
When you sell a partnership or S-corporation interest, suspended passive activity losses from that activity become deductible. Not knowing you have suspended losses means you may sell without realizing you are sitting on a deduction.

**9. Ignoring the NIIT when estimating proceeds from a sale.**
On a large real estate gain, the 3.8% NIIT on top of capital gains rates and state taxes can add thousands to your tax bill. Do not underestimate proceeds without running the NIIT calculation.

**10. Letting the basis ledger go unrecorded in real time.**
It is exponentially harder to reconstruct 15 years of capital improvements from old emails and faded receipts than it is to keep a running ledger updated as you go. Start the ledger on Day 1.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before acting if any of the following conditions are present:

1. **You are selling a property you have owned for more than a few years and have not reconciled your depreciation schedule.** The tax cost of using the wrong basis can exceed the cost of professional review many times over.

2. **You inherited property and are unsure whether a Form 706 was filed.** Contact the estate executor or estate attorney to find out. Your basis depends on the answer.

3. **You inherited property in a community property state.** The double step-up is a significant benefit that requires confirmation of the property's community property character under state law.

4. **You received gifted property and the donor's basis is unknown.** Basis in gifted property cannot be assumed. You must obtain the donor's records.

5. **You have done a 1031 like-kind exchange at any point in the property's history.** Substituted basis chains can be complex; do not guess.

6. **You have claimed (or should have claimed) bonus depreciation or Section 179 on property components.** These accelerate basis reduction and require careful accounting at sale.

7. **Your partnership K-1 shows a share of liabilities that changed significantly this year.** Liability shifts can cause unexpected taxable income (if your liability share decreased) or additional basis (if it increased).

8. **You are planning to sell and your MAGI will exceed the NIIT threshold.** Model the total federal plus state tax before signing a purchase agreement.

9. **You are selling an S-corp interest or partnership interest.** Outside basis and stock basis calculations should be reviewed for accuracy before closing; errors are extremely difficult to correct after the fact.

10. **You are a beneficiary who received a Form 8971 Schedule A.** Confirm with your advisor that your intended basis matches the value in column (h) to avoid the 20% consistency penalty.

## Before You Act

Basis errors are among the most consequential and hardest-to-correct mistakes in individual tax filings. The calculations in this playbook are designed to give you a complete framework and accurate reference points. However, the correct basis for YOUR specific property depends on facts -- the exact purchase price, which improvements were made, whether a Form 706 was filed, whether your state has depreciation conformity, and dozens of other details that only you and your records can supply.

**Before you file a tax return that includes a property sale, elect any tax treatment, distribute property from an entity, or transfer property in any way, confirm your specific basis calculation with your advisor.** Do not rely on a round number or an estimate. Do not assume that basis equals purchase price if you have owned the property for more than one year. Do not assume that inherited basis equals the decedent's purchase price.

The cost of getting this right with professional review is a fraction of the cost of an IRS audit, accuracy penalty, or amended return.
