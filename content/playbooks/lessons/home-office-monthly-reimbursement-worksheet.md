# Home Office Monthly Reimbursement Worksheet

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

When a business owner operates as an S-corporation (a corporation that passes its income and deductions through to the owners' personal tax returns) and works from home, the corporation can reimburse the owner-employee for the business portion of actual home costs -- rent or mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, and more -- tax-free, with no payroll tax on either side. The reimbursement is paid through an accountable plan (a written IRS-approved arrangement explained in Key Terms below), which requires the owner to calculate the correct amount each month using their home office square footage percentage, document the supporting expenses, and submit a signed reimbursement form before the company issues a check. This worksheet is that monthly form: it guides you through the calculation, captures all required documentation, and creates the audit record the IRS expects if your home office deduction is ever questioned.

## Who This Applies To

This lesson and its download apply to:

- **S-corporation owner-employees** who use a dedicated, exclusive area of their home regularly for business, who are on W-2 payroll from the S-corp, and whose S-corp has adopted a written accountable plan before any reimbursement was made.
- **C-corporation owner-employees** in the same situation (W-2, written accountable plan in place, dedicated home office).
- **W-2 employees of any corporation or partnership** who maintain a qualifying home office for the convenience of the employer and whose employer has an accountable plan covering home office costs.
- **Any entity with employees** where the entity wants to reimburse home office costs and has not yet set up the monthly calculation and documentation routine this worksheet provides.

You do NOT need to be a homeowner. Renters qualify too, because rent, renters insurance, and utilities all count in the calculation.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs taxed as disregarded entities (not S-corps):** You are not your own employee, so no accountable plan can exist between you and your business. You claim home office on Schedule C (Form 1040) instead, using Form 8829 or the simplified method. This worksheet is not the right tool.
- **Partners in a partnership (for their own home office costs):** Partners are not employees of their own partnership under IRS rules. The accountable plan path is not available. Ask your advisor about guaranteed payments or other approaches.
- **Owners who have not yet set up a written accountable plan and corporate resolution:** The reimbursement arrangement must exist in writing and be adopted by the corporation before any money moves. If you have not done that yet, stop here and start with the "Accountable Plan Reimbursements" lesson first.
- **Spaces that fail the regular-and-exclusive-use test:** The home office must be used regularly (continuously and consistently, not occasionally) and exclusively (only for business -- no personal or family use of the same area) for business. A guest bedroom that doubles as an office fails this test. A dedicated room used only for business passes.
- **Owners not on W-2 payroll:** If the S-corp is not running payroll and issuing W-2s, an accountable plan cannot provide a tax-free reimbursement. Payroll must be running first.
- **Situations where the office is already being rented to the business (Augusta Rule / home-office-as-rental approach):** Those are separate strategies with different rules. Do not combine them.

## Key Terms

**S-corporation (S-corp):** A corporation that has elected to pass its income, deductions, and credits through to its shareholders' personal tax returns rather than paying corporate income tax. The IRS treats the owner as a W-2 employee of the S-corp for compensation purposes.

**Accountable plan:** A written employer reimbursement arrangement that meets three IRS tests -- (1) business connection, (2) substantiation, and (3) return of excess -- so that reimbursements are excluded from the employee's taxable wages and from payroll tax. Governed by Treasury Regulation 1.62-2 and Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 62(a)(2)(A). Reimbursements paid under a plan that fails any one of these three tests are treated as taxable wages and subject to income tax withholding and FICA (Social Security and Medicare taxes).

**Business connection:** The reimbursed expense must be a deductible business expense under IRC Section 162 (ordinary and necessary business expenses) that the employee paid while performing services as an employee of the company.

**Substantiation:** The employee must provide the employer with records showing the amount, date, and business purpose of each expense within a reasonable period of time. For home office costs, this means the monthly worksheet with supporting bills.

**Return of excess:** Any reimbursement that exceeds the employee's actual, substantiated expense must be returned to the employer within a reasonable period of time.

**Corporate resolution:** A written decision made by a corporation's board of directors. Before an S-corp begins reimbursing home office costs, the board (which may be you alone as sole director) must pass a resolution formally adopting the accountable plan. The resolution must be dated before the first reimbursement.

**Regular and exclusive use test:** Under IRC Section 280A, a home office qualifies only if the space is used regularly (continuously and consistently, not sporadically) and exclusively (for business only -- personal use of the same space, even occasionally, disqualifies it). Two limited exceptions exist: storage of inventory for retail or wholesale businesses, and licensed daycare facilities.

**Principal place of business:** Your home office qualifies for deduction (and reimbursement) if it is the primary location where you conduct the administrative or management activities of your business and you have no other fixed location where you conduct substantial administrative or management work. If you manage your business from home -- scheduling, emailing, bookkeeping, billing -- and have no other office, your home is your principal place of business even if you also meet clients elsewhere.

**Business-use percentage:** The fraction of your home's total finished square footage occupied by the dedicated home office space. For example, a 200-square-foot office in a 2,000-square-foot home produces a 10% business-use percentage. This percentage is applied to all indirect expenses (costs that benefit the entire home) to calculate the reimbursable share.

**Indirect expenses:** Home costs that benefit the entire property and are allocated using the business-use percentage. Examples: mortgage interest or rent, property taxes, homeowners or renters insurance, utilities (electricity, gas, water, trash, sewer), general repairs and maintenance (roof, HVAC, plumbing).

**Direct expenses:** Costs that apply only to the home office space itself, deductible at 100%. Examples: painting only the office room, carpet installed only in the office, a dedicated business phone line in the office.

**Home office depreciation:** An annual deduction that represents the wear on the portion of the home used for business. The home office portion of the building (not the land) is depreciated over 39 years using the straight-line method (equal amounts each year) under IRC Section 168. Depreciation reduces the home's tax basis and triggers depreciation recapture tax (up to 25% under IRC Section 1250) when the home is sold -- even if you never claimed the depreciation. This is a complex calculation and must be handled by your advisor. Because of the recapture risk, some owners choose not to include depreciation in the monthly reimbursement; confirm the right approach for your situation with your advisor.

**Depreciation recapture:** When you sell a home that was used partly for business, the IRS taxes any gain attributable to depreciation that was "allowed or allowable" (meaning whether you took it or not) at a maximum federal rate of 25% under IRC Section 1250. The IRC Section 121 home sale exclusion (which shelters up to $250,000 or $500,000 of gain for a primary residence) does not protect the depreciated portion.

**FICA:** Federal Insurance Contributions Act taxes -- Social Security and Medicare taxes paid by both employer and employee on wages. The combined employer-side rate is 7.65% (6.2% Social Security plus 1.45% Medicare). Accountable plan reimbursements are exempt from FICA.

**W-2:** IRS Form W-2, Wage and Tax Statement. Properly substantiated accountable plan reimbursements are not included as wages in Box 1 of the W-2.

**IRC Section 280A:** The Internal Revenue Code section that governs when expenses related to business use of a home are deductible. It imposes the regular-and-exclusive-use test and the principal-place-of-business requirement.

**Actual expense method:** The required calculation method for home office reimbursements through an employer accountable plan. You calculate the actual costs of operating the home for the month, multiply the indirect expenses by the business-use percentage, and add 100% of any direct expenses. Revenue Procedure 2013-13, which established the simplified option ($5 per square foot), explicitly excludes employees receiving reimbursements under an accountable plan from using the simplified method. The simplified method is available only for self-employed taxpayers claiming the deduction on Schedule C.

**Simplified method (not available here):** An IRS optional method under Revenue Procedure 2013-13 that allows self-employed taxpayers to deduct $5 per square foot of home office space (maximum 300 square feet, maximum $1,500 per year) without tracking actual expenses. This method is NOT available for employer reimbursements through an accountable plan. You must use the actual expense method.

**Safe harbor timing windows:** The IRS provides three bright-line windows that automatically qualify as a "reasonable period of time" under Treasury Regulation 1.62-2(g): (1) you may receive an advance no more than 30 days before the expense is incurred; (2) you must submit substantiation (the completed worksheet and supporting documents) no more than 60 days after the expense is paid or incurred; (3) you must return any excess reimbursement no more than 120 days after the expense is paid or incurred.

**Form 8829:** The IRS tax form used by sole proprietors (Schedule C filers) to calculate the home office deduction. S-corp owner-employees using an accountable plan do not use Form 8829 for their own personal return. The corporation deducts the reimbursed amount as a business expense on its return (Form 1120S). Your advisor handles the corporate-return deduction.

**Form 1120S:** The annual income tax return filed by an S-corporation. The home office reimbursements paid to the owner-employee are deducted here as business expenses.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before completing the first monthly worksheet. Once gathered, most will stay the same month to month.

1. **Home office square footage:** Measure the dedicated office space in square feet (length times width of the room or area used exclusively for business). Round to the nearest whole square foot.
2. **Total home finished square footage:** The total finished (livable) square footage of your home, excluding unfinished basement, garage, and utility spaces. Check your property records, lease, or a prior appraisal if you do not know the number.
3. **Business-use percentage:** Divide the home office square feet by the total home square feet. Express as a percentage rounded to two decimal places. You will use this same percentage every month until your home arrangement changes.
4. **Confirmation of exclusive use:** Verify that the office space has no personal use. If family members use the space for personal activities, it does not qualify.
5. **Monthly home expense amounts:** Collect the actual cost of each of the following for the month you are filing:
   - Rent payment (if renting) OR the interest portion only of your mortgage payment (principal is not deductible). If you do not know the interest portion, consult your monthly mortgage statement or amortization schedule.
   - Property taxes paid during the month (if applicable; some owners pay these annually through escrow).
   - Homeowners or renters insurance premium for the month.
   - Utilities for the month: electricity, gas, water, sewer, trash. Obtain the actual bills.
   - General repairs and maintenance paid during the month that benefit the whole home (e.g., HVAC servicing, roof repair, plumbing).
   - Any direct expenses for the office only (e.g., paint, carpet, dedicated phone line).
6. **Depreciation amount for the office (if applicable):** This must be calculated by your advisor. If you are not including depreciation, note that in the worksheet. See the Key Terms section for why this decision matters at the time of home sale.
7. **Confirmation of payroll status:** Confirm your S-corp is currently running W-2 payroll and that you are on payroll as an employee.
8. **Corporate resolution and written accountable plan:** Confirm these documents exist, are signed and dated before the first reimbursement, and are filed in your corporate records. If they do not exist, complete the "Accountable Plan Reimbursements" lesson setup steps before submitting any reimbursement.

## Required Documents And Records

### Documents to establish the plan (one-time, keep permanently)

| Document | Purpose | When to create |
|---|---|---|
| Written accountable plan policy | States which expenses qualify, documentation requirements, and the 60-day substantiation and 120-day return-of-excess deadlines | Before the first reimbursement |
| Corporate board resolution | Formal adoption of the accountable plan by the S-corp board | Dated before the first reimbursement |
| Home office measurement record | A diagram or sketch showing the room dimensions, square footage, and confirmation of exclusive business use | Once; update if you move or change the office space |

### Records to collect and keep for each monthly reimbursement

| Record | What it covers | Retention |
|---|---|---|
| Completed Home Office Monthly Reimbursement Worksheet | The signed monthly form showing the calculation and total reimbursement requested | Minimum 4 years from the return due date |
| Mortgage statement (interest portion) or rent payment receipt | Shows the monthly housing cost | Minimum 4 years |
| Utility bills (electricity, gas, water, sewer, trash) | Each bill for the month | Minimum 4 years |
| Insurance premium statement or payment confirmation | Monthly or pro-rated annual premium | Minimum 4 years |
| Receipts for repairs and maintenance | Each invoice or receipt for work done | Minimum 4 years |
| Receipts for direct expenses (if any) | Invoices for work or materials applying only to the office | Minimum 4 years |
| Proof of reimbursement payment | Bank statement or canceled check showing the company paid the reimbursement | Minimum 4 years (employment tax records) |
| Depreciation schedule (if claiming) | Prepared by your advisor; shows annual depreciation amount and accumulated total | Keep until the property is sold, plus 4 years after the sale tax return is filed |

**Retention rule:** The standard IRS statute of limitations for auditing a return is 3 years from the filing date (IRC Section 6501(a)). Employment tax records (which include payroll-adjacent items like accountable plan reimbursements) require 4 years. Keep all home office reimbursement records for a minimum of 4 years from the due date of the return for that year. If you claim depreciation, keep the depreciation schedule and related records until the year you sell the home and the statute expires on that year's return.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Setup (do once before the first reimbursement)

**Step 1 -- Confirm eligibility with your advisor.**
Before running any numbers, confirm: (a) your entity is an S-corp or qualifying corporation, (b) you are on active W-2 payroll, (c) you have a dedicated home office that passes the regular-and-exclusive-use test, and (d) your home is your principal place of business (or you meet clients there, or it is a separate structure). Do not skip this step. If any of these conditions is not met, the reimbursements will not be excludable from income.

**Step 2 -- Establish the written accountable plan and corporate resolution.**
If you do not already have both documents: draft the written accountable plan policy (covering eligible expense categories, substantiation requirements, and the 60-day and 120-day deadlines) and pass a corporate resolution adopting it. Both documents must be signed and dated before any reimbursement is made. File them in your corporate records book. Your advisor can review the documents before you sign.

**Step 3 -- Measure your home office and calculate the business-use percentage.**
Measure the office room (or dedicated area) in square feet. Measure or obtain the total finished square footage of your home. Divide. Record the percentage. This is the number you will use every month until the office space changes.

Example: Office = 180 sq ft. Home = 1,800 sq ft. Business-use percentage = 180 / 1,800 = 10.00%.

**Step 4 -- Identify your monthly home cost categories.**
List every indirect home expense you pay each month (or on another regular schedule, prorated to a monthly amount): rent or mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, utilities, and regular maintenance. Determine where to find the actual amount for each cost every month (e.g., mortgage statement, utility app, insurance portal). Set up a recurring folder (physical or digital) where you store these bills each month.

**Step 5 -- Decide whether to include depreciation and confirm the amount with your advisor.**
Ask your advisor whether to include home office depreciation in the monthly reimbursement. If yes, your advisor will calculate the annual depreciation amount (home building cost basis times the business-use percentage, divided by 39 years). Divide the annual amount by 12 for the monthly reimbursement line item. Understand the recapture risk before proceeding (see Key Terms: Depreciation recapture).

### Phase 2: Monthly reimbursement cycle (recurring, every month)

Run this cycle within the first five business days of the month following the month you are reimbursing. That keeps you comfortably inside the 60-day substantiation safe harbor for expenses incurred throughout the prior month.

**Step 6 -- Collect all supporting documents for the month.**
Pull together:
- Mortgage statement (identify the interest line) or rent payment confirmation
- All utility bills for the month (electricity, gas, water, sewer, trash)
- Insurance premium for the month (or the monthly pro-rate of the annual premium)
- Any repair or maintenance invoices paid during the month
- Any direct office expenses (receipts for paint, carpet, equipment for the office only)

**Step 7 -- Complete the Home Office Monthly Reimbursement Worksheet (the attached download).**
Fill in every section of the worksheet. See "Using The Download(s)" below for a field-by-field guide. The worksheet will calculate:
- The indirect expense total for the month (sum of all indirect home costs)
- The business-use amount for indirect expenses (indirect total multiplied by your business-use percentage)
- The direct expense total (100% reimbursable)
- The depreciation amount for the month (if applicable)
- The grand total reimbursement requested for the month

**Step 8 -- Attach all supporting documentation to the worksheet.**
Physically attach (or save digitally in the same folder) every bill, receipt, and statement that supports the amounts on the worksheet. For expenses of $75 or more, a receipt or official statement is required under Treasury Regulation 1.274-5(c)(2)(iii). For smaller amounts, keep the documentation anyway -- the IRS may ask.

**Step 9 -- Sign the worksheet as employee and submit to the business.**
As the owner-employee, you sign the worksheet certifying that the expenses were incurred for business purposes and that you have attached all required documentation. You are submitting this to the corporation (even though you are the same person in both roles). If your corporation has another officer or administrator, route it through them. Keep a copy of the signed form for your personal records.

**Step 10 -- Issue the reimbursement from the business bank account.**
The corporation writes a check or initiates an ACH transfer from the business checking account to you (the employee) for the exact amount on the worksheet. The payment must come from a business account -- not your personal account. Record the payment in your accounting software as a business expense (category: home office expense) and NOT as wages, distributions, or an owner draw.

**Step 11 -- File the completed worksheet and all backup in your records.**
Store the signed worksheet and supporting documents in a dedicated folder (by tax year and month) for at least 4 years from the return due date. This is your audit file for this strategy.

**Step 12 -- Confirm no excess was advanced.**
If the corporation advanced any funds to you before the expenses were incurred, confirm that the advance did not exceed the substantiated amount. Return any excess to the business account within 120 days of the date you incurred the expense.

### Phase 3: Annual tasks

**Step 13 -- Reconcile total annual reimbursements at December 31.**
Add up all 12 monthly reimbursements for the year. Confirm each payment appears in the company's books as a business expense. The total will flow through to the S-corp tax return (Form 1120S) as an ordinary and necessary business expense.

**Step 14 -- Confirm the W-2 does not include home office reimbursements.**
Before your payroll provider finalizes your W-2 in January, verify that none of the monthly reimbursements were included in Box 1 (wages). They should not appear on the W-2 at all. If they were accidentally included, a corrected Form W-2 (Form W-2c) must be issued before you file the return.

**Step 15 -- Update the business-use percentage and cost figures if anything changed.**
If you moved, remodeled, changed the size of your office, or the square footage of your home changed, recalculate the business-use percentage for the new period. Update the worksheet template for the coming year. Pass a new corporate resolution if the plan terms changed materially.

**Step 16 -- Confirm depreciation schedule with your advisor.**
If you are including depreciation, ask your advisor to confirm the accumulated depreciation amount at year-end. This matters both for the corporate deduction and for tracking the recapture exposure on your home.

## Timing And Deadlines

| Requirement | Safe harbor window | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Advance to employee | No more than 30 days before the expense is incurred | Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g)(2) |
| Submit completed worksheet and documentation (substantiation) | No more than 60 days after the expense is paid or incurred | Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g)(2) |
| Return any excess advance or reimbursement | No more than 120 days after the expense is paid or incurred | Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g)(2) |
| Periodic statement alternative | Employer issues quarterly statements; employee has 120 days from each statement to substantiate or return excess | Treas. Reg. 1.62-2(g)(2) |

**Recommended monthly cadence:** Complete and submit the worksheet by the 5th business day of the following month. For example, submit the January worksheet by February 7 (approximately). This keeps every January expense well within the 60-day window.

**Year-end note:** All December expenses must be substantiated by approximately March 1 of the following year (60 days from December 31). Any December excess must be returned by approximately May 1 (120 days from December 31).

**One-time deadline:** The corporate resolution and written accountable plan must be signed and dated before the first reimbursement payment. Retroactive plans are not recognized by the IRS.

**Annual deadline:** Each January, review the business-use percentage and home cost figures. The mileage rate (if you also reimburse vehicle use) changes January 1 based on the IRS announcement in December.

**Note on depreciation:** The annual depreciation on the business portion of your home must be calculated by your advisor using your specific cost basis (what you paid for the home, adjusted for improvements, minus the land value). The formula above is illustrative; your advisor supplies the actual number. Do not estimate this figure.

**Note on the simplified method:** Rev. Proc. 2013-13, Section 4.05, states that the optional safe harbor method "does not apply to an employee with a home office if the employee receives advances, allowances, or reimbursements for expenses related to the qualified business use of the employee's home under a reimbursement or other expense allowance arrangement." If your S-corp reimburses you under an accountable plan, you must use the actual expense method. You cannot use $5 per square foot.

**Note on mortgage principal:** Only the interest portion of your mortgage payment is an indirect expense eligible for the business-use percentage calculation. The principal portion reduces loan balance; it is not a deductible or reimbursable home expense. Look for the interest amount on your monthly mortgage statement.

**Note on property taxes paid through escrow:** Many homeowners pay property taxes through monthly escrow payments. The reimbursable amount is the property tax actually assessed and paid during the month (or your pro-rated monthly share of the annual bill), not the escrow deposit. Your mortgage statement or county tax records show the actual tax amount.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

### Entity caveats

**S-corp owner-employees:** You must be receiving a W-2 salary that reflects reasonable compensation (a wage comparable to what you would pay an arm's-length employee for the same work) before taking advantage of accountable plan reimbursements. Home office reimbursements are in addition to your W-2 salary -- they cannot substitute for it. If the IRS determines your W-2 salary is unreasonably low and reclassifies distributions as wages, the reimbursements can also come under scrutiny.

**S-corp shareholders owning more than 2% of stock:** You are treated as a partner rather than an employee for purposes of certain fringe benefits (such as employer-paid health insurance). However, ordinary business expense reimbursements under an accountable plan -- including home office costs -- are NOT subject to this 2% shareholder limitation and are excluded from income as working condition fringe benefits under IRC Section 132(d). Confirm the specific treatment of any non-standard reimbursement with your advisor.

**C-corporation owner-employees:** The accountable plan rules apply identically. The reimbursement is excluded from your wages. The corporation deducts it on Form 1120.

**Sole proprietors and disregarded-entity single-member LLCs:** Cannot use this worksheet. You deduct home office on Schedule C using Form 8829 or the simplified method.

**Partnerships:** Partners cannot use an accountable plan for their own home office costs. Non-partner employees of the partnership can be covered by an accountable plan. Consult your advisor about the correct treatment for partner home office costs.

### Payroll caveats

- Payroll must be active and running before any reimbursement. If your S-corp stops running payroll, the accountable plan reimbursements lose their tax-free status during the period when no payroll runs.
- Reimbursements must be recorded as business expenses in the company books -- not as wages, owner draws, or distributions. A separate check or ACH (not combined with a payroll check) is the cleanest approach.
- Do not rely on your payroll software to process reimbursements as wages. Set them up as non-payroll expense reimbursements.

### State caveats

- Most states conform to the federal accountable plan rules, but not all. Before your first monthly reimbursement, confirm with your advisor that your state treats home office reimbursements the same way the IRS does.
- States with known differences include California, New Jersey, and Pennsylvania. If you operate in any of these states, get specific confirmation from your advisor before proceeding.
- Some states impose additional documentation requirements or may treat home office reimbursements differently for state income tax or state payroll tax purposes.
- If you work from home offices in more than one state (for example, you move mid-year), the allocation and state filing issues become more complex. Confirm with your advisor.

### Current-year caveats (2026)

**OBBBA eliminates employee deductions permanently:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed into law July 4, 2025 (Public Law 119-21), permanently eliminated the miscellaneous itemized deduction for unreimbursed employee business expenses -- including home office costs -- for most W-2 employees, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. For tax year 2026 and beyond, an employer accountable plan reimbursement is the ONLY way most W-2 employees (including S-corp owner-employees) can receive any tax benefit from home office costs. A qualifying home office that is not reimbursed produces zero tax deduction for the owner.

**Simplified method remains unavailable for accountable plan reimbursements:** Rev. Proc. 2013-13 continues to prohibit use of the $5/sq ft simplified method for home office expenses reimbursed through an employer accountable plan. This has not changed for 2026.

**Depreciation recapture risk is permanent:** The tax code has not changed the rule that "allowed or allowable" depreciation (meaning depreciation you were entitled to take, whether you took it or not) reduces your home's basis and triggers recapture at up to 25% upon sale. This is a long-term planning consideration every time you include depreciation in your monthly reimbursement.

## Common Mistakes

**1. No written accountable plan and corporate resolution.**
Reimbursing home office costs without these documents in place means all payments are taxable wages. The documents must exist and be dated before the first reimbursement.

**2. Including personal-use areas in the square footage.**
Measuring a room that is also used as a guest bedroom, hobby space, or family hangout disqualifies the entire room. The exclusive use test is strict. Only fully dedicated business space counts.

**3. Using estimated or averaged costs instead of actual monthly bills.**
The accountable plan requires substantiation of actual expenses. Using a round number you estimated ("about $200 in utilities") rather than the actual bill amounts fails the substantiation test. Pull real numbers from real bills.

**4. Including the mortgage principal payment as a home cost.**
Only mortgage interest is a deductible home expense. Principal repayment is not. Using the full monthly mortgage payment inflates the reimbursement and overstates the deduction.

**5. Submitting the worksheet more than 60 days after month end.**
If the December worksheet is not submitted until March, the December expenses fail the substantiation safe harbor. Late filing can convert those reimbursements to taxable wages. Set a firm monthly deadline: submit by the 5th of the following month.

**6. Paying the reimbursement from the personal account.**
The reimbursement must come from the business bank account to establish the employer-employee transaction. Payments from your personal account look like personal transfers and undermine the accountable plan paper trail.

**7. Including depreciation without advisor guidance.**
Calculating depreciation incorrectly -- or including it without understanding the recapture consequences -- can create problems at the time you sell the home. Run this calculation through your advisor.

**8. Treating the reimbursement as wages or a distribution in the bookkeeping.**
Recording the monthly reimbursement as payroll wages creates unnecessary income tax and FICA exposure. Recording it as a distribution does not produce a corporate deduction. It must be booked as a business expense.

**9. Not tracking the home-use percentage change.**
If you move to a new home, remodel the office, or change the space's use, the business-use percentage changes. Using a stale percentage overstates or understates the reimbursement and can cause problems in an audit.

**10. Reimbursing internet costs without separating the business portion.**
Your home internet bill is a mixed-use (partly personal, partly business) expense. Only the business-use portion is reimbursable. If you have a separately established internet line exclusively for business, 100% is reimbursable. If it is a shared household internet connection, you must apply a documented business-use percentage (for example, 60% if 60% of actual usage is for business).

**11. Combining the home office reimbursement with the Augusta Rule (home rental) strategy.**
These are separate strategies with different legal foundations. Mixing them without advisor guidance creates compliance risk. If you use both strategies, keep the documentation and payments completely separate.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and confirm with your advisor before acting on any of the following:

- [ ] **Before your first reimbursement:** Confirm entity type, payroll status, and that the office passes the regular-and-exclusive-use test. Have your advisor review the written accountable plan document and corporate resolution before you sign them.
- [ ] **Business-use percentage determination:** If your home has unusual floor plan characteristics, mixed-use rooms adjacent to the office, or multiple business spaces, have your advisor confirm the correct square footage approach.
- [ ] **Whether to include depreciation:** Before adding a depreciation line to the monthly worksheet, ask your advisor to calculate the correct annual amount and explain the recapture exposure at the time of home sale.
- [ ] **State conformity:** Before the first reimbursement, confirm your state follows federal accountable plan rules for home office costs. This is especially important if you operate in California, New Jersey, or Pennsylvania.
- [ ] **When your home arrangement changes:** If you move, buy a new home, refinance, remodel the office, or change how you use the space, confirm with your advisor before updating the worksheet figures and continuing reimbursements.
- [ ] **W-2 review each January:** Before your W-2 is finalized, confirm with your advisor or payroll provider that none of the monthly reimbursements were included in Box 1 wages.
- [ ] **If reimbursement amounts increase significantly year over year without a corresponding change in costs:** A sharp increase in claimed amounts can attract IRS scrutiny. Confirm the new amounts are fully supported by actual bills.
- [ ] **If you also rent your home to the business (Augusta Rule):** Combining that strategy with an accountable plan home office reimbursement requires careful structuring. Get explicit advisor guidance before doing both.

## Using The Download(s)

### Home Office Monthly Reimbursement Worksheet

This is the form you complete and sign each month to request home office reimbursement from your S-corp (or other employing entity). It creates the required substantiation record for your accountable plan.

**Where it fits in the workflow:** Use it in Step 7 of the monthly cycle, after you have collected all supporting bills for the month but before the company issues the reimbursement check.

**How to complete the form -- field by field:**

| Section / Field | What to enter |
|---|---|
| Employee name | Your legal name as it appears on your W-2 |
| Business / Employer name | The legal name of your corporation |
| Reimbursement period | The month and year being reimbursed (for example, "May 2026") |
| Submission date | Today's date (must be within 60 days of the last expense date for the period) |
| Home office square footage | The dedicated office space in square feet (e.g., 180 sq ft) |
| Total home square footage | Total finished square footage of the home (e.g., 1,800 sq ft) |
| Business-use percentage | Office sq ft / total home sq ft, expressed as a percent (e.g., 10.00%) |
| Indirect expenses -- Rent / Mortgage interest | The actual interest portion of the monthly mortgage payment, or the full rent payment if renting |
| Indirect expenses -- Property taxes | Taxes paid or accrued during the month |
| Indirect expenses -- Homeowners / Renters insurance | Monthly premium amount |
| Indirect expenses -- Electricity | Actual bill for the month |
| Indirect expenses -- Gas | Actual bill for the month |
| Indirect expenses -- Water, sewer, trash | Actual bill(s) for the month |
| Indirect expenses -- General repairs and maintenance | Invoices for repairs that benefit the whole home |
| Total indirect expenses | Sum of all indirect expense lines |
| Business-use amount (indirect) | Total indirect expenses multiplied by the business-use percentage |
| Direct expenses | List each direct expense (costs that apply only to the office) with a description and amount; 100% is reimbursable |
| Depreciation (if applicable) | Monthly depreciation amount as calculated by your advisor; enter "N/A" if not claiming |
| Total reimbursement requested | Business-use amount (indirect) + direct expenses + depreciation |
| Employee signature | Sign and date; your signature certifies the expenses are business-related and supporting documents are attached |
| Attachments checklist | Check off: mortgage/rent statement, utility bills, insurance statement, repair receipts, direct expense receipts, mileage log (if applicable) |

**After completing the worksheet:**
1. Attach all supporting bills and receipts to the completed form.
2. Keep a signed copy for your personal records.
3. Submit the original to the corporation (even if that means placing it in a business file you maintain).
4. Issue the reimbursement from the business bank account for the exact amount on the form.
5. File the form and all attachments in your monthly records folder for this tax year.

**Frequency:** Complete one worksheet per calendar month. Do not combine multiple months on one form.

**Digital vs. paper:** Both are acceptable. If digital, save the completed worksheet as a locked or read-only file with the date of submission in the file name. Keep the supporting bills as digital attachments in the same folder.

## Before You Act

**Stop here before you submit the first worksheet or issue any reimbursement.**

The home office reimbursement strategy works -- but only when the setup is correct and every monthly step is followed exactly. A reimbursement paid without a written accountable plan predating the payment, without a corporate resolution, without actual bills as backup, or filed more than 60 days after the month ends can be reclassified by the IRS as taxable wages. That means back income taxes, back FICA taxes, interest, and potentially penalties -- the opposite of the intended result.

Before you act:
- Confirm your entity type, payroll status, and home office qualify with your advisor.
- Have your advisor review the written accountable plan document and corporate resolution before signing them.
- Do not submit the first reimbursement until both documents are signed, dated, and filed.
- Confirm your state's treatment of home office accountable plan reimbursements with your advisor.
- Confirm whether to include depreciation in the monthly reimbursement with your advisor before adding that line.
- Confirm the mortgage interest amount (not total mortgage payment) is what you are using for the housing cost line.

This page and the attached worksheet teach the strategy and provide the calculation tool. Your advisor applies it to your specific facts and confirms it is implemented correctly.
