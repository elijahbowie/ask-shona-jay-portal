# Business Travel Deduction

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The business travel deduction allows self-employed business owners and certain qualifying employees to deduct the ordinary and necessary costs of traveling away from their tax home for business purposes. Qualifying expenses include transportation, lodging, meals (at 50%), and other incidental costs. Because you are deducting the full cost of a trip that serves a genuine business purpose, this strategy converts out-of-pocket travel spending into a pre-tax business expense, reducing both income tax and, for self-employed filers, self-employment tax on those amounts. The deduction is governed by Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 162 (ordinary and necessary business expenses) and the strict substantiation rules of IRC Section 274(d), which require a contemporaneous record of every trip.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is most valuable for:

- **Self-employed business owners, sole proprietors, and single-member LLCs taxed as sole proprietorships:** You deduct qualifying travel directly on Schedule C (Form 1040), reducing both income tax and self-employment tax.
- **S-corporation and C-corporation owner-employees:** Your entity deducts the travel expense (either paid directly or reimbursed to you under an accountable plan). Travel reimbursed under an accountable plan is also excluded from your W-2 income.
- **Partners in a partnership or multi-member LLC:** Partners generally deduct unreimbursed partnership expenses on Schedule E, or the partnership pays and deducts travel directly.
- **Qualifying employee exceptions (see below):** Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, and fee-basis state or local government officials may still deduct unreimbursed travel using Form 2106 as an above-the-line deduction.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **W-2 employees who are not in a qualifying exception category:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed into law July 4, 2025, permanently eliminated the miscellaneous itemized deduction for unreimbursed employee business expenses effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. If you are a regular W-2 employee and your employer does not reimburse your travel through an accountable plan, you cannot deduct those travel costs for tax year 2026 and beyond. The solution is to ask your employer to adopt an accountable plan.
- **Trips that are primarily personal:** If your trip is primarily for vacation or personal reasons and business is merely incidental, none of the transportation costs are deductible. Only the separately identifiable, documented business expenses (such as a conference registration) may qualify.
- **Commuting:** The daily drive between your home and your regular place of business is never deductible, regardless of how far you live from your office. Commuting is a personal expense under IRC Section 262.
- **Indefinite or permanent work assignments away from home:** If a work assignment is expected to last (or does in fact last) more than one year at a single location, that location becomes your new tax home. Travel to and from that location is commuting, not deductible travel.
- **Travel with no contemporaneous records:** The Section 274(d) substantiation rules are a hard gate. If you cannot show a record that was created at or near the time of travel documenting amount, date, place, and business purpose, the deduction is disallowed -- even if the trip was genuinely for business.
- **Lavish or extravagant expenses:** Expenses the IRS considers unreasonably large relative to the business purpose are not deductible. This is a facts-and-circumstances standard, not a fixed dollar cap, but luxury upgrades without business justification are a common audit flag.

## Key Terms

**Ordinary and necessary:** Two requirements under IRC Section 162. "Ordinary" means the expense is common and accepted in your trade or business. "Necessary" means it is helpful and appropriate -- not that it is indispensable. Both conditions must be met.

**Tax home:** Your main place of business or post of duty, regardless of where your family lives. If you work in multiple locations, the IRS uses three factors to determine your principal place of business: the total time you spend there, your level of business activity there, and the amount of income you earn there. If you have no regular or principal place of business, your tax home may be where you regularly live -- but if you have no regular place of business AND no regular living place, the IRS may classify you as an "itinerant," meaning you have no tax home and cannot claim travel deductions for lodging or meals.

**Away from home:** You are traveling away from home when your business duties require you to be away from the general area of your tax home for a period substantially longer than an ordinary day's work, AND you need to sleep or rest to meet the demands of your work while away. Both conditions must be satisfied.

**Temporary assignment:** A work assignment at a single location that is realistically expected to last (and does in fact last) one year or less. Travel to a temporary assignment location is deductible. If the assignment later exceeds one year, or if you expect from the start that it will exceed one year, it is not temporary and the expenses are not deductible from the point the expectation changes.

**Ordinary day's work:** Your normal daily schedule. A trip that requires you to sleep away from home -- even if you return the same calendar day -- can qualify. A day trip that does not require sleep or rest does not satisfy the "away from home" test.

**IRC Section 274(d) substantiation:** A strict set of record-keeping rules that apply to travel, meal, vehicle, and gift expenses. You must document four elements for every expense: (1) the amount, (2) the time and date, (3) the place or description of the expense, and (4) the business purpose. These records must be maintained in a contemporaneous manner -- meaning at or near the time the expense occurred, not reconstructed from memory months later.

**Contemporaneous record:** A log, diary, receipt, or other written record created at or near the time of each expense, not reconstructed later. The IRS gives substantial weight to timely records and is highly skeptical of reconstructed ones.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The tax form sole proprietors and single-member LLC owners use to report business income and expenses. Travel deductions for the self-employed appear on Schedule C, Part II, Line 24a (travel) and Line 24b (meals, after the 50% reduction).

**Form 2106 (Employee Business Expenses):** The form used by the narrow category of employees who may still deduct unreimbursed employee business expenses above the line: Armed Forces reservists, qualified performing artists, and fee-basis state or local government officials.

**Schedule E (Form 1040):** The form partners in a partnership or S-corporation shareholders use to report their share of pass-through income and losses. Unreimbursed partnership expenses (UPE) that a partner pays personally may be deducted on a separate Schedule E line.

**50% meal limitation:** Under IRC Section 274(n), only 50% of the cost of meals during business travel is deductible. The 50% limit applies whether you use actual meal costs or a standard meal allowance (per diem).

**Per diem:** A fixed daily allowance used instead of tracking actual lodging and meal costs. Employers may pay employees a per diem at or below IRS-approved rates as a simplified substantiation method. Self-employed individuals using per diem for meals still apply the 50% limit.

**High-low per diem method:** An IRS-approved simplified alternative to city-by-city GSA per diem rates. Under this method, there are only two rates: one for travel to designated high-cost localities and one for all other CONUS (continental United States) locations. The high-low method is available to employers reimbursing employees; self-employed individuals may use the standard GSA M&IE rate for meals but must substantiate lodging with actual receipts.

**M&IE:** Meals and incidental expenses. "Incidental expenses" means fees and tips for porters, baggage carriers, hotel staff, and similar services. M&IE is the meal-plus-incidentals portion of a per diem rate.

**CONUS:** The continental United States -- the 48 contiguous states plus the District of Columbia, excluding Alaska, Hawaii, and U.S. territories.

**Accountable plan:** An employer reimbursement arrangement that meets three IRS requirements: (1) business connection, (2) timely substantiation, and (3) return of excess advances. Reimbursements under a valid accountable plan are excluded from the employee's W-2 income and not subject to payroll tax. See the separate Accountable Plan Reimbursements lesson for full implementation details.

**Primary purpose test:** For trips that mix business and personal activities, the test used to determine deductibility of transportation costs. If the trip's primary purpose is business, the round-trip transportation cost is fully deductible. If the primary purpose is personal, none of the transportation is deductible. Only the days spent on actual business activities are used to determine primary purpose.

**Self-employment tax:** The tax paid by self-employed individuals covering Social Security and Medicare (equivalent to the combined employer-plus-employee FICA rate). Because Schedule C deductions reduce net self-employment income, a travel deduction saves both income tax AND self-employment tax for sole proprietors and single-member LLCs.

**IRC Section 162:** The general tax code provision allowing a deduction for all "ordinary and necessary expenses paid or incurred during the taxable year in carrying on any trade or business."

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before starting implementation:

1. **Entity type and tax treatment:** Are you a sole proprietor (Schedule C), an S-corp or C-corp owner-employee, a partner in a partnership, or a W-2 employee? This determines which form captures the deduction and whether an accountable plan is required.
2. **Tax home location:** What city or general area is your main place of business? If you have multiple locations, which is the primary one? If you work from home, what is your home address?
3. **List of business trips taken (or planned) in 2026:** For each trip, you need: destination, dates, total days away, number of business days, number of personal days, and the business purpose.
4. **Actual expenses per trip:** Transportation costs (airfare, rail, rideshare, gas, tolls, parking), lodging costs (actual receipts), meal costs (actual receipts or per diem election), and other qualifying costs (baggage, laundry, business calls, tips).
5. **Vehicle information (if driving):** Make, model, year, total miles driven in 2026, business miles driven, and whether you want to use the standard mileage rate or actual expenses. Note: you must choose the standard mileage rate in the first year you use a vehicle for business if you want the option to use it in later years.
6. **Reimbursement status:** Did your employer or entity reimburse any of these expenses? If so, how much and under what arrangement (accountable plan or not)?
7. **International travel:** For any trips outside the United States, note the total days outside the U.S., total business days outside the U.S., and total personal days outside the U.S.
8. **Assignment duration:** For trips to a single out-of-town location, what is the expected total duration? Has it exceeded or is it expected to exceed one year?

## Required Documents And Records

**Create and retain the following for every business trip:**

- **Travel log or trip diary:** A contemporaneous record (created during or immediately after the trip) documenting for each day: date, destination, miles driven or transportation used, business purpose of the day, and names of business contacts met. A phone note, email to yourself, or a paper log all work.
- **Receipts for transportation:** Airfare confirmations, train tickets, rideshare receipts, rental car invoices, toll and parking receipts. No dollar-amount threshold eliminates the requirement to substantiate; keep all receipts.
- **Receipts for lodging:** Hotel folios or confirmation records showing the date, amount, and location. Lodging costs cannot be estimated using a per diem if you are self-employed; you must use actual costs.
- **Meal receipts (or per diem election documentation):** If using actual costs, keep all meal receipts. If using a standard M&IE rate, document your election and apply the 50% limit to the allowance.
- **Business purpose documentation:** Emails, contracts, client correspondence, meeting agendas, conference registration confirmations, or any other record that connects the trip to a specific business activity. The stronger the paper trail connecting the trip to your business, the less work you will do defending it.
- **Calendar or agenda:** Your business calendar showing scheduled meetings, calls, or events at the destination.
- **For vehicle use:** A mileage log showing date, starting point, destination, business purpose, and odometer reading (start and end) for each business drive. A mileage log app is acceptable.
- **For mixed personal/business trips:** A schedule showing which days were business days and which were personal, with evidence supporting the business days (agendas, client emails, etc.).

**Retention period:** Keep all travel records for at least three years after the filing date of the return on which the deduction appears. If you underreport income by more than 25% of gross income, the IRS has six years to assess. When in doubt, keep records for six years. Employment tax records (if your entity employs others) must be kept for at least four years after the tax becomes due or is paid.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

**Before you travel:**

1. **Confirm the trip qualifies.** Verify that (a) your tax home is established, (b) the destination is away from your tax home, (c) the trip will require overnight sleep or rest, and (d) the primary purpose is business. If any element is uncertain, consult your advisor before booking.

2. **Create a trip file.** Open a folder (physical or digital) for this trip. Label it with the destination and dates. Everything you collect about this trip goes here.

3. **Document the business purpose before you leave.** Write a brief note (email to yourself, calendar event description, or text document) stating: where you are going, why (the specific business activity), and what outcome you expect. Doing this before the trip makes the business purpose far more credible than a note written during an audit.

**During travel:**

4. **Keep every receipt.** Transportation, lodging, meals, baggage fees, laundry, tips, business calls -- all of it. Take a photo of paper receipts immediately and store in your trip file.

5. **Log each day as it happens.** At the end of each day, record in your travel log: date, location, who you met with or what business activity you performed, and the business purpose. Three sentences is enough. Do not wait until you return home.

6. **Track your vehicle miles if driving.** For every business drive, note start odometer, end odometer, destination, and business purpose before you forget.

**After you return:**

7. **Organize expenses by category.** Sort receipts into: (a) transportation, (b) lodging, (c) meals, (d) other. Total each category. Apply the 50% limit to the meals total.

8. **Fill in the Travel Quick Reference Guide download.** Use the guide to record the trip summary: dates, destination, business purpose, total days, business days, personal days, and expense totals by category. One row per trip, completed within a week of returning.

9. **Determine your deductible amount:**
   - Transportation: 100% deductible (if primary purpose is business)
   - Lodging: 100% deductible (actual cost, no per diem option for self-employed)
   - Meals: 50% of actual cost, OR 50% of applicable M&IE per diem rate
   - Other qualifying costs (baggage, laundry, business calls, tips): 100% deductible
   - Vehicle: either (a) business miles times the standard mileage rate ($0.725 per mile for 2026) or (b) actual vehicle expenses times the business-use percentage

10. **For mixed personal/business trips:** Count the total days of the trip and the business days. Transportation is deductible in full if business days exceed personal days (primary purpose is business). If personal days exceed business days, transportation is not deductible. Either way, only the lodging and meals on actual business days are deductible -- you cannot deduct the hotel for a personal vacation day.

11. **For international trips:** If the trip is longer than seven days (not counting the day you depart) and personal days are 25% or more of total days outside the U.S., you must allocate transportation costs: multiply total transportation by (business days outside U.S. divided by total days outside U.S.). If personal days are less than 25% of total time outside the U.S., full transportation is deductible. Lodging and meals are still limited to actual business days only.

12. **Report the deduction on the correct form:**
    - Sole proprietor / single-member LLC: Schedule C, Part II, Line 24a (travel) and Line 24b (deductible meals)
    - S-corp or C-corp entity paying directly: deduct on the entity's return (Form 1120S or 1120) as a business expense
    - S-corp or C-corp owner reimbursed under an accountable plan: entity deducts the reimbursement; you do not report it as income
    - Partner (unreimbursed partnership expenses): Schedule E, with a separate UPE line -- confirm treatment with your advisor
    - Qualifying employees (reservists, performing artists, fee-basis officials): Form 2106, then carried to Schedule 1 (Form 1040) as an above-the-line adjustment

13. **Archive the trip file.** Store all receipts, the travel log, the business purpose documentation, and your Travel Quick Reference Guide row together. Keep digitally or in a physical binder organized by tax year.

## Timing And Deadlines

- **Contemporaneous records are required.** The closer to real-time your log is written, the stronger your substantiation. The IRS scrutinizes reconstructed records. Write the log entry the same day or the day after. Do not wait until you prepare your return.
- **Annual mileage rate applies calendar-year.** The 2026 standard mileage rate of $0.725 per mile applies to all business miles driven between January 1, 2026 and December 31, 2026.
- **Vehicle method election:** If you use the standard mileage rate, you must choose it in the FIRST year the vehicle is placed in service for business. In subsequent years, you may switch between standard mileage and actual expenses for a vehicle you own (but not for a leased vehicle -- a leased vehicle must use the same method for the entire lease term including renewals).
- **Per diem rates reset October 1.** The IRS special per diem rates (Notice 2025-54) and the GSA CONUS rates run on a federal fiscal year (October 1 through September 30). The FY 2026 rates in this playbook apply to travel on or after October 1, 2025 through September 30, 2026. Confirm updated rates for travel after September 30, 2026.
- **Return filing deadline:** The travel deduction is claimed on your annual income tax return. For sole proprietors on a calendar year, the return (Form 1040 with Schedule C) is due April 15, 2027 for tax year 2026, with an automatic six-month extension available (Form 4868) to October 15, 2027. The extension extends the filing deadline, not the payment deadline.
- **Entity returns:** S-corps (Form 1120S) and partnerships (Form 1065) are due March 15, 2027 for the calendar year 2026, with a six-month extension to September 15, 2027.
- **Quarterly bookkeeping rhythm:** Tally travel expenses by category at the end of each quarter. Do not wait until December to reconstruct a year's worth of trips. Update your Travel Quick Reference Guide after every trip.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure matters significantly:**

- **Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C):** Travel deductions reduce both income tax and self-employment tax (15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base, 2.9% above). This makes each dollar of travel deduction more valuable than for an S-corp owner who takes a salary.
- **S-corp owner-employees:** If you are both an officer and a shareholder of your S-corp, your travel should either be paid directly by the corporation or reimbursed to you under a written accountable plan. If the S-corp pays travel directly (on a corporate card or directly to the vendor), it is an entity-level deduction with no income or payroll tax consequences to you. If you pay personally and the S-corp reimburses you under an accountable plan, the reimbursement is excluded from your W-2 and not subject to payroll tax. If you pay personally and the S-corp does not reimburse you, you may have an unreimbursed S-corp expense issue -- discuss with your advisor because this is not cleanly handled on a personal return the same way UPE works for partnerships.
- **C-corps:** Business travel paid by the C-corp is a corporate deduction. Owner-employees reimbursed under an accountable plan owe no income or payroll tax on the reimbursement.
- **Partnerships:** A partner who pays qualifying partnership business expenses personally may deduct those as unreimbursed partnership expenses (UPE) on Schedule E. The partnership agreement should support this treatment. Ask your advisor to confirm the partnership agreement language before claiming UPE.

**Payroll implications:**

- Travel reimbursements paid to employees OUTSIDE an accountable plan are treated as wages -- they are added to Box 1 of the W-2 and subject to FICA withholding. This is a payroll error that creates both a tax cost and potential penalties. If you have employees you reimburse for travel without a written accountable plan, correct this immediately.

**State tax caveats:**

- Most states conform to federal rules on business travel deductibility, but conformity is not universal. Some states have their own per diem rates or disallow certain deductions. A handful of states (notably California) have their own conformity rules that may differ from federal law. Confirm state-specific treatment with your advisor, especially if you travel across state lines regularly.
- Several states have not fully conformed to OBBBA changes as of the date of this playbook. Confirm whether your state still allows a deduction for unreimbursed employee business expenses on the state return even though the federal deduction is eliminated.

**OBBBA and employees -- 2026 is a clean break:**

- The permanent elimination of the miscellaneous itemized deduction under the OBBBA means that for tax year 2026 and all future years, W-2 employees (outside the narrow qualifying categories) have no federal deduction path for unreimbursed travel. If you are a business owner who also receives W-2 income from a separate employer and pays unreimbursed travel for that W-2 job, that travel is not deductible. The business travel deduction covered in this playbook applies to your business (Schedule C, S-corp, or partnership), not your W-2 job.

**No deduction for family travel without independent business purpose:**

- You cannot deduct the travel costs of a spouse, dependent, or other person accompanying you on a business trip unless that person (a) is your employee, (b) has a bona fide business reason for traveling that would independently qualify them for a deduction, and (c) actually performs substantial business services -- not just incidental assistance. "My spouse attended the dinners" does not qualify. The business companion's expenses are a personal cost to you.

**Cruise ship and convention special rules:**

- Conventions on cruise ships: deductible only up to $2,000 per year [confirm with your advisor -- the IRS imposes specific limitations under IRC Section 274(h)(2) and Publication 463; verify the current dollar cap against Publication 463 for your filing year]. Strict reporting requirements apply. Consult your advisor before booking.
- Conventions outside North America: subject to additional tests under IRC Section 274(h). The IRS looks at whether it is as reasonable to hold the convention outside North America as within it. The rules are complex and deductions have been challenged in audit. Get advisor sign-off before claiming.

## Common Mistakes

1. **No contemporaneous log.** Reconstructing a year's worth of trips from credit card statements and memory at tax time is the most common reason travel deductions fail in an IRS examination. The log must be contemporaneous. Build the habit of logging during or immediately after each trip.

2. **Mixing personal and business days without a clear count.** If you extend a business trip by three days for tourism, only the business days' lodging and meals are deductible. Failing to segregate costs by day turns the entire trip into a gray area -- and auditors notice.

3. **Deducting commuting as travel.** The drive from your home to your regular office is never deductible. If your home IS your principal place of business (a qualifying home office under IRC Section 280A), then trips FROM home to client sites or temporary work locations may qualify -- but the home office requirement is separate and must be independently met. Do not claim business mileage from home without confirming your home office qualifies.

4. **Claiming the standard mileage rate after using actual expenses in year one.** If you used actual vehicle expenses in the first year a car was placed in business service, you cannot switch to the standard mileage rate for that vehicle. The election is binding for that car.

5. **Using a per diem for lodging as a self-employed person.** Self-employed individuals cannot use the GSA per diem rate to estimate lodging costs. You must have actual lodging receipts. Only the M&IE portion can be estimated using the standard rate (still subject to the 50% meal limit).

6. **Forgetting the 50% meal reduction.** Every business meal during travel is subject to the 50% deduction limit. Many business owners deduct 100% of meal costs and have the excess disallowed on audit.

7. **Not applying the primary purpose test to transportation.** If you took a trip that was primarily personal and tried to add a business meeting to make it deductible, the IRS looks at purpose, not the presence of a meeting. The primary purpose test is applied to the overall trip, and if personal days outnumber business days, transportation is not deductible.

8. **Deducting travel for indefinite assignments.** If you have been working at an out-of-town client site for more than a year, or if you expected from the start that the assignment would last more than a year, those travel costs are not deductible. This is a common mistake for business owners with long-term consulting arrangements.

9. **Failing to apply international allocation.** On a mixed trip abroad lasting more than seven days with personal days at 25% or more, you must prorate transportation costs. Deducting the full round-trip airfare on a trip where half the days were vacation is an error that will be flagged.

10. **Assuming W-2 employees can deduct unreimbursed travel after 2025.** The OBBBA permanently eliminated this deduction. If an employee is paying for travel out of pocket and expecting a federal tax deduction, that assumption is wrong for 2026. The correct answer is to ask the employer to adopt an accountable plan.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

- **Your tax home is unclear.** If you work in multiple cities, work from home without a qualifying home office, or have recently moved, your tax home may not be what you assume. An incorrect tax home invalidates travel deductions.
- **You are claiming travel for an assignment lasting close to or more than one year.** The temporary-vs.-indefinite line is a facts-and-circumstances test, and getting it wrong can unwind an entire year of deductions.
- **You intend to deduct a trip with significant personal days.** Before filing, have your advisor review the business-day count and confirm the primary purpose test is met.
- **You are deducting international travel where personal days are 25% or more of total days.** The allocation calculation must be correct, and the business purpose for each business day must be documented.
- **You want to use an employer per diem rather than actual costs.** The high-low method has specific eligibility rules and a list of designated high-cost localities that changes annually. Confirm your method and the applicable rate with your advisor.
- **Your entity is an S-corp and you are paying travel personally without an accountable plan in place.** This creates unreimbursed S-corp expense treatment that is handled differently than UPE for partners. Clarify the right approach with your advisor before filing.
- **You are an employee and believe you fall into a qualifying exception (reservist, performing artist, fee-basis official).** The exception rules are narrow. Confirm your specific facts qualify before filing Form 2106.
- **You are considering deducting a cruise ship convention or an international convention.** Special statutory limitations apply. Do not claim these without advisor review.
- **Your state has not conformed to OBBBA changes.** If you believe your state still allows an employee business expense deduction, confirm with your advisor before claiming it on the state return.
- **You are starting to use a vehicle for business for the first time in 2026.** The choice between standard mileage and actual expenses locks in for that vehicle. Discuss which method is more advantageous before you file.

## Using The Download(s)

**Travel Quick Reference Guide**

This download is your trip-by-trip tracking sheet. Use it as follows:

- **When to use it:** Complete one row per business trip, within one week of returning home. Do not wait until year-end.
- **What to record in it:** Each row captures: trip dates (departure and return), destination city, primary business purpose (one sentence), total days of trip, number of business days, number of personal days, transportation cost (total), lodging cost (actual total), meal costs (total before the 50% reduction), other deductible costs (baggage, laundry, etc.), vehicle miles driven (if applicable), and whether any reimbursement was received from your entity.
- **How it connects to your return:** At year-end, the guide gives you a pre-totaled summary of all trips by category. Your tax preparer uses this summary, along with your underlying receipts and logs, to complete Schedule C Line 24a (travel), Line 24b (meals after the 50% reduction), and the applicable vehicle section.
- **Where it lives in the workflow:** After Step 8 in the Step-By-Step Implementation section above. It is your running summary document for the year, not a substitute for receipts or your daily travel log.
- **State it clearly:** The Travel Quick Reference Guide is a summary tool. It does not replace the underlying substantiation (receipts, travel log, business purpose documentation). If you are audited, the IRS will ask for both the summary and the backup records.

- [ ] If personal days are 25% or more of total, I flagged the trip for advisor review and transportation allocation
- [ ] I documented the business purpose for each specific business day abroad

**At year-end:**

- [ ] I totaled all trips on the Travel Quick Reference Guide by category
- [ ] I confirmed the standard mileage rate to use ($0.725 for all of 2026, per IRS Notice 2026-10)
- [ ] I calculated total deductible vehicle expense (miles times rate, OR actual cost times business-use percentage)
- [ ] I applied the 50% meal limitation to all business meal costs across all trips
- [ ] I confirmed my entity type and identified the correct tax form line for the deduction
- [ ] I passed the Trip Summary to my tax preparer along with the underlying receipts and logs
- [ ] I confirmed with my advisor that any trip with personal days, international components, or assignment duration close to one year was reviewed before filing
- [ ] I stored all records in a place I can retrieve them for at least three years (six if any concern about the 25% underreporting threshold)

## Before You Act

Before claiming a business travel deduction on any return, reimbursement, or entity expense report, confirm the following with your advisor:

1. Your tax home is clearly established and is what you think it is.
2. Each trip you are deducting genuinely satisfies the "away from home" and "overnight" requirements.
3. Each trip's primary purpose is business, and you have documentation to support that conclusion.
4. Any trip with significant personal days has been reviewed for the primary purpose test and proper expense allocation.
5. Any international trip has been reviewed for the 25% personal-day allocation rule.
6. Any out-of-town assignment has been confirmed as a temporary assignment (one year or less).
7. Your vehicle method election (standard mileage vs. actual) is consistent with prior years for that vehicle.
8. Your entity structure supports the deduction path you are using (Schedule C, accountable plan, UPE, etc.).
9. Your state return treatment has been confirmed -- not all states conform to all federal rules.
10. Your records (receipts, travel log, business purpose documentation, Travel Quick Reference Guide) are complete, contemporaneous, and stored where you can find them.

Do not file the return, submit the reimbursement request, or claim the deduction until you have confirmed these facts with your advisor.
