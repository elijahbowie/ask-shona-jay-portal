# IRS Account, Transcript, Notice, and Refund Workflow

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Your IRS Online Account is a secure portal that lets you pull tax transcripts, view your balance, see notices, track your refund, and make or schedule payments, all without calling the IRS. This playbook walks you through how to set up that account, how to read and use each type of transcript, how to respond to the most common IRS notices before deadlines pass, and how to track your refund or resolve a delayed one. Acting quickly and with the right information is the single most effective way to avoid escalating penalties, interest, and collection action.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook applies to you if any of the following describe your situation:

- **Any individual or business owner who has filed, or needs to file, a federal tax return:** The IRS Online Account and transcript system is available to all taxpayers with a Social Security number (SSN) or Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN).
- **Anyone who has received an IRS notice:** Any letter or notice that proposes a change to your tax, requests information, shows a balance due, or references a levy or lien requires a timely response. This playbook covers the most common notice types.
- **Anyone expecting a refund:** Whether you filed electronically or by paper, this playbook explains how and when to track your refund and what to do if it is delayed.
- **Anyone who received a CP2000, CP2501, CP14, CP3219A, LT11, or CP90 notice:** These are the notices most likely to require action on a deadline.
- **Business owners (sole proprietors, S-corp shareholders, partners, LLC members):** Separate business transcript access is available through the IRS Business Tax Account.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Taxpayers with complex audit situations:** If the IRS has opened a formal examination (audit) of your return, the notice workflow described here does not apply. Audits require a separate process. Route this to your advisor immediately.
- **Taxpayers facing active collection (levy on wages or bank account):** Once a levy is in progress, the standard response timelines no longer apply. Contact your advisor the same day you receive any levy notice.
- **Identity theft victims:** If someone has already filed a return using your SSN, the standard Online Account setup and transcript process is disrupted. See the IRS Identity Theft page and contact your advisor.
- **Non-resident aliens (Form 1040-NR filers):** Some tools described here, including certain transcript types and the standard Online Account registration, may operate differently for non-residents. Confirm with your advisor.
- **Taxpayers who have a Power of Attorney (POA) on file for a representative:** Your representative uses the IRS Tax Pro Account, not the Individual Online Account, to pull your transcripts on your behalf.

## Key Terms

**IRS Online Account:** The individual taxpayer self-service portal at irs.gov where you can view your tax records, download transcripts, see notices, make payments, and manage payment plans. Access requires identity verification through ID.me.

**ID.me:** A federally certified third-party identity verification service the IRS uses to confirm who you are before granting access to your Online Account. You create one ID.me account and use it across multiple government sites. Your biometric data (selfie) is deleted after successful verification.

**Tax transcript:** An official IRS record that summarizes the information in your tax file. Transcripts are not copies of your return; they are formatted summaries produced by IRS systems. There are five types (described in The Numbers section).

**Tax Return Transcript:** Shows most line items from your original Form 1040-series return as filed, without any post-filing changes. Commonly requested by mortgage lenders for income verification.

**Tax Account Transcript:** Shows the history of your account for a given tax year, including filing status, taxable income, payments, penalties, interest, and any changes made after you filed. This is the transcript that shows IRS transaction codes.

**Wage and Income Transcript:** Shows the data the IRS received from third parties, such as your employer (Form W-2), banks (Form 1099-INT), brokers (Form 1099-B), and others (Forms 1099-MISC, 1099-NEC, 1098, 5498). This is useful for confirming what the IRS knows about your income before you respond to a CP2000.

**Record of Account Transcript:** Combines the Tax Return Transcript and Tax Account Transcript into one document.

**Verification of Non-Filing Letter:** An official IRS statement that no Form 1040-series return has been processed for a given year. Often required by financial aid offices.

**Form 4506-T (Request for Transcript of Tax Return):** The paper form used to request any transcript type by mail when you cannot use the online system, or when you need a transcript for a year older than what the online system provides. Free to file; no fee for the transcript.

**Form 4506 (Request for Copy of Tax Return):** Different from 4506-T. This requests an actual photocopy of your original return, not a transcript. There is a fee per return copy.

**Transaction code:** A three-digit number that appears on a Tax Account Transcript to describe a specific IRS action on your account. Common codes include: TC 150 (return filed and tax liability established), TC 570 (refund hold pending review), TC 806 (withholding credit posted), TC 846 (refund issued), TC 826 (refund offset to pay a prior debt), and TC 971 (notice sent or miscellaneous account action).

**CP2000 notice:** A notice from the IRS Automated Underreporter (AUR) program that proposes an adjustment to your return based on a mismatch between what you reported and what third parties (employers, banks, brokers) reported to the IRS. It is a proposal, not a final bill. You have the right to agree or disagree.

**CP2501 notice:** A preliminary inquiry notice, issued before a CP2000, asking you to review a potential discrepancy. If you do not respond, the IRS will issue a CP2000.

**CP14 notice:** A balance due notice telling you that you owe unpaid taxes and demanding payment. The due date appears on the face of the notice.

**CP3219A notice (Statutory Notice of Deficiency, also called the 90-Day Letter):** The final IRS notice before a tax assessment becomes permanent. It gives you 90 days (150 days if you are outside the United States) to either pay the proposed amount, reach an agreement, or petition the U.S. Tax Court. This deadline cannot be extended.

**LT11 / Letter 1058 notice:** A notice of intent to levy, which informs you the IRS plans to seize your wages, bank accounts, or other property to collect unpaid taxes. It also notifies you of your right to request a Collection Due Process (CDP) hearing within 30 days.

**CP90 notice:** A notice of intent to levy sent to certain taxpayers; equivalent to LT11 for CDP purposes.

**Levy:** The legal seizure of your property or rights to property (wages, bank accounts, Social Security benefits) to satisfy a tax debt. A levy is different from a tax lien, which is a legal claim against your property.

**Collection Due Process (CDP) hearing:** A formal appeal you can request within 30 days of receiving an LT11 or CP90 notice. It pauses collection action while an IRS Appeals officer reviews your case. You may propose alternatives such as an installment agreement or offer in compromise.

**Installment agreement:** A payment plan under which you pay your tax debt in monthly installments over time instead of in one lump sum. Subject to setup fees and continued accrual of interest and penalties during the plan.

**Offer in Compromise (OIC):** An IRS program that allows certain taxpayers to settle their tax debt for less than the full amount owed. You must demonstrate that you cannot pay the full amount or that it would cause financial hardship.

**First-Time Penalty Abatement (FTA):** An IRS administrative waiver that removes the failure-to-file, failure-to-pay, or failure-to-deposit penalty for taxpayers who have a history of good compliance (no penalties in the prior three tax years and all returns filed on time).

**Form 843 (Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement):** The paper form used to request abatement (removal) of penalties or interest, or to claim a refund of taxes paid in error.

**Where's My Refund:** The IRS online tool (and IRS2Go mobile app) that lets you check the status of your federal refund using your SSN or ITIN, filing status, and exact refund amount.

**PATH Act hold:** A statutory requirement under the Protecting Americans from Tax Hikes (PATH) Act that prevents the IRS from issuing refunds containing the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) or Additional Child Tax Credit (ACTC) before mid-February, regardless of when you filed.

**Identity Protection PIN (IP PIN):** A six-digit number issued annually by the IRS that must be included on your tax return to verify your identity. Once you opt in, any return filed without your current IP PIN will be rejected, protecting you from tax-related identity theft.

**Amended return (Form 1040-X):** A corrected version of a previously filed Form 1040. Used when you discover an error on your original return (missed income, omitted deduction, wrong filing status). Processed separately from original returns and takes 8 to 16 weeks.

**Taxpayer Advocate Service (TAS):** An independent organization within the IRS that helps taxpayers who are experiencing economic harm, who have been unable to resolve issues through normal IRS channels, or who have faced an IRS delay of more than 30 days. TAS assistance is free and confidential.

**Collection Statute Expiration Date (CSED):** The date on which the IRS's legal authority to collect a specific tax debt expires. Under IRC Section 6502, the IRS generally has 10 years from the date a tax is assessed to collect it. Certain events (bankruptcy, CDP hearings, installment agreements) can suspend or extend this period.

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect these documents before responding to any IRS notice:**

- The original notice (do not discard it; the notice date and response deadline are legally significant)
- Your original tax return for the year in question
- All Forms W-2, 1099, 1098, and other information returns for the year in question
- Bank statements, brokerage statements, or other records substantiating any income or deduction the IRS is questioning
- Prior-year tax return (one year back), if the IRS notice relates to a prior year
- Proof of any payments already made (Direct Pay confirmation numbers, canceled checks, EFTPS records)

**Records to keep after any IRS interaction:**

- Copies of all transcripts you download, with the date downloaded noted in the filename
- Certified mail receipts or fax confirmation pages from any response you send to the IRS
- The response letter or form you submitted, signed and dated
- Any IRS written acknowledgment of your response
- Retain all IRS-related documents for at least seven years from the date of the return. If the IRS asserts fraud (which has no statute of limitations for assessment), retain records indefinitely.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Part A: Set Up Your IRS Online Account

**Step 1: Navigate to the IRS account registration page.**
Go to irs.gov and click "Sign in to your online account." Select "Individual." You will be directed to ID.me.

**Step 2: Create your ID.me account.**
Provide your email address, create a password (at least 8 characters), and set up multifactor authentication (an authenticator app, biometric unlock, or SMS text code). You must be age 18 or older to create an account.

**Step 3: Verify your identity with ID.me.**
You will need your SSN or ITIN and a government-issued photo ID (driver's license, state ID, passport, or passport card). You will also be prompted to take a selfie with your smartphone or webcam. The self-service process takes approximately 5 to 10 minutes if your documents are ready. ID.me deletes your biometric data after successful IRS verification.

**Step 4: Access your IRS Online Account dashboard.**
After ID.me verification, you are redirected to your IRS account. From the dashboard you can access: tax transcripts, payment history (up to 5 years), scheduled payments, your current balance by tax year, digital copies of IRS notices, your refund status, and your IP PIN.

**Step 5 (Optional but recommended): Opt in to paperless notices.**
Within your account settings, switch to electronic notices so that you receive IRS correspondence in your account rather than waiting for mail. You can also enable email notifications.

**Step 6 (Strongly recommended): Request an IP PIN.**
In the IP PIN section of your profile, request your six-digit Identity Protection PIN for the current year. The PIN is valid for one calendar year; you must retrieve a new one each January. Once you enroll, include your IP PIN on every federal tax return you file.

---

### Part B: Pull and Read Transcripts

**Step 7: Navigate to Tax Records.**
From your IRS Online Account dashboard, click "Tax Records." Select the transcript type you need and the tax year.

**Step 8: Choose the correct transcript type for your purpose.**

| Purpose | Transcript to request |
|---|---|
| Verify what you filed | Tax Return Transcript |
| See payments, penalties, interest, and account history | Tax Account Transcript |
| Check what W-2s and 1099s the IRS received | Wage and Income Transcript |
| Respond to a CP2000 | Wage and Income Transcript (to compare against what you reported) |
| Mortgage lender income verification | Tax Return Transcript |
| See your full account history including post-filing changes | Record of Account Transcript |
| FAFSA financial aid non-filing verification | Verification of Non-Filing Letter |

**Step 9: Download or print the transcript.**
Transcripts are available immediately in your online account at no charge. Save the file with a date-stamped filename (for example: "2024-TaxAccountTranscript-pulled-20260624.pdf").

**Step 10: Read the Tax Account Transcript for key transaction codes.**
The transcript lists each IRS action on your account in reverse chronological order. Key codes to look for:

- TC 150: Your return was filed and processed. The date next to this code is when the IRS accepted your return.
- TC 806: Your withholding credit was posted. The dollar amount should match your W-2 box 2 withholding.
- TC 846: A refund was issued. The date is the refund issue date.
- TC 826: Part or all of your refund was applied to offset a prior debt (past-due taxes, student loans, child support).
- TC 570: A hold is placed on your refund, pending review. This does not mean you did anything wrong; it may resolve on its own, but you should monitor it.
- TC 971: The IRS sent a notice or took a miscellaneous account action. Paired with TC 570, it often means a notice was mailed.
- TC 290: Additional tax was assessed, often after an audit adjustment or CP2000 agreement.

**Step 11: If you cannot use the online account, order by mail or phone.**
Call 800-908-9946 to use the automated transcript request line. Transcripts ordered by phone or the "Get Transcript by Mail" tool arrive in 5 to 10 calendar days. For older years not available online, submit Form 4506-T by mail.

**Step 12: For business transcripts, use the IRS Business Tax Account.**
Go to irs.gov/businesses/business-tax-account. Eligible entity types include sole proprietors, S corporations, C corporations, partnerships, LLCs, and exempt organizations. The Business Tax Account provides entity transcripts, account transcripts, and tax return transcripts. If you file electronically, wait 2 to 3 weeks after filing before requesting a transcript.

### Part C: Respond to IRS Notices

**Step 13: Identify the notice type and find the response deadline.**
The notice type (CP or LT number) appears in the upper-right corner of every IRS notice. The response deadline appears on the face of the notice. Never ignore a notice; even if you disagree, you must respond by the deadline to preserve your rights.

**Step 14: Determine the correct response for your notice type.**

**If you received a CP2501 (Preliminary Underreporter Inquiry):**
- This is a request for information before a formal adjustment.
- Pull your Wage and Income Transcript (Step 8) and compare it to your original return.
- If the transcript matches what you reported, send a written response explaining why (include copies of the relevant forms). Write "CP2501" on any amended return you include.
- If you agree with the IRS, indicate agreement and provide the requested information.
- Response deadline: stated on the notice. If you do not respond, the IRS will issue a CP2000.

**If you received a CP2000 (Notice of Underreported Income):**
- The CP2000 is a formal proposal. It is not yet a bill. You have 30 days from the notice date to respond (60 days if you live outside the United States).
- Pull your Wage and Income Transcript and compare line by line to the IRS's proposed adjustments.
- If you agree: complete and sign the Response form included with the notice. Submit it via the IRS Document Upload Tool (available at irs.gov), by fax to the number on the notice, or by certified mail.
- If you disagree: check the disagreement box on the Response form. Attach a signed written statement explaining why and include supporting documents (corrected W-2s, 1099s, brokerage statements, receipts for deductions).
- If you have additional income or deductions to report beyond the CP2000 adjustment: file a Form 1040-X amended return at the same time, writing "CP2000" at the top of the 1040-X.
- Paying the proposed amount within 30 days stops additional interest from accruing on the proposed amount during that window.
- If you do not respond: the IRS will issue a CP3219A (Step 16).
- Use the Implementation Worksheet to document each line of the IRS's proposed adjustments versus your records.

**If you received a CP14 (Balance Due Notice):**
- The CP14 is a demand for payment. The due date is printed on the notice.
- If you agree and can pay in full: pay by the date on the notice using IRS Direct Pay (directpay.irs.gov), EFTPS, check, or through your Online Account.
- If you cannot pay in full: contact the IRS or apply online for a payment plan (installment agreement) before the due date on the notice. Waiting until after the due date increases penalties and interest.
- If you believe you already paid: gather your proof of payment (Direct Pay confirmation number, canceled check, EFTPS record) and call the number on the notice or respond in writing.

**Step 15: Track your notice response status.**
After submitting your response to a CP2000 or CP2501, allow 8 to 12 weeks for the IRS to process it. Log into your Online Account and check the Tax Account Transcript for new transaction codes indicating IRS action. Keep the fax confirmation page or certified mail receipt as proof of timely response.

**Step 16: If you receive a CP3219A (Statutory Notice of Deficiency, the 90-Day Letter):**
This is the most time-critical notice. You have exactly 90 days from the notice date (150 days if the notice is addressed to an address outside the United States) to take one of three actions:
1. Pay the proposed amount in full.
2. Reach a signed agreement with the IRS.
3. File a petition with the U.S. Tax Court (at ustaxcourt.gov).

This deadline is set by law under IRC Section 6213 and cannot be extended by the IRS. If you miss it, the IRS will assess the tax and you lose the right to challenge it in Tax Court without first paying the full amount. Contact your advisor immediately upon receiving a CP3219A. Do not wait.

**Step 17: If you receive an LT11 or CP90 (Notice of Intent to Levy):**
You have 30 days from the notice date to request a Collection Due Process (CDP) hearing using Form 12153 (Request for a Collection Due Process or Equivalent Hearing). File Form 12153 with the IRS office identified in the notice. Requesting a CDP hearing pauses (suspends) the IRS's ability to levy while the hearing is pending. At the hearing, you can propose alternatives such as an installment agreement, an offer in compromise, or currently not collectible status. Contact your advisor the same day you receive this notice.

### Part D: Request Penalty Abatement

**Step 18: Determine if you qualify for First-Time Penalty Abatement (FTA).**
You qualify for FTA if all three of the following are true:
1. You have filed the same type of return (for example, Form 1040) for each of the three tax years immediately before the year you received the penalty.
2. You received no penalties during those three prior years, or any penalty during that period was removed for a reason other than FTA.
3. You are current on all filing and payment obligations now (or have set up an approved payment plan).

FTA applies to the failure-to-file penalty, the failure-to-pay penalty, and the failure-to-deposit penalty (for employment taxes).

**Step 19: Request FTA by phone or in writing.**
Call the phone number on your penalty notice. You do not need to use the phrase "First-Time Abatement." Simply say you are requesting penalty relief due to a good compliance history. The IRS will review your account and apply FTA if you qualify, then confirm relief in writing. If the IRS cannot approve it over the phone, submit a written request or Form 843.

**Step 20: If you do not qualify for FTA, consider reasonable cause relief.**
Reasonable cause applies when you can demonstrate that you exercised ordinary business care and prudence but were still unable to comply. Examples include serious illness, a death in the family, a natural disaster, or reliance on incorrect written advice from the IRS. If you have documentation supporting reasonable cause, include it with a written request or Form 843.

### Part E: Track and Resolve Your Refund

**Step 21: Check your refund status using Where's My Refund.**
Go to irs.gov/refunds or use the IRS2Go mobile app. You will need your SSN or ITIN, your filing status, and the exact dollar amount of your expected refund (from your return).

- For an e-filed return: refund status is available 24 hours after the IRS accepts your return.
- For a paper-filed return: allow 4 weeks after mailing before checking.
- The tool updates once per day, usually overnight.

**Step 22: Understand normal refund timelines.**
- E-filed return with direct deposit: typically within 3 weeks from the date the IRS accepts your return. The IRS processed over 80 percent of refunds in under 21 days during the 2026 filing season.
- Paper-filed return: 6 or more weeks from the date the IRS received your mailed return.
- Amended return (Form 1040-X): 8 to 16 weeks. Track using the Where's My Amended Return tool at irs.gov or by calling 866-464-2050 (available 3 weeks after filing).

**Step 23: If you claimed EITC or ACTC, expect a mid-February hold.**
By law (PATH Act, Section 201), the IRS cannot issue refunds that include the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) or Additional Child Tax Credit (ACTC) before mid-February. For 2026, the IRS held EITC/ACTC refunds until at least February 15, 2026. The IRS expected most of these refunds to be available in bank accounts by March 2, 2026, for taxpayers who chose direct deposit and had no other issues. Where's My Refund provided projected deposit dates for most early filers by February 21, 2026.

**Step 24: If your refund is delayed beyond the normal window, look at your Tax Account Transcript.**
Log into your Online Account and pull the Tax Account Transcript for the relevant year. Look for:
- TC 570: A hold is in place. Monitor for a follow-up TC 571 (hold released) or TC 971 (notice mailed).
- TC 971 paired with TC 570: A notice has been mailed requesting information (often an identity verification letter).
- TC 826: Your refund was applied to a prior debt. The amount applied and the period it was applied to should be listed.

**Step 25: If Where's My Refund shows "We cannot provide any information about your refund" for longer than 16 weeks after filing, contact TAS.**
Call the Taxpayer Advocate Service at 1-877-777-4778 or file Form 911 (Request for Taxpayer Advocate Service Assistance) at your local TAS office. TAS can intervene when normal IRS channels have failed to resolve your issue after 30 or more days, or when a delay is causing financial hardship.

---

### Part F: Set Up a Payment Plan if You Owe

**Step 26: If you owe and cannot pay in full, apply for a payment plan online.**
Log into your IRS Online Account. Click "Payment Plans." Select the plan type that fits your situation (see The Numbers section for thresholds). If you owe $50,000 or less in combined tax, penalties, and interest, you can apply online without calling the IRS. If you owe more than $50,000, you must call or submit Form 9465 (Installment Agreement Request).

**Step 27: Choose between a short-term and long-term plan.**
- Short-term plan (up to 180 days): No setup fee. Available if you owe less than $100,000 in combined tax, penalties, and interest. Interest and penalties continue to accrue.
- Long-term plan (installment agreement): Setup fee applies (see The Numbers section). Available for online application if you owe $50,000 or less. Payments made monthly. Direct debit reduces the setup fee and the failure-to-pay penalty rate.

**Step 28: Keep up with the plan once approved.**
Missing a payment on an installment agreement can default the agreement and reactivate collection action. Set up automatic bank withdrawals (Direct Debit Installment Agreement) to avoid missing payments.

## The Numbers

All figures are verified for tax year 2026 against canonical IRS sources cited in the Sources section.

**Transcript fees:** None. All individual and business transcripts are free.

**Form 4506-T transcript requests:** Free. Allow 5 to 10 calendar days by mail.

**Form 4506 (actual return copy):** [confirm with your advisor -- could not verify current fee against a public source at time of writing; historically $30 per return period].

**Transcript availability online:**
- Tax Return Transcript: current year plus 3 prior years
- Tax Account Transcript: current year plus 9 prior years online; current year plus 3 prior years by mail or phone
- Wage and Income Transcript: current year plus 9 prior years
- Record of Account Transcript: current year plus 3 prior years
- Verification of Non-Filing Letter: current year (after June 15) plus 3 prior years

**CP2000 response deadlines:** 30 days (domestic); 60 days (outside the United States)

**CP3219A Tax Court petition deadline:** 90 days (domestic); 150 days (outside the United States). Cannot be extended.

**CDP hearing request deadline (LT11/CP90):** 30 days from notice date

**Refund processing times:**
- E-filed return: typically within 3 weeks (IRS processed over 80% of 2026 e-filed refunds in under 21 days)
- Paper-filed return: 6 or more weeks
- Amended return (Form 1040-X): 8 to 16 weeks

**EITC/ACTC refund hold (2026):** Cannot be issued before mid-February by law. IRS expected most EITC/ACTC refunds in bank accounts by March 2, 2026, for direct deposit filers with no other issues.

**Failure-to-file penalty:** 5% of unpaid tax per month (or partial month) the return is late, up to 25%. If both failure-to-file and failure-to-pay penalties apply in the same month, the filing penalty is reduced to 4.5% and the payment penalty is 0.5% for that month. Minimum penalty for returns more than 60 days late: $525 (for returns required to be filed after December 31, 2025) or 100% of the unpaid tax, whichever is less.

**Failure-to-pay penalty:** 0.5% of unpaid tax per month (or partial month), up to 25%. Reduced to 0.25% per month during an approved installment agreement for individuals who filed on time. Increased to 1% per month if the IRS issues a notice of intent to levy and you do not pay within 10 days.

**IRS interest rate on underpayments (individuals):** The federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, compounded daily, adjusted quarterly. Q1 2026: 7%. Q2 2026: 6%. Q3 2026: 7%. Interest accrues from the original return due date until the balance is paid in full.

**Installment agreement setup fees (long-term plans):**
- Online Direct Debit: $22
- Phone/mail/in-person Direct Debit: $107
- Online (other payment method): $69
- Phone/mail/in-person (other payment method): $178
- Low-income taxpayers (at or below 250% of federal poverty level): $43 (waived entirely for Direct Debit agreements)
- Modification of an existing agreement online: $10
- Short-term payment plan (up to 180 days): $0 setup fee (interest and penalties continue)

**Installment agreement eligibility thresholds for online application:**
- Short-term plan: owe less than $100,000 in combined tax, penalties, and interest
- Long-term plan (online application): owe $50,000 or less in combined tax, penalties, and interest; balances above $50,000 require Form 9465 or phone application

**Guaranteed installment agreement eligibility (individual, no financial disclosure required):** Tax owed of $10,000 or less (excluding interest and penalties); returns filed on time and taxes paid for the prior 5 years; no prior installment agreements in that period; full amount paid within 3 years.

**First-Time Penalty Abatement (FTA):** Applies to failure-to-file, failure-to-pay, and failure-to-deposit penalties. Requires: (1) same return type filed for each of the prior 3 tax years; (2) no penalties in those 3 years, or any penalty removed for a reason other than FTA; (3) current compliance now (all returns filed, payments current or in an approved plan).

**Form 843 refund/abatement filing deadline:** Generally within 3 years of the original return due date or 2 years from the date you paid the tax, whichever is later.

**Collection Statute Expiration Date (CSED):** 10 years from the date the tax was assessed (IRC Section 6502).

**IRS Direct Pay single payment limit:** $10 million per payment; no more than 5 payments in a 24-hour period.

**IP PIN:** Six digits, valid for one calendar year, renewed each January. Required on every federal return once you opt in.

**Identity verification (ID.me):** Must be age 18 or older. Requires government photo ID and selfie. Self-service process takes approximately 5 to 10 minutes.

**TAS income eligibility for free LITC representation:** Low Income Taxpayer Clinics represent taxpayers whose income is at or below certain limits. Contact your local LITC for current income thresholds.

**Interest rate Q2 2026 change:** Decreased from 7% (Q1 2026) to 6% effective April 1, 2026, then increased back to 7% effective July 1, 2026.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**S-corporation shareholders:** If you receive both a W-2 from your S-corporation and pass-through income on Schedule K-1, IRS transcripts will show the W-2 income (via the Wage and Income Transcript) but will not show the K-1 distribution separately. A CP2000 comparing your W-2 to total income on your return should take K-1 income into account; your advisor can help you document the correct treatment if the IRS questions it.

**Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs:** Your business income and refunds flow through your personal SSN, not an EIN. All transcripts are pulled under your individual account. If you also have an EIN for employment tax purposes, use the Business Tax Account for payroll-related transcripts (Forms 940, 941).

**Partnerships and multi-member LLCs:** The entity files Form 1065 and receives its own EIN and entity transcript. Partners receive Schedule K-1. If the IRS sends a notice adjusting partnership items at the entity level (under the centralized partnership audit rules, known as BBA rules), each partner's individual return may also be affected. Route any partnership-level IRS notice to your advisor immediately.

**State taxes:** This playbook covers federal IRS account, transcript, notice, and refund workflows only. Every state with an income tax has its own portal, notice types, and deadlines. State refund timing, state notice response deadlines, and state payment plans operate independently of the IRS. Do not assume that resolving a federal notice resolves a corresponding state issue.

**Current-year filing season (2026):** As of June 2026, the IRS is operating with a significantly reduced workforce relative to prior years. Processing delays on paper returns and amended returns may be longer than the standard published timelines. Use electronic filing and electronic responses wherever possible. Monitor your Online Account and transcript for status updates.

**Business entities with payroll:** Failure-to-deposit penalties for employment taxes (Forms 941, 944) use a separate rate structure and timeline from income tax penalties. If you receive a payroll-related penalty notice, route it to your advisor and do not rely on the income tax penalty abatement rules in this playbook.

**Refund offsets (Treasury Offset Program):** If you or your spouse owe past-due federal taxes, state income taxes, student loans, or child support, the Bureau of the Fiscal Service may reduce (offset) your federal refund. You will receive a notice of offset. You cannot prevent an offset through the IRS; contact the agency that holds the debt.

**Bankruptcy:** Filing for bankruptcy suspends the CSED and most IRS collection action. It also suspends some deadlines and creates new ones. If you are in or considering bankruptcy, route all IRS notices to your bankruptcy attorney and your advisor immediately.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Ignoring a notice because you think it is wrong.**
Even if the IRS is wrong, you must respond by the deadline. Silence is treated as agreement. A CP2000 you ignore becomes a CP3219A. A CP3219A you ignore becomes a permanent assessment.

**2. Responding to the wrong address.**
Each notice contains a specific address, fax number, or upload portal for responses. Do not send your CP2000 response to a general IRS address; it may not reach the correct department within the deadline window.

**3. Confusing the 30-day CP2000 deadline with the 90-day CP3219A deadline.**
These are two different notices at two different stages of the same process. The 30-day deadline on a CP2000 is an administrative deadline; missing it triggers escalation. The 90-day deadline on a CP3219A is a statutory deadline set by law; missing it permanently eliminates your right to petition Tax Court.

**4. Not keeping proof of timely response.**
If you fax your response, keep the fax confirmation page showing the date, time, and number of pages transmitted. If you mail, use certified mail with return receipt. Without proof, the IRS may claim it never received your response.

**5. Assuming a refund will arrive in 21 days.**
That timeline applies only to e-filed returns with no issues. Paper returns, amended returns, returns with EITC or ACTC, returns flagged for identity review, and returns with refund offsets all take longer.

**6. Not checking your Wage and Income Transcript before responding to a CP2000.**
The CP2000 is generated by matching your return against third-party information the IRS received. Before you respond, pull your Wage and Income Transcript and compare it to both your original return and the IRS's proposed adjustments. Sometimes the IRS is right (you omitted a 1099). Sometimes the IRS has incorrect information from a third party (a 1099 issued in error). You need the transcript to know which situation you are in.

**7. Missing the IP PIN enrollment window.**
The IP PIN you request is valid only for the tax year in which you file. New IP PINs are assigned each January. If you lose your IP PIN, retrieve it through your Online Account. If you cannot retrieve it, you may have to file by paper for that year and verify identity by mail.

**8. Requesting penalty abatement before becoming current.**
FTA and reasonable cause abatement are much harder to obtain while you still have unfiled returns or unpaid balances without a payment plan in place. Get current first, then request abatement.

**9. Using Form 4506 (return copy) when you only need a transcript.**
Form 4506 costs a fee and takes longer. If a lender, financial aid office, or the IRS itself needs your return information, a transcript is almost always acceptable and is free.

**10. Overlooking the refund offset notice.**
The Bureau of the Fiscal Service mails a separate offset notice before or around the same time as your refund would have arrived. Taxpayers sometimes discard this as junk mail. Read all official correspondence from federal agencies during refund season.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and contact your advisor before acting if any of the following apply:

- **You received a CP3219A (Statutory Notice of Deficiency).** The 90-day deadline is absolute and irreversible. Engage your advisor the same day the notice arrives.
- **You received an LT11, CP90, or any other notice referencing a levy or lien.** Collection action can escalate quickly. Your advisor can evaluate CDP hearing options, installment agreements, and whether an offer in compromise is appropriate.
- **The IRS is proposing an adjustment of more than $5,000.** Even if you believe the IRS is wrong, the stakes are high enough to warrant professional review before responding.
- **The CP2000 or other notice involves income from a pass-through entity (K-1), a business sale, real estate, cryptocurrency, or stock options.** The tax treatment of these items is complex and a mishandled response can make matters worse.
- **You have unfiled returns.** Responding to an IRS notice while having unfiled returns creates additional exposure. Your advisor can help sequence the resolution correctly.
- **You are considering an offer in compromise.** OICs require detailed financial disclosure and strict eligibility criteria. An accepted OIC can resolve your debt for less than you owe, but a rejected OIC can accelerate collection. Your advisor can assess whether you qualify before you apply.
- **You believe the IRS has made an error, but the deadline is within 15 days.** Do not wait for a perfect response. Your advisor can file a protective response to preserve your rights while the correct response is prepared.
- **You are in or considering bankruptcy.** Bankruptcy triggers complex interactions with tax debts, deadlines, and the CSED. Your advisor and bankruptcy counsel must coordinate.
- **A refund has been missing for more than 16 weeks after e-filing or more than 20 weeks after paper filing.** Your advisor or TAS can determine whether to file a formal hardship claim.

**Download name:** IRS Account, Transcript, Notice, and Refund Workflow Implementation Worksheet

**What it contains:** A fillable checklist and log organized in five tabs corresponding to the five parts of this playbook: (A) Online Account Setup, (B) Transcript Pull Log, (C) Notice Response Tracker, (D) Penalty Abatement Request Log, and (E) Refund Status Log.

**Where to use it in the workflow:**

- **Part C (notices):** Use the Notice Response Tracker tab as soon as you receive any IRS notice. Record the notice type, notice date, response deadline, response method (fax/upload/mail), date sent, and confirmation number or certified mail tracking number.
