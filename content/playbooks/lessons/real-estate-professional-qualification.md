# Real Estate Professional Qualification

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Real estate professional status (REPS) is a federal tax designation under Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 469(c)(7) that can allow a qualifying taxpayer to deduct unlimited rental property losses against wages, business income, and other non-rental income in the same tax year the losses occur. Without this status, rental losses are "passive" and are typically suspended until you sell the property or generate passive income to absorb them. Qualifying requires meeting two strict hour-based tests every single calendar year, and the IRS audits this status aggressively. This playbook walks you through every qualification requirement, the documentation you must maintain, and the elections you must file.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is for you if any of the following describe your situation:

- You own one or more rental properties (residential, commercial, short-term, or mixed-use) that produce losses, and those losses are currently suspended because you cannot use them against your other income.
- You or your spouse spends the majority of your total working time in real estate activities (property management, leasing, construction oversight, renovation, acquisition, brokerage, or development) and wants to convert those rental losses from passive to non-passive.
- Your household income exceeds $150,000, which means the standard $25,000 passive rental loss allowance (the fallback rule available to lower-income landlords) has phased out completely and you cannot deduct any rental losses at all without REPS.
- You are tracking toward 750 or more hours in real estate activities this year and want to understand what qualifies, what does not, and what records you must keep.
- You have large suspended passive losses from prior years (shown on Form 8582 from your last tax return) and want to evaluate whether qualifying for REPS would allow you to release those losses.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not pursue real estate professional status without first discussing your specific facts with your advisor if any of the following apply:

- **You or your qualifying spouse holds a full-time job outside real estate that consumes as many or more hours than your real estate activities.** REPS requires that MORE than half of all personal services across every trade and business during the year are in real property trades or businesses. A 40-hour-per-week non-real-estate job (roughly 2,000 hours per year) makes this threshold very difficult to meet unless your real estate activities consume even more time. Run the math before year-end.
- **You are a purely passive investor with no involvement in operations.** REPS requires active participation in real property trade or business activities. Investors who receive K-1 income from a fund, syndication, or REIT they do not manage will generally not be able to count those hours toward either REPS test.
- **You are employed by a real estate company but do not own more than 5% of that employer.** Hours worked as an employee in real estate do not count toward REPS unless you own more than 5% of the employer (the "5% owner" rule under IRC Section 469(c)(7) and Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-9). If you manage properties for someone else's company without a significant ownership stake, those employee hours are excluded.
- **Your rental losses are modest and your income is below $100,000.** If your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) is $100,000 or below, you may already be eligible to deduct up to $25,000 of rental losses against ordinary income under the active participation allowance in IRC Section 469(i), without needing full REPS qualification. Pursuing REPS involves significant recordkeeping burden; your advisor can confirm whether the simpler allowance already covers you.
- **You own only land, not structures.** Land is never depreciable and generates no depreciation losses. REPS provides its greatest benefit when combined with depreciation deductions from buildings or improvements. If you own land only, REPS does not produce a current-year deduction.
- **You plan to sell all rental properties imminently.** When you dispose of your entire interest in a passive activity in a fully taxable sale, all previously suspended passive losses from that activity become deductible in the year of sale, whether or not you ever qualify for REPS. If a sale is the near-term plan, the value of qualifying for REPS in the current year may be limited.

## Key Terms

**Passive activity:** Under IRC Section 469, a trade or business or rental activity in which you do not materially participate. Losses from passive activities can only offset income from other passive activities, not wages, self-employment income, or investment income. Rental real estate is treated as passive by default under IRC Section 469(c)(2), regardless of how much work you put into it, unless you qualify for REPS.

**Real estate professional status (REPS):** A federal tax designation under IRC Section 469(c)(7) that removes the "rental activities are always passive" presumption for a qualifying taxpayer. A taxpayer who meets both REPS tests may treat rental activities as non-passive IF they also materially participate in each rental property (or make a grouping election). REPS qualification alone does not make all rental losses deductible; the taxpayer must also satisfy material participation for each property.

**Non-passive income and losses:** Income or losses from trades, businesses, or activities in which you materially participate. Non-passive losses can offset any type of income: wages, self-employment income, investment income, or business income. A taxpayer who qualifies for REPS and materially participates in their rentals treats rental losses as non-passive.

**The two REPS tests (both must be met independently, every calendar year):**
- **More-than-50%-of-personal-services test:** More than half of all personal services you perform during the year across every trade and business are in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate. IRC Section 469(c)(7)(B)(i). "Personal services" means any work you do in connection with a trade or business. If you work 1,000 hours in real estate and 800 hours in a non-real-estate business, you pass this test (1,000 of 1,800 total hours = 55.6%). If you work 1,000 hours in real estate and 1,100 hours elsewhere, you fail (1,000 of 2,100 = 47.6%).
- **750-hour test:** You perform more than 750 hours of services during the year in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate. IRC Section 469(c)(7)(B)(ii). This is a hard floor. Reaching 748 hours does not qualify. There is no partial-year proration.

**Real property trade or business:** Defined in IRC Section 469(c)(7)(C) as any real property development, redevelopment, construction, reconstruction, acquisition, conversion, rental, operation, management, leasing, or brokerage trade or business. Hours in any of these activities count toward the REPS tests. Hours spent investing (researching stocks, attending general investment seminars, or participating as a passive investor in a fund) do not count.

**Material participation:** Active, regular, and continuous involvement in an activity. Defined through seven tests in Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-5T(a). You satisfy material participation for any single activity if you meet at least one of the following:
- Test 1: You spent more than 500 hours in the activity during the year.
- Test 2: Your participation in the activity was substantially all the participation of any individual (no paid helper or co-owner participated more than a token amount).
- Test 3: You spent more than 100 hours in the activity during the year AND at least as many hours as any other single person (including paid cleaners, property managers, or contractors).
- Tests 4 through 7 cover aggregation of significant participation activities, prior-year participation patterns (5 of 10 years for personal service activities, any 5 prior years for others), and a facts-and-circumstances test for participation over 100 hours with regular, continuous, and substantial involvement. Confirm with your advisor if you believe you may qualify under Tests 4 through 7.

For purposes of satisfying material participation in a rental property, a spouse's hours ARE counted (Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-9), even if the spouses file separately. This is an important distinction: for the REPS qualification tests themselves (750 hours and more-than-50%), each spouse must qualify independently; but when testing whether a particular activity meets a material participation test, you may count your spouse's hours.

**Grouping election (REPS grouping):** Under IRC Section 469(c)(7)(A) and Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-9(g), a real estate professional may elect to treat all rental real estate interests as a single rental activity for the purpose of testing material participation. Without the election, you must separately meet a material participation test for each property. With the election, your hours across all properties are combined and tested against a single threshold. The election is made by attaching a written statement to your tax return (see Step-By-Step Implementation for exact language). The election is binding in subsequent years unless you revoke it due to a material change in facts and circumstances.

**Suspended losses:** Passive activity losses from prior years that exceeded passive income in those years and were therefore not deductible. They are carried forward to future years on Form 8582. Suspended losses become deductible when (a) you generate passive income to absorb them, (b) you qualify for REPS and meet material participation (which releases future losses but generally does not retroactively release prior-year suspended losses that arose in years when you were not a REPS), or (c) you dispose of your entire interest in the passive activity in a fully taxable transaction.

**Active participation:** A lower standard than material participation, used only for the $25,000 passive rental loss allowance under IRC Section 469(i). Active participation means you make management decisions in a significant and bona fide sense: approving new tenants, setting rental terms, approving expenditures. You must own at least 10% of the activity by value. You do not need to satisfy any of the seven material participation tests to be an active participant, but you also cannot use suspended losses above the $25,000 cap under this standard.

**Form 8582 (Passive Activity Loss Limitations):** The IRS form used by noncorporate taxpayers (individuals, estates, and trusts) to calculate the amount of any passive activity loss allowed in the current year and to report the application of prior-year unallowed losses. If you have any rental property producing a loss, you or your advisor will almost certainly file Form 8582 with your Form 1040. The current form was last updated March 30, 2026. Form 8582 is also where suspended loss carryforwards from prior years are tracked.

**Schedule E (Form 1040):** The form used to report rental income and expenses. Rental property income and losses flow through Schedule E. If you qualify for REPS and materially participate, your Schedule E losses are marked as non-passive and flow through to your Form 1040 without limitation.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A 3.8% surtax under IRC Section 1411 on net investment income (which includes rental income) for taxpayers whose modified adjusted gross income exceeds $200,000 (single filers) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), or $125,000 (married filing separately). Rental income that qualifies as income from a real estate trade or business in which you materially participate (i.e., from a REPS-qualified taxpayer who also materially participates) may be excluded from NIIT if it meets the Section 162 trade or business safe harbor (participation in rental activities for more than 500 hours in the current tax year or in any 5 of the prior 10 years). Confirm your NIIT exposure with your advisor.

**MAGI (Modified Adjusted Gross Income):** For the $25,000 passive activity loss allowance, MAGI is generally your adjusted gross income (AGI) calculated without the rental real estate loss itself and without certain other deductions. Your tax software or advisor will calculate this; do not subtract the rental loss from your income when estimating MAGI for this threshold.

**5% owner (employee rule):** Under Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-9, hours worked as an employee do not count toward the REPS tests unless you own more than 5% of your employer. "5% owner" has the meaning given by IRC Section 416(i)(1)(B): owning more than 5% of the outstanding stock, or more than 5% of the total combined voting power of all stock of a corporate employer. For non-corporate employers, it means owning more than 5% of the capital or profits interest. Only hours worked during periods of qualifying ownership count.

**IRC Section 469:** The Internal Revenue Code section that created the passive activity loss rules. All references in this playbook to "passive activity rules" or "the passive activity limitation" refer to Section 469 and the regulations issued under it.

**IRC Section 416(i)(1)(B):** The statutory definition of "5-percent owner" used in the employee-hours exclusion rule, borrowed from the rules for top-heavy retirement plans.

**At-risk rules (IRC Section 465):** A separate limitation that caps your deductible loss to the amount you have financially "at risk" in an activity, generally your cash invested plus debt for which you are personally liable plus certain qualified nonrecourse financing on real property. The at-risk rules apply in addition to the passive activity rules. Even if a loss is non-passive (because you qualify for REPS and materially participate), the at-risk rules may further limit the deductible amount on highly leveraged properties.

**Form 4868:** The form used to request an automatic 6-month extension to file your individual income tax return (Form 1040). Filing Form 4868 by the April 15 due date extends your filing deadline to October 15.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following information before meeting with your advisor and before beginning the REPS qualification tracking process.

**Your time and work profile:**
1. Total hours you have worked or expect to work this tax year in all trades, businesses, and employment (real estate and non-real-estate combined). Include W-2 jobs, self-employment, consulting, and any other professional activity.
2. Total hours you have worked or expect to work this tax year specifically in real property trade or business activities (see the definition in Key Terms), broken out by property and by activity type (acquisition research, financing, leasing, tenant management, maintenance oversight, renovation management, property management tasks, etc.).
3. If you have a spouse: the same two breakdowns for your spouse.
4. Whether either spouse is employed by a real estate company, and if so, what percentage of that employer each spouse owns.

**Your property profile:**
5. Full address, property type, and entity holding title for each rental property you own (personal name, LLC, partnership, S-corp, trust).
6. Whether each property has produced a loss in the current year and, if so, the approximate amount.
7. The prior-year Form 8582 from your last-filed tax return, showing any suspended passive loss carryforward balance.
8. Whether you have made a grouping election in any prior year (look for a written statement attached to prior tax returns or ask your advisor).

**Your income profile:**
9. Estimated current-year modified adjusted gross income (MAGI). Your advisor will use this to determine whether the $25,000 allowance still provides any benefit and to model the NIIT exposure.
10. Whether your income includes wages, self-employment income, or other non-passive income that REPS-qualified rental losses would offset.

## Required Documents And Records

**Keep throughout the year, every year you intend to claim REPS:**

- **Contemporaneous time log:** A record created at or near the time of each activity (not reconstructed from memory at year-end). The log must show, for each entry: the date, the property or project involved, the number of hours worked, and a specific description of the task performed. Generic entries such as "real estate work, 2 hours" carry very little weight in an IRS audit. Specific entries are far stronger: "Drafted lease renewal for unit 4B and emailed to tenant, 45 minutes; Inspected roof damage and met with contractor for repair quote at 123 Main St, 2.5 hours; Reviewed and approved invoices for HVAC repair, 30 minutes." Keep the log in a spreadsheet, a dedicated app, or a dated calendar. Export or save the log at least monthly so no entries are lost.
- **Log of total hours in all non-real-estate activities:** To prove the more-than-50% test, you must also be able to document the denominator (all personal service hours across every activity). If you have a W-2 job, your annual W-2 and offer letter establish your typical work schedule. If you are self-employed in non-real-estate work, keep a separate log for those hours. If you have no other employment, a brief written statement explaining that real estate is your only trade or business can support the test.
- **Property-level records:** For each property, retain leases or rental agreements, contractor and service invoices, bank statements for the rental account, maintenance request records, and any correspondence with tenants. These records both support the deductions on Schedule E and corroborate your time log entries.
- **Prior-year Form 8582:** Keep every Form 8582 from every prior year you owned rental property. These show your suspended loss carryforward history and will be needed to calculate how prior suspended losses interact with any current-year REPS qualification.
- **Grouping election statement:** If you have ever made or plan to make the grouping election, keep a copy of the written election statement that was attached to your tax return. This is your evidence that the election is in effect and binding on future years.

**Retention guidance:**
Keep the time log, property records, and all REPS-related documentation for at least 6 years after the tax year they relate to. The IRS statute of limitations for audit is generally 3 years from the return due date, but extends to 6 years if income is understated by more than 25%. REPS is one of the most-audited positions on individual returns; keep records for the full 6 years. Keep records relating to depreciation deductions for as long as you own the property AND for at least 6 years after the year you sell it, since depreciation affects the gain calculation on sale.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Determine Whether You Can Qualify This Year

**Step 1: Identify the qualifying spouse (if filing jointly).**
Both REPS tests must be satisfied independently by one spouse. You cannot combine hours from two spouses to meet either test. Decide which spouse will be the "real estate spouse" for the year. This is the spouse whose hours in real property trades or businesses must exceed:
- 750 total hours in those activities, AND
- More than 50% of their total personal services hours across all trades and businesses.

The real estate spouse must not have a non-real-estate job or business that consumes equal or more hours than their real estate activities.

**Step 2: List all real property trade or business activities that count.**
Write down every activity in which you work during the year that qualifies under IRC Section 469(c)(7)(C): development, redevelopment, construction, reconstruction, acquisition, conversion, rental, operation, management, leasing, or brokerage of real property. These are the activities whose hours count toward the 750-hour test and the more-than-50% test. Create a list now and review it with your advisor so there is no disagreement at year-end about which activities qualify.

Activities that do NOT count toward REPS (even if real-estate-adjacent):
- Hours spent as a passive investor in a real estate fund or syndication you do not manage
- Attending general investment conferences or educational seminars not tied to a specific property or project you manage
- Hours working for a real estate employer in which you own 5% or less
- Financial planning or tax preparation related to your properties (this is personal financial management, not a real property trade or business)

**Step 3: Count your hours-to-date and project your year-end position.**
Mid-year (ideally at the end of each quarter), add up your logged real estate hours and compare them to:
- The 750-hour floor, and
- 50% of your total projected personal service hours for the year.

If you are on pace to reach both thresholds, continue. If you are behind on either test, discuss with your advisor whether you can increase qualifying real estate activity in the remaining months.

**Step 4: Confirm your employee status (if applicable).**
If the real estate spouse works for a real estate company as a W-2 employee, confirm with your advisor whether you own more than 5% of that employer. If you do not, those employee hours are excluded from your REPS hour count. This is a common qualification trap.

### Phase 2: Decide Whether to Make the Grouping Election

**Step 5: Evaluate the grouping election.**
Without the grouping election, you must separately meet a material participation test for EACH rental property. If you own three properties and work 150 hours across them combined (but fewer than 100 hours at any single property, and a contractor works more at one of the properties than you do), you may fail the material participation test for individual properties even though you qualify for REPS overall.

With the grouping election, all properties are combined into one activity. If you work 150 hours total across all three properties and no single non-owner works more than 150 hours combined across all three, you can satisfy material participation Test 3 for the combined activity.

The grouping election is binding for all subsequent years unless you can demonstrate a material change in facts and circumstances that justifies revocation. Once made, you cannot selectively un-group a profitable property to keep it passive while keeping others grouped. Consider this carefully before electing.

**When the grouping election is most beneficial:**
- You own multiple properties and your hours are distributed across several of them rather than concentrated in one.
- No single property individually reaches 100 hours of your personal activity.
- You are confident you will remain a real estate professional in future years.

**When NOT to make the grouping election:**
- You own one rental property (the election provides no benefit with a single property).
- One property is highly profitable and passive income from it offsets losses from others: grouping would combine the loss and income together, potentially eliminating losses you otherwise want to carry forward.
- Your future status as a real estate professional is uncertain.

Review this decision with your advisor before making the election.

**Step 6: Draft the grouping election statement (if electing).**
The election is made by attaching a written statement to your original, timely-filed tax return for the year the election is to take effect. Per the 2025 Schedule E instructions, the statement must declare that you are a qualifying taxpayer for the year and that you are making the election under IRC Section 469(c)(7)(A). A minimal compliant statement reads:

> "Pursuant to IRC Section 469(c)(7)(A) and Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-9(g), [Taxpayer Name], Social Security Number [XXX-XX-XXXX], hereby elects to treat all interests in rental real estate as a single rental real estate activity for the tax year ending December 31, [year] and all subsequent years."

Your advisor will draft and attach this statement to your return. If you missed the election on a timely-filed return, Revenue Procedure 2011-34 may allow a late election under certain conditions; confirm with your advisor.

### Phase 3: Track Hours Throughout the Year

**Step 7: Start or continue a real-time time log from January 1.**
There is no partial-year or prorated REPS election. Hours are counted from January 1 through December 31. Starting the log on March 15 means you have already lost 74 days of documented time. If you are reading this mid-year and have not logged hours, reconstruct what you can now (with the understanding that reconstructed records carry less audit weight than contemporaneous ones) and start logging in real time going forward.

For each entry, record:
- Date
- Property address or project name
- Activity type (tenant communication, maintenance coordination, leasing, acquisition research, renovation oversight, etc.)
- Exact hours and minutes
- Brief description of the specific task

**Step 8: Track non-real-estate hours separately.**
To prove the more-than-50% test, document the denominator. Keep a separate log or a clearly labeled section of your main log for hours in non-real-estate activities: your W-2 job (use your pay stubs and work schedule), other businesses you operate, consulting work, etc. Your advisor will divide your real estate hours by total hours at year-end to confirm the test is met.

**Step 9: Check your pace at each quarter-end.**
At the end of March, June, and September:
- Sum your logged real estate hours to date.
- Estimate remaining real estate hours for the year.
- Sum your logged total personal service hours to date.
- Confirm you are on track to meet both tests by December 31.

If you are at risk of missing the 750-hour floor or the 50% test in the fourth quarter, bring real estate projects forward if operationally possible (leasing activity, renovation planning, acquisition research) and document them thoroughly.

**Step 10: Year-end log review.**
By December 31, compile the full-year log. Add up:
- Total hours in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate (the REPS numerator and the 750-hour count).
- Total hours in all personal service activities (the REPS denominator for the 50% test).

Confirm both tests are met. Save the log as a PDF or printed document. Your advisor will reference the log when preparing your return.

### Phase 4: Confirm Material Participation for Each Property (or the Grouped Activity)

**Step 11: Test material participation for each property or the grouped activity.**
Apply the material participation tests from Key Terms to each property individually (if you did not make the grouping election) or to all properties combined (if you made the grouping election). You need to satisfy at least one of the seven tests.

For most active owner-operators:
- Test 3 (more than 100 hours AND at least as many hours as anyone else in the activity) is the most commonly achievable. Confirm your hours against any property manager, contractor, or cleaner who works on each property. If you use a property manager who puts in 200 hours per year on a specific property and you only work 110 hours on that property, you fail Test 3 for that property. Either increase your hours or switch to a property manager who works fewer hours, or make the grouping election.
- Test 1 (more than 500 hours) is the strongest, most audit-resistant test. It requires 500 hours in a single activity or the grouped activity.

**Step 12: Track your contractors' and managers' hours.**
If any paid helper (cleaner, property manager, contractor) works on your rental properties, estimate and document approximately how many hours they spend. You do not need their time logs, but you need to be able to credibly explain that you worked at least as many hours as they did. Ask your property manager for a time report if possible. This is one of the first questions an IRS agent asks when auditing material participation.

### Phase 5: File Your Return Correctly

**Step 13: Provide your advisor with the final time log and a summary.**
Before your tax appointment (or before providing your tax documents to your advisor), prepare a one-page summary of:
- Total logged hours in real property trade or business activities for the year: [X] hours.
- Total logged hours in all other trades and businesses: [Y] hours.
- Percentage of total hours in real property activities: X / (X + Y) = [Z]%.
- Confirm both tests are met: 750-hour test ([X] > 750, YES/NO), more-than-50% test ([Z] > 50%, YES/NO).
- Whether material participation is met for each property or the grouped activity.

**Step 14: Review Schedule E for correct passive/non-passive classification.**
On your Schedule E, losses from rental activities that are non-passive (because you qualify for REPS and materially participate) should be marked as non-passive. Your advisor will confirm that losses flow to the front page of Form 1040 as a deduction rather than being limited on Form 8582.

If you do NOT qualify for REPS this year, your advisor will complete Form 8582 to calculate any allowable passive loss (up to $25,000 if your MAGI is below $100,000, phased out completely at $150,000 MAGI) and carry forward any excess as a suspended loss.

**Step 15: Attach the grouping election statement (if making the election for the first time).**
If you decided in Step 5 to make the grouping election, confirm that your advisor attaches the written election statement (drafted in Step 6) to your original, timely-filed return. The statement must be on the return when it is first filed. It cannot be added by amendment after the fact under normal procedures.

**Step 16: Keep a copy of your time log with your tax records.**
Store the full-year time log (printed or as a PDF) with the copy of your tax return. The log is your primary audit defense document. Your advisor does not file the log with your return; it is kept in your files and produced if the IRS requests it.

### Phase 6: Year-End and Ongoing Maintenance

**Step 17: Start a new time log on January 1 of each new year.**
REPS qualification is determined year by year. A prior-year qualification does not carry forward. You must meet both tests fresh every calendar year. The grouping election remains in effect from a prior year (you do not re-make it annually), but the underlying qualification must be re-established each year.

**Step 18: Review your REPS position any time your work situation changes.**
If you take a new non-real-estate job, reduce your real estate activities, change property managers, or sell properties during the year, check with your advisor whether the change affects your ability to meet either REPS test for that year. A mid-year change can disqualify you for the entire year.

## Timing And Deadlines

- **REPS qualification is determined on December 31 of the tax year.** There is no partial-year REPS designation. You either meet both tests for the full calendar year or you do not. There is no election to be a partial-year real estate professional.
- **Time logs should be maintained in real time throughout the year.** At minimum, update your log monthly. Waiting until November or December to reconstruct the year from memory produces a log that will not survive an IRS audit.
- **The grouping election must be attached to your original, timely-filed tax return for the first year it is to take effect.** For the 2026 tax year, that is the Form 1040 due April 15, 2027 (or October 15, 2027, with a timely-filed Form 4868 extension). You cannot amend a return to add the grouping election after the original filing date under normal procedures. A late election may be available under Revenue Procedure 2011-34 if specific conditions are met; confirm with your advisor.
- **The quarterly pace check rhythm:** End of March (Q1 check), end of June (Q2 check), end of September (Q3 check), December 31 (final count).
- **Form 8582 is filed with your tax return (Form 1040) by April 15, 2027 for the 2026 tax year**, or October 15, 2027, with extension. If you do NOT qualify for REPS and have any rental losses or passive income, Form 8582 is required.
- **The filing extension (Form 4868)** must be filed by April 15, 2027, to extend your 2026 individual return to October 15, 2027. The extension extends the time to file, not the time to pay; any tax owed is still due April 15.

| IRS audit statute of limitations, 25%+ understatement | 6 years from return due date | IRC Section 6501(e) |

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure and where REPS applies:**
The passive/non-passive determination is made at the individual taxpayer level, not the entity level. If rental properties are held in a single-member LLC that is disregarded for tax purposes, the analysis is the same as if you owned the property individually. If properties are held in a multi-member LLC or partnership (taxed on Form 1065), the rental loss flows to you via Schedule K-1, and the passive/non-passive determination is made on your individual Form 1040 based on your REPS status and your material participation in the partnership's activities. If properties are held in an S corporation (Form 1120-S), losses pass through via K-1, and the same individual-level determination applies. Confirm the entity structure with your advisor, particularly if properties are held in a trust or family limited partnership, where the analysis can differ.

**C corporations:**
Passive activity rules under IRC Section 469 apply to personal service corporations and closely held corporations (those where 5 or fewer individuals own more than 50% of the stock), but the rules differ from those for individuals. REPS for individuals is not the same concept as for closely held C corporations, which have a separate test requiring more than 50% of gross receipts from real property trades or businesses. This playbook covers the individual taxpayer and pass-through entity context only. If you hold rental property in a C corporation, discuss the C-corp-specific passive activity rules with your advisor.

**Spousal REPS and community property states:**
In community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, Wisconsin), rental income and losses from property may be treated as owned 50/50 by each spouse. The interaction between community property rules and REPS qualification can be complex. Confirm with your advisor whether community property law affects which spouse's hours are relevant and whether the property's losses are attributed differently.

**State conformity:**
Most states follow the federal passive activity rules under IRC Section 469 and recognize REPS status if the federal tests are met. However, some states have their own passive activity regimes or do not conform to IRC Section 469 in all respects. California, for example, has its own passive activity rules that do not always mirror federal treatment. Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor before projecting net savings. State conformity laws change; do not rely on prior-year state treatment without a current-year confirmation.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) and REPS:**
Qualifying for REPS does not automatically exclude rental income from the 3.8% NIIT. A separate safe harbor applies under the Form 8960 instructions: rental income is excluded from NIIT if the rental activity qualifies as a Section 162 trade or business, which requires either (a) more than 500 hours of participation in the rental activity during the current tax year, or (b) more than 500 hours of participation in any 5 of the prior 10 tax years. Meet with your advisor specifically about NIIT if your MAGI exceeds the applicable threshold ($200,000 single, $250,000 married filing jointly) and you have net rental income.

**At-risk rules (IRC Section 465) apply in addition to passive activity rules:**
Even if your rental loss is non-passive due to REPS qualification, the at-risk rules independently cap your loss deduction to the amount you have "at risk" in the activity: generally your cash invested plus personally liable debt, plus qualified nonrecourse financing on real property. For highly leveraged properties, the at-risk rules may limit the deductible loss below what REPS alone would permit. Your advisor will calculate your at-risk basis when preparing your return.

**Self-employment tax:**
Rental income reported on Schedule E is generally not subject to self-employment tax (the 15.3% combined Social Security and Medicare tax on net earnings from self-employment). REPS qualification does not change this. However, if rental activities are conducted in a way that rises to the level of a Schedule C business (for example, certain short-term rentals with substantial personal services), some or all of the net income could become subject to self-employment tax. Confirm the correct reporting form with your advisor.

**Interaction with cost segregation and bonus depreciation:**
REPS is most powerful when combined with a cost segregation study and bonus depreciation, which accelerate large depreciation deductions into the current year. A REPS-qualified taxpayer who materially participates in a rental property that undergoes a cost segregation study can deduct those accelerated losses against wages or business income in the year the deductions are generated. The bonus depreciation rules under IRC Section 168(k) (as permanently amended by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Public Law 119-21, July 4, 2025) allow 100% bonus depreciation on qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. Confirm whether your property qualifies with your advisor; cost segregation is addressed in a separate Beyond Freedom Financial playbook.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Failing to track hours from January 1.**
REPS qualification is an annual determination based on the full calendar year. Clients who wait until October or November to begin logging hours and then try to reconstruct the year from memory frequently cannot produce records that survive an IRS audit. A reconstructed log created all at once, with round-number hour entries and no specific task descriptions, is one of the most common reasons REPS claims are rejected by IRS examiners and Tax Court judges. Start the log on January 1 and update it in real time.

**2. Focusing only on the 750-hour test and ignoring the more-than-50% test.**
Reaching 760 hours in real estate does not qualify you for REPS if you also worked 800 hours in a non-real-estate job or business. Both tests must be met. Clients with any W-2 employment, consulting practice, or other business outside real estate must count all of those hours when calculating the denominator. Review the more-than-50% math explicitly with your advisor before year-end.

**3. Counting employee hours that don't qualify.**
If you work in real estate as a W-2 employee and own 5% or less of your employer, those hours do not count toward REPS under Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-9. This surprises clients who work as a property manager, real estate agent, or construction manager for a company they do not substantially own. Confirm with your advisor whether your employee hours are countable.

**4. Combining spousal hours to meet either REPS test.**
Both REPS tests must be met independently by one spouse. The statute is explicit: "the requirements of the preceding sentence are satisfied if and only if either spouse separately satisfies such requirements." IRC Section 469(c)(7)(B). Clients sometimes incorrectly believe that if one spouse works 400 hours in real estate and the other works 400 hours, they jointly have 800 hours and qualify. This is wrong. One spouse must hit 750 hours and the more-than-50% test individually.

**5. Failing material participation at the property level after qualifying for REPS overall.**
REPS qualification is only the first step. You must also materially participate in each rental property (or in the grouped activity, if the election is made) to treat that property's losses as non-passive. A taxpayer who qualifies for REPS but uses a full-service property manager who works more hours on the property than the owner does will fail material participation Test 3. Clients who own multiple properties spread their hours thin; the grouping election is often the solution, but it requires careful planning.

**6. Making the grouping election without considering future implications.**
Once made, the grouping election is binding for all future years unless you can demonstrate a material change in facts. Grouping properties together can be a problem if one property later becomes profitable and passive income from that property would otherwise offset losses from others. Revocation requires a showing of material change in circumstances. Do not make the election on an impulse at tax time; plan it with your advisor.

**7. Not tracking contractor and property manager hours.**
A property manager who bills you for 15 hours per month (180 hours per year) at a single property means you must work at least 181 hours at that property to satisfy material participation Test 3. Clients frequently underestimate how many hours their vendors work. Ask your property manager for a breakdown of time spent on your property, and document it.

**8. Assuming prior-year suspended losses are automatically released by current-year REPS qualification.**
Qualifying for REPS in the current year releases current-year and future rental losses as non-passive, but it generally does not retroactively release suspended passive losses from years in which you did not qualify. Those prior-year suspended losses remain passive and can only be used against passive income or upon disposition of the activity. Confirm the treatment of your carryforward balance with your advisor.

**9. Missing the grouping election deadline.**
The grouping election must be attached to the original, timely-filed return for the year it is to take effect. It cannot normally be added by amending a return. Clients who decide in January (after the tax year has ended) that they should have made the election for the prior year typically cannot do so retroactively without meeting the specific conditions of Revenue Procedure 2011-34. Plan the election before December 31.

**10. Not confirming state tax treatment.**
A REPS position that saves significant federal income tax may produce a different or even adverse result in your state if the state does not conform to the federal passive activity rules or has its own separate calculation. Do not plan based on federal savings alone.

## Advisor Review Gates

Do not act on any of the following without first reviewing your specific facts with your advisor:

1. **Before declaring REPS status on your return:** Provide your advisor with the complete time log and both test calculations (750-hour count and percentage of personal services) before filing. REPS is among the most-audited positions on individual income tax returns. Documentation must be complete and your advisor must independently confirm both tests are met.

2. **Before making the grouping election:** Review the long-term implications. Once made, the election binds all future years. Understand how grouping interacts with any planned property sales, potential future profitable properties, and your expected REPS status in future years.

3. **Before the end of each quarter if you are at risk of missing either REPS test:** Do not wait until December 31 to discover you are 50 hours short. A quarterly check gives you time to bring qualifying activities forward.

4. **Before taking on a new non-real-estate job or significantly expanding a non-real-estate business:** These changes increase the denominator in the more-than-50% test and may disqualify you mid-year. Review the impact with your advisor before accepting new employment or substantially expanding non-real-estate business activity.

5. **Before relying on REPS to absorb a large cost segregation loss:** Confirm that you have separately met the material participation test for the property being studied, and that the at-risk rules do not limit the loss below what REPS alone would permit.

6. **Before selling a property that has suspended passive losses:** Confirm with your advisor whether the REPS position will release those losses in the year of sale or whether the sale itself triggers the release independently as a complete disposition of the activity.

7. **If you receive an IRS notice or are selected for examination:** Do not reconstruct or alter your time log after receiving a notice. Contact your advisor immediately. Provide only what you have already contemporaneously documented.

8. **If your work situation changes materially mid-year:** Starting a new W-2 job, leaving real estate activities, changing property managers, or selling a large property mid-year can all affect both REPS tests. Review with your advisor as soon as the change occurs, not at year-end.

## Before You Act

Do not report rental losses as non-passive on your tax return, attach a grouping election to your return, claim REPS status, or represent to any party that you qualify as a real estate professional without first confirming your specific facts with your advisor. The real estate professional designation is one of the most-audited positions in individual income tax. Both tests (the 750-hour test and the more-than-50%-of-personal-services test) must be met independently and supported by contemporaneous records. The consequences of a failed REPS position include recharacterization of all claimed non-passive losses as passive, possible suspension of those losses, accuracy-related penalties under IRC Section 6662, and interest on any underpayment. Confirm your hour count, confirm your documentation, and have your advisor independently review both tests before filing.
