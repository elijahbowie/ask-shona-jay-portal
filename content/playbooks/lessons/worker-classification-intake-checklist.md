# Worker Classification Intake Checklist

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Every time you pay someone for work, the IRS requires you to decide: is this person your employee or an independent contractor (a self-employed person providing services to your business)? Getting that decision wrong -- in either direction -- can trigger back payroll taxes, penalties, interest, and audits. This playbook and its attached checklist walk you through exactly how to gather the right facts about each worker relationship, apply the IRS three-category common-law test, identify the risk level of each classification, and document your conclusion. Done correctly, this intake process protects you before a problem arises and gives you a defensible paper trail if the IRS ever questions your classification decisions.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook applies to you if ANY of the following are true:

- You currently pay, or are about to pay, one or more individuals for services performed for your business and those individuals are not on a W-2 payroll.
- You are converting from a sole proprietor or single-person LLC to an S-corp or other entity structure and need to classify workers carried forward into the new entity.
- You have received an IRS or Department of Labor inquiry about a worker's employment status.
- You are preparing to file Form 1099-NEC for contractors and want to verify that each person you will report is properly classified as a non-employee.
- Your business model relies on a workforce of independent contractors and you have never formally documented the basis for each classification.
- You recently hired someone as a "1099 contractor" based on a verbal agreement or a contract alone, without analyzing the IRS factors.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **You have only W-2 employees and no independent contractors.** If every person who works for your business is already on payroll with a W-2, the classification decision has been made and documented through your payroll system. You do not need this checklist unless you are considering adding contractors.
- **The worker is a business entity, not an individual.** Payments to corporations (including S-corps and C-corps) for services generally are not subject to the same worker-classification analysis, though you should confirm the entity status in writing. Note: payments to single-member LLCs are treated as payments to the individual unless the LLC has elected corporate tax treatment.
- **You are classifying statutory employees or statutory nonemployees.** Certain categories of workers are classified by statute regardless of the common-law test: for example, licensed real estate agents and direct sellers are statutory nonemployees under IRC Section 3508. If you believe your worker falls into a statutory category, confirm with your advisor before applying this checklist.
- **You are a household employer paying domestic workers.** Household employment (nannies, housekeepers, gardeners) is governed by Schedule H and different thresholds. This playbook covers business workers, not household employees.

## Key Terms

**Independent contractor:** A self-employed individual who provides services to a business but is not legally its employee. The business does not withhold income taxes, Social Security, or Medicare taxes from payments to independent contractors. The contractor is responsible for paying self-employment tax on their own earnings.

**Employee (common-law employee):** A worker whom the IRS considers your employee based on the degree of control you exercise over what they do and how they do it, regardless of any contract or label you apply. Employees are subject to FICA withholding, federal income tax withholding, and FUTA.

**Worker classification:** The IRS determination of whether a person performing services is an employee or an independent contractor for federal tax purposes. The classification governs which taxes are owed by whom and which federal information return must be filed.

**Common-law test:** The IRS standard for classifying workers. Under the common law, a worker is your employee if you have the right to control not just the result of the work, but the details of how the work is done. The IRS groups the evidence into three categories: behavioral control, financial control, and type of relationship. No single factor is automatically determinative; you must weigh the full picture.

**Behavioral control:** One of the three IRS categories. It covers evidence of whether the business directs or has the right to direct what the worker does and how they do it. This includes instructions about when, where, and how to work; what tools to use; what sequence to follow; where to purchase supplies; and whether the business trains the worker in its methods. If the business can tell the worker exactly how to do the job, that points toward employee status.

**Financial control:** One of the three IRS categories. It covers the business aspects of the work: whether the worker has unreimbursed business expenses, whether the worker has invested in their own equipment or facilities, whether the worker makes their services available to the general market (not just one client), how the worker is paid (hourly vs. flat fee vs. project), and whether the worker can realize a profit or incur a loss. Workers who bear their own business risk and work for multiple clients point toward independent contractor status.

**Type of relationship:** One of the three IRS categories. It covers how the parties have structured and described the relationship: whether there is a written contract, whether the business provides benefits (health insurance, pension, paid time off), how permanent the arrangement is, and whether the services are central to the business's core operations. Ongoing, permanent work that is integral to the business points toward employee status.

**Misclassification:** Treating a worker as an independent contractor when the IRS would classify them as an employee under the common-law test. Misclassification exposes the business to back payroll taxes (both the employer share and, under IRC Section 3509, a portion of the employee share), interest, penalties, and potential FUTA liability.

**IRC Section 3509:** The Internal Revenue Code provision that sets the reduced tax rates a business owes when the IRS reclassifies independent contractors as employees. Under Section 3509(a) (where required Forms 1099-NEC were filed), the rates are: 1.5% of wages for federal income tax withholding (FITW) and 9.18% for FICA (the full 7.65% employer share plus 20% of the 1.53% employee share), for a combined rate of approximately 10.68%. Under Section 3509(b) (where Forms 1099-NEC were not filed), the FITW rate rises to 3.0% and the FICA rate to 10.71%, for a combined rate of approximately 13.71%. These rates do not apply if the business intentionally disregarded the withholding requirement; in that case, full rates apply.

**Section 530 relief:** A provision in the Revenue Act of 1978, extended indefinitely by the Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act of 1982, that protects businesses from employment tax liability for misclassification if: (1) they filed all required information returns (such as Form 1099-NEC) consistently treating the worker as a non-employee, (2) they consistently treated the worker and all similar workers as non-employees, and (3) they had a reasonable basis for the non-employee treatment (such as prior IRS audit, court decision, or long-standing industry practice). Section 530 relief does not protect a business that failed to file required 1099s.

**Form 1099-NEC:** Nonemployee Compensation. The federal information return you file to report payments of $2,000 or more made to an independent contractor during 2026 (the threshold for payments made in 2026 increased from $600 to $2,000 under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025; the $2,000 amount will be adjusted for inflation beginning in 2027). You must furnish a copy to the contractor and file a copy with the IRS by January 31 of the following year. For payments made in 2026, the filing deadline is January 31, 2027.

**Form SS-8:** Determination of Worker Status for Purposes of Federal Employment Taxes and Income Tax Withholding. Either the business or the worker can submit this form to ask the IRS to officially determine a worker's employment status. The IRS review process typically takes at least six months and results in a binding written determination. Filing Form SS-8 is appropriate when classification is genuinely unclear and the financial stakes are high enough to warrant waiting for an official ruling.

**Form 8952:** Application for Voluntary Classification Settlement Program (VCSP). Filed by businesses that want to voluntarily reclassify workers from independent contractors to employees for future periods. Must be filed at least 120 days before the intended reclassification start date.

**VCSP (Voluntary Classification Settlement Program):** An IRS program that allows businesses to reclassify independent contractors as employees going forward, in exchange for paying a reduced tax amount for prior years. The payment equals 10% of the employment tax liability on compensation paid to the workers in the most recent tax year, calculated at the reduced Section 3509(a) rates, with no interest or penalties. To qualify, the business must have: (1) consistently filed all required Forms 1099 for the workers over the prior three years, (2) not be under IRS or Department of Labor audit, and (3) not be currently contesting worker classification in court.

**FICA:** Federal Insurance Contributions Act taxes. Consist of Social Security tax (6.2% each for employer and employee on wages up to $184,500 in 2026) and Medicare tax (1.45% each for employer and employee, with no wage cap). For 2026, the combined FICA rate is 15.3% on wages up to the $184,500 Social Security wage base. Employees also owe an additional 0.9% Medicare tax on wages exceeding $200,000 per year (no employer match on this additional amount).

**FUTA:** Federal Unemployment Tax Act. The employer-only federal unemployment tax, assessed at 6.0% on the first $7,000 of wages paid to each employee per year. Employers who make timely state unemployment contributions receive a credit of up to 5.4%, reducing the net federal FUTA rate to 0.6% on the first $7,000 per employee (maximum $42 per employee per year at the net rate). Independent contractors are not subject to FUTA.

**Self-employment tax:** The tax independent contractors pay on their net self-employment income to fund Social Security and Medicare. The rate is 15.3% on net self-employment earnings up to $184,500 in 2026 (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare), and 2.9% on net earnings above $184,500. Contractors pay both the "employer" and "employee" shares themselves, though they can deduct half of the self-employment tax on their personal Form 1040.

**Form W-9:** Request for Taxpayer Identification Number and Certification. The form you ask independent contractors to complete before you pay them. It provides the contractor's legal name, business name (if any), federal tax classification, address, and Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN). You use the W-9 information to prepare Form 1099-NEC at year end.

**ABC test:** A state-level alternative worker-classification standard used in some states, including California (under AB5), New Jersey, and Massachusetts. Unlike the federal common-law test, the ABC test presumes a worker is an employee unless the hiring business can prove all three of the following: (A) the worker is free from the hiring entity's control and direction, (B) the worker performs work outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business, and (C) the worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, or business. Failing any one prong means the worker is a state-law employee regardless of the federal outcome. Some states apply the ABC test for unemployment purposes but use a different test for income tax withholding; confirm your state's rules with your advisor.

**EIN:** Employer Identification Number. A nine-digit number (format XX-XXXXXXX) the IRS assigns to business entities. If you have a business structured as an LLC, partnership, corporation, or S-corp, you already have an EIN. You need an EIN to file payroll tax returns and to report payments on Form 1099-NEC.

**TIN:** Taxpayer Identification Number. An umbrella term for any number the IRS uses to identify a taxpayer: Social Security Numbers (SSNs) for individuals, EINs for businesses, or ITINs (Individual Taxpayer Identification Numbers) for individuals not eligible for an SSN. You must obtain the contractor's TIN before making payments to avoid backup withholding requirements.

**Backup withholding:** A 24% withholding that applies when a contractor does not provide a valid TIN, provides an incorrect TIN, or has been notified by the IRS that they are subject to backup withholding. If backup withholding applies, you must withhold 24% of all payments to that contractor and remit it to the IRS.

**Form 941:** Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return. Filed by businesses that have employees to report wages paid and payroll taxes owed each quarter. If all your workers are properly classified as independent contractors, you may not need to file Form 941 (unless you have separate W-2 employees). If you later reclassify workers as employees, Form 941 becomes a required quarterly filing.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts for each worker relationship before completing the classification analysis:

1. **Worker's legal name and address:** The full legal name and mailing address of the individual. Required for Form W-9 and Form 1099-NEC.
2. **Worker's TIN:** Social Security Number or EIN. Required before the first payment and for Form 1099-NEC filing. Collect via Form W-9.
3. **Description of services:** A plain-language description of what the worker actually does for your business. Be specific (for example, "designs social media graphics for our Instagram account" rather than "graphic design work").
4. **Who controls how the work is done:** Can you (or do you) tell this worker when to show up, what tools to use, what steps to follow, and in what sequence? Or does the worker decide all of that independently?
5. **Worker's investment in equipment or facilities:** Does the worker use their own computer, vehicle, tools, or workspace? Or do you supply the equipment and workspace?
6. **Whether the worker works for other clients:** Does this person provide the same or similar services to other businesses, or do they work exclusively for you?
7. **Payment method:** Is the worker paid by the hour or by salary (employee indicators), or by project, flat fee, or output (contractor indicators)?
8. **Reimbursement of expenses:** Does the worker submit expense reports for you to reimburse? Or do they absorb their own business costs?
9. **Written contract:** Is there a written agreement? If yes, obtain a copy. Note: a contract that calls the worker a "contractor" does not control; the IRS looks at the actual working relationship, not the label.
10. **Benefits provided:** Does the worker receive health insurance, paid time off, retirement contributions, or other benefits from your business?
11. **Duration and permanency:** Is the engagement for a defined project or time period, or is it open-ended with no set end date?
12. **Centrality of the work to your business:** Is the service this worker provides core to what your business sells or delivers, or is it a peripheral support function?
13. **Training provided:** Did you (or do you) train this worker in your business methods, processes, or systems, beyond an initial onboarding briefing?
14. **States where the work is performed:** In which state(s) does the worker physically perform services? State law may impose a different classification standard (see the ABC test definition above and the caveats section).
15. **Total payments in 2026:** How much have you paid or do you expect to pay this worker during the 2026 calendar year? This determines your Form 1099-NEC filing obligation.

## Required Documents And Records

**Documents to collect before or at the start of the engagement:**

- **Completed Form W-9** from the contractor, signed and dated before the first payment. Retain in your records.
- **Written services agreement or contract:** Even for informal arrangements, a short written agreement describing the scope of work, payment terms, and the contractor's independent status. The agreement should note that the contractor is responsible for their own taxes, uses their own tools, and can work for other clients.
- **Completed Worker Classification Checklist** (the attached download): completed and signed for each worker at the start of the engagement, with your classification conclusion documented.
- **Evidence supporting the classification decision:** any materials you reviewed (industry practice, prior IRS guidance, court decisions, or business license of the contractor showing an independently established business).

**Ongoing and annual records to maintain:**

- **Annual re-evaluation using the Worker Classification Checklist:** Complete a fresh checklist annually for each contractor relationship that continues into the next year, because the facts can change (for example, a contractor who started working exclusively for you is developing a different risk profile than in the prior year).
- **Form 1099-NEC copies:** Retain copies of all Forms 1099-NEC filed, along with proof of timely filing (electronic submission confirmation or certified mail receipt). File with the IRS and furnish to the contractor by January 31, 2027 for payments made in 2026.
- **Payment records:** Retain all invoices, receipts, or payment confirmations from each contractor showing the date, amount, and description of services. These are the underlying support for Form 1099-NEC and your classification defense.
- **W-9 updates:** Obtain a new W-9 if the contractor's name, address, or TIN changes, or if there is a change in the contractor's business entity structure (for example, they form an LLC that elects S-corp status).

**Retention guidance:**

The IRS generally has three years from the filing date to assess additional taxes, but may extend to six years if income is understated by more than 25%, and there is no statute of limitations on fraud. Retain all worker classification records (classification checklists, W-9s, contracts, payment records, and 1099-NEC copies) for a minimum of six years after the tax year to which they relate. Retain the underlying classification analysis (the completed checklist and your written rationale) indefinitely, because misclassification disputes can arise years after the fact and the burden of proof rests on you.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Step 1: Identify each worker relationship that requires classification

Make a list of every individual or single-member LLC you pay for services who is NOT currently on your W-2 payroll. Include all current arrangements and any new ones you are starting. Do not limit the list to people you pay above the $2,000 Form 1099-NEC threshold; classification applies to every service relationship regardless of dollar amount. You only file a 1099-NEC for payments of $2,000 or more in 2026, but the underlying tax treatment is required regardless of whether a 1099 is filed.

**Output:** A list of every non-payroll worker by name, with the nature of the services and total payments to date in 2026.

### Step 2: Collect a Form W-9 from each contractor

Before making any payment to an individual contractor, request a completed and signed Form W-9. The W-9 asks for: full legal name, business name (if any), federal tax classification (individual/sole proprietor, LLC, C-corp, S-corp, partnership), address, and TIN (Social Security Number or EIN).

- If the contractor does not provide a TIN or provides an incorrect one, you must apply backup withholding of 24% to all payments.
- If the contractor indicates they are a C-corp or S-corp on the W-9, payments to that entity are generally exempt from 1099-NEC reporting (though you should still obtain the W-9 for your records).
- File the signed W-9 in a secure location and retain it for the full retention period.

**Output:** A signed, dated Form W-9 on file for each contractor before the first payment.

### Step 3: Complete the Worker Classification Checklist for each worker

Open the attached Worker Classification Checklist. Complete one checklist per worker. The checklist walks you through each factor in the IRS three-category common-law test: behavioral control, financial control, and type of relationship.

For each factor, record:
- What the actual working arrangement is (not what your contract says, but what actually happens).
- Whether that factor points toward employee status, toward independent contractor status, or is neutral.

Do not skip factors because they seem obvious. The IRS examines the total picture, and a weak checklist with blank fields is worse than a completed one that honestly reflects some employee-pointing factors.

**Output:** A completed Worker Classification Checklist for each worker, with every factor addressed in writing.

### Step 4: Tally the checklist and assign a risk level

After completing the checklist, count the number of factors pointing toward employee status and the number pointing toward independent contractor status. Assign a risk level using this framework:

- **Low risk (contractor classification well-supported):** The majority of factors point toward independent contractor status, including the most heavily weighted ones: the worker provides services to multiple clients, uses their own tools and workspace, controls how the work is done, can realize a profit or loss, and the work is not integral to your core business.
- **Moderate risk (mixed factors):** A mix of employee-pointing and contractor-pointing factors. The worker may work primarily for you, or you may provide some direction over how the work is done, but the worker controls significant aspects of the engagement.
- **High risk (employee indicators dominate):** Most factors point toward employee status: you control how and when the work is done, you supply tools, the worker works only for you, the relationship is ongoing with no defined end date, and the services are central to your business.

**Output:** A written risk-level assessment attached to or incorporated into the checklist.

### Step 5: Document your classification decision and reasoning

Write a short classification memo (even one paragraph is sufficient) for each worker stating:

- The classification you have concluded (employee or independent contractor).
- The three to five most significant factors supporting that conclusion.
- The risk level you assigned.
- Any factors that cut the other way and why they did not change the overall conclusion.
- The date the analysis was completed and by whom.

For low-risk, clearly-contractor relationships, a brief note on the checklist itself is sufficient. For moderate- or high-risk relationships, a more detailed written memo is appropriate.

**Output:** Signed, dated classification memo or annotated checklist for each worker.

### Step 6: Resolve high-risk classifications before paying (or continuing to pay)

If Step 4 results in a high-risk conclusion, stop and take one of the following actions before making another payment:

- **Option A -- Convert to employee:** Add the worker to your payroll as a W-2 employee. Set up payroll withholding, enroll in EFTPS, and run the first paycheck per your payroll setup. This eliminates the misclassification risk going forward.
- **Option B -- Apply for VCSP:** If you want to reclassify going forward but limit liability for prior years, complete Form 8952 and apply for the VCSP. The form must be submitted at least 120 days before the desired reclassification date. The VCSP requires that you have filed all required Forms 1099-NEC for the prior three years.
- **Option C -- File Form SS-8:** If the classification is genuinely uncertain and you need an official IRS determination, file Form SS-8. The IRS typically takes at least six months to respond. Do not use this option as a delay tactic; filing Form SS-8 does not protect you from liability during the period before the determination.
- **Option D -- Restructure the engagement:** Modify the working arrangement so that it more clearly reflects independent contractor status: require the worker to use their own tools, allow them to work for other clients, pay by project rather than hourly, remove training requirements, and document the change.

Confirm with your advisor which option is appropriate before implementing.

**Output:** A documented decision on how to resolve each high-risk relationship, with a timeline for implementation.

### Step 7: Obtain Section 530 safe-harbor documentation (if applicable)

If you have been treating workers as independent contractors and are concerned about prior-year exposure, gather evidence that supports Section 530 relief:

- Copies of all prior-year Forms 1099-NEC filed for these workers (shows reporting consistency).
- Evidence of a reasonable basis for the non-employee treatment: a prior IRS audit that accepted the classification, a court decision supporting it, or written documentation of longstanding industry practice of treating this type of worker as a contractor.
- A written memo noting that you have consistently treated this worker and all workers in the same role as non-employees.

This documentation does not guarantee Section 530 protection, but it is the starting point for a potential defense. Review the documentation with your advisor.

**Output:** Section 530 documentation file, if applicable.

### Step 8: File Form 1099-NEC for qualifying payments

For each contractor you paid $2,000 or more during the 2026 calendar year, file Form 1099-NEC:

- Obtain the contractor's information from their Form W-9.
- Report the total nonemployee compensation in Box 1.
- Furnish Copy B to the contractor and file Copy A with the IRS by January 31, 2027.
- If you submit 10 or more information returns (counting all types, not just 1099-NEC), you must file electronically through the IRS IRIS Taxpayer Portal or the FIRE system.
- If you submit fewer than 10, paper filing is permitted but electronic is recommended.
- If you have applied backup withholding (24%) to any payments, report the withheld amount in Box 4.

**Output:** Timely-filed Form 1099-NEC for each contractor paid $2,000 or more in 2026, with electronic submission confirmation saved.

### Step 9: Set a calendar reminder for annual re-evaluation

Worker classification is not a one-time decision. Relationships evolve, and a worker who was clearly a contractor in Year 1 may have developed an employee-pointing profile by Year 3.

Set a calendar reminder in October or November of each year to:

- Pull the current Worker Classification Checklist for each ongoing contractor relationship.
- Update the checklist to reflect any changes in the working arrangement over the past year.
- Assign a new risk level.
- Determine whether any relationships need to be restructured or converted to employee status before year-end.

**Output:** Annual review completed and documented before December 31 of each year.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity caveats:**

- **LLC treatment depends on elections.** If a contractor tells you they operate through an LLC, ask whether the LLC is taxed as a sole proprietor (disregarded entity), partnership, S-corp, or C-corp. A single-member LLC with no corporate election is disregarded for federal tax purposes and is treated the same as an individual for 1099-NEC reporting. An LLC that has elected S-corp or C-corp treatment does not require a 1099-NEC. Collect a Form W-9 to confirm.
- **S-corp owner-employees cannot be treated as contractors for their own S-corp.** If the person you want to classify as a contractor is also a shareholder in your business entity, different rules apply. S-corp officers who perform services are employees by law; paying them via 1099 instead of payroll is itself a classification error. See the S-Corp Owner Payroll / Reasonable Compensation playbook for that situation.
- **Joint ventures and partnerships.** If two businesses jointly provide services and one pays the other, the payment is generally between entities and is not the same as paying an individual worker. But if the arrangement is actually structured to disguise an employment relationship, the IRS can look through the structure.

**Payroll caveats:**

- **A 1099 contract does not make someone a contractor.** The written agreement is one factor, and a relatively weak one. The IRS looks at the economic reality of the relationship, not the label in the contract. A worker who is called a "contractor" in writing but is told exactly how, when, and where to work is still an employee under the common-law test.
- **Classification is worker-by-worker, not role-by-role.** Two workers doing the same type of work can have different classifications if the actual working arrangements differ. Document each relationship separately.
- **Reclassification is not retroactive under VCSP.** The VCSP only covers prospective reclassification. It does not eliminate prior-year liability for periods before the program takes effect; it only reduces that liability for the most recent prior year.

**State caveats:**

- **Many states use the ABC test, which is stricter than the federal common-law test.** If your workers perform services in California, New Jersey, Massachusetts, Connecticut, or other ABC-test states, federal classification as an independent contractor does not guarantee state compliance. A worker who passes the federal three-category test may still be a state-law employee under the ABC test. Confirm state-specific classification rules with your advisor for each state where work is performed.
- **State wage and hour laws add additional exposure.** Even where a state uses the common-law test for tax purposes, state labor law may have its own standard for minimum wage, overtime, and benefits that applies to workers who are misclassified. Worker classification errors can trigger state labor audits that are separate from IRS scrutiny.
- **Some states require 1099-NEC copies to be filed with the state.** Filing with the IRS does not automatically satisfy state reporting requirements. Check your state's information return filing rules.

**Current-year caveats:**

- **The 1099-NEC threshold changed for 2026.** As of January 1, 2026, the reporting threshold for Form 1099-NEC is $2,000 per contractor per year, up from $600, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21). This means fewer 1099s are required, but tax obligations on all payments remain unchanged. Contractors and businesses must still report all income even if no 1099 is issued.
- **The threshold will be adjusted for inflation starting in 2027.** The $2,000 amount is not permanent in dollar terms; it will be adjusted annually for inflation beginning in calendar year 2027. Verify the current threshold each January.
- **Federal DOL independent contractor rules are in flux.** The Department of Labor's "economic reality" test for Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) purposes is separate from the IRS common-law test and has been subject to regulatory changes. The IRS classification and the DOL classification can produce different outcomes for the same worker. This playbook covers federal tax classification only; labor-law classification is a separate question for your advisor.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Relying on the contract label instead of the economic reality.**
Writing "independent contractor" in a services agreement does not create contractor status. The IRS disregards labels and looks at who controls the work, who bears business risk, and how permanent the relationship is. A contract that says "contractor" but describes a relationship with daily direction, exclusive work, and employer-provided tools will not protect you.

**2. Not collecting Form W-9 before the first payment.**
If a contractor refuses to provide a TIN or you forget to collect the W-9, you are required to withhold 24% of all payments (backup withholding). Missing this step also makes it harder to file accurate Forms 1099-NEC and easier for the IRS to flag the payments during an audit.

**3. Treating workers as contractors because they asked to be.**
A worker's preference is not a classification factor. If the economic reality of the relationship says "employee," the worker is an employee regardless of what either party prefers or what any contract says.

**4. Never re-evaluating classification as the relationship evolves.**
A worker who starts as a genuine contractor -- working for multiple clients, using their own tools, taking on project-by-project work -- can drift into employee territory if over time they stop working for other clients, start working exclusively for you, and begin receiving more direction. Failing to catch this drift means you are accumulating misclassification exposure year over year.

**5. Skipping the checklist for workers below the 1099 threshold.**
The $2,000 threshold determines whether you file a Form 1099-NEC, not whether you have to classify the worker. If you pay a contractor $1,800 in 2026, you do not file a 1099-NEC, but the person is still either your employee or contractor for tax purposes. If they are an employee, you owe payroll taxes on those wages regardless of whether a 1099 was filed.

**6. Filing a Form SS-8 hoping to delay an IRS problem.**
Form SS-8 is a legitimate classification tool, but it does not pause IRS enforcement, stop a Department of Labor audit, or protect you from liability during the review period. If your classification is high-risk, address it proactively rather than filing Form SS-8 as a delay tactic.

**7. Assuming VCSP eliminates all prior exposure.**
The VCSP covers federal employment tax liability for the most recent prior year at reduced rates. It does not eliminate state employment tax exposure, Department of Labor penalties, or claims by workers for employee benefits they were denied while misclassified.

**8. Failing to file 1099-NEC forms and losing Section 530 protection.**
One of the three requirements for Section 530 relief is that you filed all required federal information returns (1099-NEC forms) consistently. If you treated workers as contractors but never filed the required 1099s, you lose the Section 530 safe harbor entirely, and the higher Section 3509(b) rates apply to any reclassification.

**9. Using the same classification analysis for every state.**
Federal contractor status does not carry over automatically to states that use the ABC test. Many businesses focus on IRS compliance and discover only during a state audit that they owe state unemployment taxes, state income tax withholding, and penalties for all the years the worker performed services in an ABC-test state.

**10. No written rationale for the classification decision.**
The burden of proof in a misclassification dispute rests on you. An unwritten classification decision -- even a correct one -- leaves you with nothing to present in an audit. The completed Worker Classification Checklist, with your notes on each factor, is the document that converts a good decision into a defensible one.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and confirm with your advisor before acting in any of the following situations:

1. **A new contractor engagement is moderate- or high-risk on the checklist.** Do not make the first payment until you have reviewed the classification with your advisor.
2. **A worker currently classified as a contractor has begun working exclusively for your business.** Exclusive work is a significant employee indicator. Discuss whether the relationship needs to be restructured or the worker needs to be converted to employee status.
3. **A worker or a government agency (IRS, Department of Labor, state unemployment agency) has challenged the classification.** Do not respond to any notice or inquiry without advisor review first.
4. **You are considering applying for the VCSP.** VCSP eligibility requirements are strict (three years of consistent 1099 filing, no active audits, no pending court challenges). Have your advisor confirm eligibility before filing Form 8952.
5. **A contractor performs services in California, New Jersey, Massachusetts, or any other state you know uses the ABC test.** The federal analysis alone is not sufficient. Get state-specific guidance before classifying.
6. **You have not filed 1099-NEC forms for contractors in prior years.** This affects both your Section 530 relief eligibility and your exposure under Section 3509(b). Have your advisor assess the prior-year risk before you file corrected or late forms.
7. **The total annual payments to a single contractor exceed $50,000.** At this payment level, the financial stakes of a misclassification error are high enough to warrant a formal advisor review and a written classification opinion.
8. **You are converting a former employee to a contractor role.** Converting a worker from W-2 to 1099 status is one of the highest-scrutiny situations in IRS enforcement. The analysis must show genuine changes in the working relationship, not merely a paperwork reclassification. Have your advisor review the restructuring before implementing it.
9. **A contractor files Form SS-8 with the IRS.** When a worker files Form SS-8, the IRS notifies you and requests your response. You have a limited window to respond. Engage your advisor immediately upon receiving this notice.
10. **You are unsure whether your state or the state where the work is performed uses the ABC test.** State rules vary and change. Do not assume the federal analysis is the only applicable standard.

## Using The Download(s)

**Download: Worker Classification Checklist**

This checklist is the central working document for this playbook. It is a structured questionnaire that walks you through every factor in the IRS common-law three-category test and produces a documented, signed classification conclusion for each worker.

**When to use it:** Complete one checklist for each worker or contractor at the beginning of every new service engagement. Return to it annually for ongoing relationships to update the facts and re-assess the risk level.

**How to fill it out:**

- **Section 1 -- Worker Information:** Enter the worker's name, contact information, TIN (from their Form W-9), the date the engagement started, and the type of services they provide.
- **Section 2 -- Behavioral Control Factors:** Answer each question about direction and control over the work. Note what actually happens, not what the contract says.
- **Section 3 -- Financial Control Factors:** Answer each question about the business economics of the arrangement: who supplies tools, whether the worker works for others, how payments are structured, and whether the worker can profit or lose.
- **Section 4 -- Relationship Factors:** Answer each question about the nature of the arrangement: written contract terms, benefits, permanency, and centrality of the work to your business.
- **Section 5 -- Risk Level and Conclusion:** Tally the factors, assign a risk level (low, moderate, or high), record your classification conclusion (employee or independent contractor), and note the action you are taking for any moderate- or high-risk relationship.
- **Signature block:** Sign and date the completed checklist. If your advisor has reviewed the classification, have them sign or provide a written confirmation to attach.

**Where to file it:** File the completed checklist with the corresponding Form W-9 and any written services agreement in a dedicated folder (physical or digital) for that worker. Retain for the full retention period described in the Required Documents And Records section.

## Before You Act

Stop here and confirm the following with your advisor before you pay a contractor, reclassify a worker, apply for the VCSP, file a Form SS-8, or respond to any IRS or Department of Labor inquiry:

- Every worker you are treating as an independent contractor has a completed Worker Classification Checklist on file showing a documented, reasoned conclusion.
- You have a signed Form W-9 on file for each contractor.
- Any moderate- or high-risk classification has been reviewed by your advisor and either confirmed or resolved.
- You have identified every state where each contractor performs services and confirmed the applicable state classification standard.
- You have filed (or are on track to file) Form 1099-NEC for all contractors paid $2,000 or more in the 2026 calendar year by January 31, 2027.
- You understand that changing the label of the relationship (calling someone a "contractor" in a contract) does not change the underlying classification if the economic reality says otherwise.

The cost of misclassification -- back taxes, interest, Section 3509 penalties, state penalties, and potential Department of Labor exposure -- can far exceed the cost of getting the classification right from the beginning. Complete the intake process for every non-payroll worker before the first payment, not after a problem arises.
