# Short-Term Rental Strategy

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The short-term rental (STR) strategy lets owners of Airbnb-style vacation or short-stay rental properties convert what would otherwise be passive (locked-up) tax losses into active deductions that offset wages, business profits, or any other income. The key is a provision in Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A): when guests stay an average of 7 days or fewer per booking, the property is not treated as a "rental activity" under the passive activity rules. Instead it is treated as a trade or business, and losses from a trade or business in which you materially participate (meaning you are actively and regularly involved in running it) can be deducted against any income -- not just other rental income. Combined with accelerated depreciation (particularly after the One Big Beautiful Bill Act restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying property), an STR can produce five- or six-figure deductions in a single year that directly reduce what you owe in federal income tax.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is for you if all of the following are true:

- You own, or are actively considering purchasing, a residential property you rent to guests for short stays (vacation home, cabin, beach house, mountain retreat, or similar).
- The average length of each guest stay is 7 days or fewer (explained under Key Terms below), or you expect to set a booking policy that achieves that average.
- You are personally involved in operating the property: communicating with guests, coordinating cleaning, managing listings, handling maintenance, and similar tasks. You do NOT rely on a full-service property manager who handles everything with no involvement from you.
- You have active income -- wages, S-corp distributions, business profit, or other non-passive income -- against which you want additional deductions.
- You can maintain a written log of the hours you spend on the STR throughout the year.

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not pursue this strategy without first discussing with your advisor if any of the following apply:

- **Your average guest stay exceeds 7 days.** The entire strategy rests on the 7-day average threshold. If your average is 7.1 days, the property reverts to a standard rental activity under the passive activity rules. It does not qualify for this exception and you cannot use the losses against active income without qualifying as a real estate professional separately.
- **You use a full-service property manager who handles everything.** Material participation (explained below) requires YOUR personal time in the activity. If a manager handles all guest contact, all cleaning coordination, all maintenance scheduling, and all listing management with no meaningful involvement from you, you will not meet any material participation test. The losses will be passive.
- **You primarily use the property personally.** If you use the property for personal vacations for more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of the total rental days during the year, the property is treated as a "vacation home" under IRC Section 280A, and your deductible expenses are capped at the rental-day ratio. This significantly limits the strategy's benefit. Track personal use days carefully and keep them below that threshold.
- **You have no active income to offset.** The point of converting the STR to a non-passive trade or business is to use its losses against active income. If you have little or no active income, the benefit is limited (though suspended losses carry forward to future years when you do have active income, or are fully released when you sell the property).
- **You are planning to sell the property very soon.** Accelerating depreciation today (particularly through cost segregation) triggers depreciation recapture when you sell. If a sale is within 1-2 years, the recapture tax on personal property (Section 1245 recapture, taxed at ordinary income rates) may offset much of the benefit. Model the numbers with your advisor first.
- **You own the property inside an S-corp or C-corp without confirming the passive activity analysis.** The passive/non-passive classification is made at the individual taxpayer level. Property held in an entity adds complexity; confirm with your advisor before acting.

## Key Terms

**Short-term rental (STR):** A residential property that you rent to guests for short stays rather than under long-term leases. Platforms like Airbnb, VRBO, and Vacasa are common channels. The tax treatment of an STR depends heavily on the average length of each guest stay, which determines whether the property is classified as a rental activity or a trade or business under the passive activity rules.

**Average period of customer use:** The number that determines whether the 7-day exception applies. You calculate it by dividing the total number of rental days during the year by the total number of separate rental bookings during the year. For example: if your property is rented 200 days across 40 separate guest stays, the average period of customer use is 200 / 40 = 5 days. That is 7 days or fewer, so the exception applies. A single long booking -- say, 30 consecutive nights for one guest -- counts as 1 booking and 30 rental days, which can push the average up significantly if not balanced by many shorter stays.

**The 7-day exception (STR loophole):** Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(A) provides that an activity is NOT a rental activity under IRC Section 469 (the passive activity rules) when the average period of customer use is 7 days or fewer. When the activity is not a rental activity, it is treated as a trade or business for passive activity purposes. A trade or business produces non-passive income and losses if you materially participate in it. This is how STR losses become usable against wages and other active income.

**The 30-day exception with significant personal services:** A second exception under Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-1T(e)(3)(ii)(B) applies when the average period of customer use is 30 days or fewer AND you provide significant personal services to guests in connection with making the property available (meaning services beyond cleaning, maintenance, and basic amenities). This exception is harder to meet and requires providing hotel-like services (concierge, daily housekeeping during stays, meal service, or similar). Most STR owners rely on the 7-day exception, not this one. Confirm with your advisor which exception applies to your situation.

**Passive activity:** Under IRC Section 469, a passive activity is a trade or business or rental activity in which you do not materially participate. Losses from a passive activity can only offset income from other passive activities, not wages or active business income. Most traditional long-term rental real estate is passive by default under IRC Section 469(c)(2).

**Material participation:** Active, regular, and continuous involvement in running an activity during the tax year. The IRS defines material participation through seven tests in Treasury Regulation Section 1.469-5T(a). You meet material participation if you satisfy ANY ONE of the seven tests. The three tests most commonly used by STR owners are:

- **Test 1:** You spent more than 500 hours in the STR activity during the year.
- **Test 2:** Your participation was substantially all the participation of any individual (no contractor, cleaner, or co-owner participated more than a minor amount).
- **Test 3:** You spent more than 100 hours in the STR activity during the year AND you spent at least as many hours as any other single person involved in the activity (including paid cleaners, property managers, or maintenance workers).

Test 3 is the most commonly used by active STR owner-operators who have at least one cleaning or maintenance contractor. You must work more hours than your most active contractor, and you must work more than 100 hours total.

**Contemporaneous time log:** A written record created at or near the time of each task, listing the date, time spent, and a specific description of the work. "Contemporaneous" means you are not reconstructing it from memory at the end of the year. IRS auditors and Tax Court judges treat reconstructed logs with deep skepticism and regularly reject them. A contemporaneous log kept in a spreadsheet, calendar app, or time-tracking tool is your primary defense in an audit.

**Passive activity loss (PAL):** A loss from a passive activity. PALs can only offset passive income. If you have more passive losses than passive income in a year, the excess is "suspended" (carried forward to future years) rather than deducted. A non-passive STR loss (from meeting the 7-day exception plus material participation) is not a PAL and is not subject to this restriction.

**Suspended passive losses:** Passive losses that exceeded your passive income in prior years and were not deductible. They carry forward indefinitely. They are released and become fully deductible when you either generate enough passive income to absorb them, or when you dispose of your entire interest in the activity in a fully taxable sale.

**Depreciation:** The tax deduction that lets you recover the cost of a business asset (including a rental property's building and certain components) over time. Under MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System), a residential rental building is depreciated over 27.5 years. Cost segregation studies can reclassify components into shorter-lived categories (5, 7, or 15 years) to accelerate those deductions.

**Bonus depreciation:** A provision under IRC Section 168(k) that allows an immediate 100% deduction for the full cost of qualifying property in the year it is placed in service. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025. Components reclassified through a cost segregation study into 5-year, 7-year, or 15-year categories qualify for 100% bonus depreciation. This means an STR owner who does a cost segregation study on a newly purchased property acquired after January 19, 2025 can potentially deduct 100% of the reclassified components' cost in year one.

**Cost segregation study:** An engineering-based analysis that breaks a building into its individual components and reclassifies those that qualify (fixtures, flooring, cabinetry, specialty electrical, land improvements like driveways and landscaping) from 27.5-year or 39-year depreciation into 5-year, 7-year, or 15-year categories. This produces larger depreciation deductions in early years. For an STR that qualifies as a non-passive trade or business, these large depreciation losses are usable against active income.

**Section 280A (vacation home rules):** The IRC section that limits deductions for properties used as both a personal residence and a rental. If you use the STR for personal purposes more than the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days, the property is treated as a "vacation home" and your rental expenses (including depreciation) are capped at a ratio of rental days to total use days. This rule applies even if you otherwise meet the 7-day exception for the passive activity analysis. The two rules operate independently.

**Schedule E (Form 1040):** The IRS form used to report income and losses from rental real estate, partnerships, and S corporations. Most STR owners who qualify for the 7-day exception and materially participate report on Schedule E. Your net income or loss flows from Schedule E to your Form 1040.

**Schedule C (Form 1040):** The IRS form used to report self-employment business income. A small number of STR owners who provide hotel-like services (daily maid service, meal preparation, concierge) may need to report on Schedule C instead of Schedule E. The distinction matters because Schedule C income is subject to self-employment tax (15.3% on net earnings up to the Social Security wage base) while Schedule E rental income is not. Your advisor determines which form applies based on the specific services you provide.

**Form 8582 (Passive Activity Loss Limitations):** The form used to calculate and track suspended passive activity losses. If your STR losses are non-passive (because you meet both the 7-day exception and material participation), Form 8582 is generally not required for that activity. If losses are passive (for example, in a year you do not materially participate), they are tracked on Form 8582.

**MAGI (Modified Adjusted Gross Income):** For purposes of the $25,000 passive loss allowance (a fallback rule described in The Numbers section), MAGI is your adjusted gross income calculated without the rental real estate loss itself. Your tax return calculates this; do not subtract the rental loss when estimating MAGI for threshold purposes.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A 3.8% federal tax under IRC Section 1411 on net investment income (including passive rental income) when your MAGI exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). Non-passive STR income -- from an STR where you materially participate -- is generally exempt from NIIT. Passive STR income is generally subject to it.

**IRC Section 469:** The Internal Revenue Code section governing passive activity losses and credits. This is the statutory foundation for the passive activity rules and the exceptions (including the 7-day exception) discussed in this playbook.

**Real estate professional status (REPS):** A separate federal designation under IRC Section 469(c)(7) that, if you qualify, allows ALL of your rental activities (including long-term rentals) to be treated as non-passive. REPS is NOT required for the STR strategy -- the 7-day exception is a separate and independent path. However, STR owners who also own long-term rental properties sometimes pursue both strategies. REPS requires more than 750 hours per year in real property trades or businesses AND more than 50% of all your personal services (across all jobs and businesses) must be in those real property trades or businesses. The STR loophole and REPS are two distinct strategies; the STR loophole is available to anyone who meets the 7-day average and material participation tests, regardless of REPS status.

**IRC Section 465 (at-risk rules):** A separate limit on deductible losses: you cannot deduct more than the amount you have "at risk" in the activity (generally, your cash invested plus the adjusted basis of property you contributed, plus certain qualified nonrecourse financing for real estate). Even if your STR loss is non-passive, the at-risk rules can further limit the deductible amount. This is especially relevant for highly leveraged properties. Your advisor will calculate your at-risk amount as part of return preparation.

**Section 1245 recapture:** When you sell property on which you claimed accelerated depreciation (including cost segregation reclassifications of personal property), the IRS "recaptures" the benefit by taxing the portion of the gain attributable to that depreciation at ordinary income rates. For the 5-year and 7-year components reclassified through cost segregation, all depreciation previously taken is recaptured at ordinary income rates (up to 37% federal in 2026) when you sell.

**Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain:** The portion of your gain on the sale of a rental property attributable to prior depreciation taken on the building structure (Section 1250 real property). This is taxed at a maximum federal rate of 25%, not at ordinary income rates and not at the lower long-term capital gains rate. It is reported on Schedule D (Form 1040).

## Required Client Inputs

Gather all of the following before your advisor meeting. Bring original documents or reliable copies.

**Property information:**
1. Full property address, type (single-family home, condo, cabin, multi-unit, etc.), and whether you own it personally or through an entity
2. Date the property was purchased (closing date) and date it was first made available for rental (placed in service for tax purposes)

4. A list of all capital improvements made since purchase, with approximate costs and years completed
5. Whether a prior cost segregation study has ever been done on the property

**Booking activity information (current year to date):**
6. Total number of separate guest bookings for the year to date
7. Total number of rental nights for the year to date
8. The rental platform(s) you use (Airbnb, VRBO, direct booking, etc.)
9. A booking history export from your platform showing each booking's check-in and check-out dates and number of nights -- this is what you use to calculate the average period of customer use

**Your time and participation:**
10. Your year-to-date total hours worked on the STR, with a breakdown by task type (guest communications, cleaning coordination, property walkthroughs, maintenance, listing management, bookkeeping, etc.)
11. The name of any cleaner, property manager, or maintenance contractor you use and an estimate of how many hours they have worked on the property this year -- you must document that you work at least as many hours as your most active contractor if you are relying on material participation Test 3
12. Whether you have any full-time W-2 job or other business (this affects whether real estate professional status may also be available)

**Personal use information:**
13. The number of days you or family members have used the property for personal purposes this year (not rental days, not maintenance days -- personal vacation or recreation days only)

**Tax and entity information:**
14. Your estimated taxable income for the year and filing status (single, married filing jointly, etc.)
15. Estimated MAGI for the year
16. The entity that holds the property: personal name, single-member LLC (disregarded for tax), multi-member LLC (taxed as partnership), S-corp, or other
17. Whether you have prior-year passive activity losses from this property or other rental properties (found on Form 8582 from your most recent return)
18. Whether you have received any IRS notices or are under examination for prior tax years

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect before your advisor meeting:**
- Closing settlement statement (HUD-1 or Closing Disclosure) from the original purchase
- All invoices and contracts for capital improvements made since purchase
- Booking history export from Airbnb, VRBO, or other platform (showing check-in, check-out, and nights per booking for the full year)
- Prior depreciation schedule (from Form 4562 of your most recent tax return) if the property was purchased in a prior year
- Prior Form 8582 if you have carried-forward passive losses on this property

**Maintain all year, every year, in real time:**

A. **Contemporaneous time log for the STR.** Every hour you spend on the STR must be logged with the date, number of minutes or hours, and a specific task description (not "STR work" -- instead: "responded to 4 guest inquiries about check-in, 30 minutes"; "coordinated cleaner turnover after checkout, sent cleaning checklist, followed up to confirm completion, 45 minutes"; "inspected property and restocked supplies after guest departure, 1.5 hours"). Log entries in real time -- weekly at minimum, daily is better.

B. **Contractor time records.** Ask your cleaner, maintenance worker, or property manager to keep (or give you) records of their hours on your property. If you cannot get their actual records, track what you observe: dates they visited, approximate time on site. You need to document that YOUR hours equal or exceed those of your most active contractor.

C. **Booking records.** Export and save your full-year booking history from your rental platform. This is your evidence for the average period of customer use calculation.

D. **Personal use log.** Record every day you or a family member uses the property for personal purposes. A separate calendar or log entry for each personal-use day is sufficient. Do not count maintenance inspection days (days spent repairing, restocking, or preparing the property -- those are not personal use days under IRS rules).

**Retention guidance:**
- Keep all property records (purchase documents, cost basis, capital improvement invoices, depreciation schedules) for as long as you own the property plus at least 6 years after the year of sale.
- Keep time logs and booking records for at least 6 years after the tax year they relate to. The IRS audits STR material participation claims frequently, and reconstructed logs are generally rejected. Contemporaneous records are essential.
- Keep Form 8582 carryforward records until all suspended losses are fully used and for 6 years thereafter.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Confirm eligibility before the year ends (or before you purchase)

**Step 1: Calculate your current average period of customer use.**

Take your booking history export from Airbnb, VRBO, or other platform. Count two things: (a) the total number of separate guest bookings and (b) the total number of rental nights across all bookings. Divide the total rental nights by the total number of bookings. The result must be 7.0 or fewer for the exception to apply.

Example: 45 bookings, 270 total rental nights = 6.0 days average. This qualifies.
Counter-example: 20 bookings, 160 total rental nights = 8.0 days average. This does NOT qualify.

Do this calculation at the beginning of the year, again at mid-year, and again at year-end. One long booking (say, 21 nights) accepted late in the year can tip the average above 7 days if your short-stay volume is not high enough to counterbalance it.

If you are evaluating a property you have not yet purchased, your advisor can model the booking strategy (minimum nights per booking, mix of booking lengths) needed to stay at or below the 7-day average.

**Step 2: Set or maintain a booking policy that protects the 7-day average.**

Work backward from your average. If you have 30 short bookings at an average of 3 days each and then accept one 30-day booking, you now have 31 bookings and (30 x 3) + 30 = 120 rental nights = 3.87 days average. Still fine. But if you had only 5 short bookings at 4 days each (20 nights), and one 30-night booking, you have 6 bookings and 50 nights = 8.33 days average. Not fine.

The practical rule: the more bookings you have, the more you can absorb longer stays without pushing the average above 7. Alert your advisor before accepting any booking longer than 14 nights, especially early in the year when your booking count is still low.

**Step 3: Start (or continue) your contemporaneous time log from January 1.**

Open a spreadsheet, a time-tracking app, or a dedicated calendar. Begin logging every task you personally perform for the STR. The format for each entry: Date | Hours | Specific task description. Do not wait until mid-year or year-end to start; hours from the beginning of the year count toward your material participation test and must be documented. If you are reading this mid-year and have not been logging, reconstruct what you can from email threads, booking platform messages, bank statements, and phone records -- then begin a contemporaneous log going forward. Tell your advisor how much is reconstructed.

**Step 4: Identify which material participation test you expect to meet.**

Based on your year-to-date activity:

- If you have worked or expect to work more than 500 hours total on the STR this year: you will meet Test 1 (more than 500 hours). This is the strongest test and the hardest to dispute in an audit.
- If you have worked or expect to work more than 100 hours total, and you work more hours than your cleaner or any other individual involved: you will meet Test 3 (more than 100 hours, at least as many as any other individual). This is the most common test for active STR owners.
- If no single contractor or manager works significant hours on the property and you handle essentially everything: you may meet Test 2 (your participation is substantially all the participation of any individual). This is fact-specific; confirm with your advisor.

If you are not going to meet any test this year, the STR losses will be passive for that year. Discuss with your advisor whether you can increase your participation before December 31.

### Phase 2: Gather and organize your records for the tax year

**Step 5: At year-end, pull your final booking summary and run the final average period of customer use calculation.**

Export your final full-year booking history from your rental platform. Calculate: total rental nights / total number of separate bookings. Confirm the result is 7.0 or fewer. Save this export as a PDF or spreadsheet. Label it with the tax year.

**Step 6: Compile your final time log and total your hours.**

Add up all logged hours for the year. Separate them by category: guest communication, cleaning coordination, listing management, property inspections, maintenance, supply runs, bookkeeping, etc. Confirm you have met the material participation test you identified in Step 4. If you relied on Test 3, confirm your total hours equal or exceed the hours your most active contractor spent on the property.

**Step 7: Count your personal use days for the year.**

Total every day you or a family member used the property for personal recreation or vacation. Compare this to the greater of (a) 14 days or (b) 10% of your total rental days. If your personal use is at or below this threshold, the Section 280A vacation home limitation does NOT apply, and you can deduct your full rental expenses including depreciation.

If your personal use EXCEEDS this threshold, you must allocate expenses between rental and personal use. Only the rental-day ratio (rental days / total use days) of each expense is deductible. Depreciation for the personal-use days is not deductible. Alert your advisor as soon as possible if personal use exceeded the threshold.

**Step 8: Confirm whether the property was used for fewer than 15 days total (if applicable).**

This is a different rule from the 7-day average. Under IRC Section 280A(g), if you rented the property for fewer than 15 days during the entire year, you do NOT report the rental income and you do NOT deduct rental expenses. This is a special exclusion (similar to the Augusta Rule) that benefits property owners who rented only a handful of times. If you rented the property for 15 or more days, this exclusion does not apply and you use the normal income/expense reporting rules.

Most STR owners who pursue the strategy described in this playbook rent for far more than 15 days per year, so this step is mostly a confirmation check. If you did rent for fewer than 15 days, tell your advisor before reporting anything -- the rules are different.

### Phase 3: Evaluate whether a cost segregation study makes sense

**Step 9: With your advisor, decide whether a cost segregation study is worth the investment for this property.**

A cost segregation study (described in Key Terms above) can dramatically accelerate the depreciation deductions on your STR property, particularly for a property acquired after January 19, 2025 where 100% bonus depreciation applies to reclassified components. The study fee typically ranges from a few thousand dollars to $15,000 or more depending on the property's cost and complexity [confirm current study fee ranges with your advisor -- cost varies by firm and property size; no published IRS figure exists].

The study makes the most financial sense when:
- The property's depreciable basis (purchase price minus land value, plus capital improvements) is $300,000 or more [industry convention; no IRS dollar threshold exists -- confirm with your advisor].
- You can use the resulting deductions in the current year (which you can, because you qualify for the 7-day exception and materially participate).
- You plan to hold the property for several years (so the early deductions have time to offset real income before recapture on sale).

If the study makes sense, your advisor will recommend a qualified cost segregation firm. The detailed implementation steps are covered in the Cost Segregation playbook. For this playbook, the key point is: if you are an active STR owner who meets the 7-day exception, any cost segregation losses on that property are NON-PASSIVE and deductible against all your income in the year the study is filed.

**Step 10: For a property purchased in a prior year, evaluate a lookback (catch-up) cost segregation study.**

If you purchased the STR in a prior year and have been depreciating the entire building as a single 27.5-year residential rental, you may be entitled to a large catch-up deduction through a lookback study. The catch-up is claimed as a Section 481(a) adjustment (a lump-sum catch-up deduction equal to all the extra depreciation you would have taken in prior years if the study had been done at purchase) by filing Form 3115 (Application for Change in Accounting Method) with your current-year tax return. Your advisor handles the Form 3115 preparation and filing. For the catch-up deduction to be non-passive, you must also materially participate in the STR in the year you file Form 3115.

### Phase 4: File your return correctly

**Step 11: Confirm the correct form and classification with your advisor before filing.**

For most active STR owners:
- Report rental income and expenses on Schedule E (Form 1040), Part I (Income or Loss From Rental Real Estate and Royalties).
- Report depreciation on Form 4562 (Depreciation and Amortization), which flows to Schedule E.
- Do NOT include the STR on Form 8582 (Passive Activity Loss Limitations) if it is non-passive.
- The net loss from Schedule E flows directly to Form 1040 and reduces your total taxable income.

If your advisor determines you provide significant services that rise to the level of hotel-like services, you may need to report on Schedule C instead. This determination requires review of the specific services you provide; confirm before filing.

**Step 12: Confirm your advisor reviews the return for passive activity classification.**

The passive/non-passive determination must be documented and applied correctly on the return. Your advisor will confirm: (a) the average period of customer use calculation and the supporting booking export; (b) which material participation test you meet and the supporting time log; (c) that no Section 280A vacation home limit applies (or, if it does, that expenses are allocated correctly); and (d) that Form 4562 reflects the correct depreciation, including any bonus depreciation on eligible components.

### Phase 5: Ongoing year-round management

**Step 13: Start fresh records on January 1 of each new tax year.**

Material participation and the average period of customer use are determined year by year. Your 2025 time log does not carry over to 2026. Begin a new time log on January 1. Run the booking average calculation at the beginning of the year to set your booking policy, again at mid-year, and at year-end.

**Step 14: Review the average period of customer use before accepting each long booking.**

Recalculate the running average every time you accept a booking that is longer than 7 nights. Set a personal rule: before accepting any booking longer than 10 or 14 nights, calculate whether it will push your year-to-date average above 7 days. If it would, discuss the situation with your advisor. Losing the 7-day exception mid-year means the STR losses for that year are passive.

**Step 15: Model exit tax consequences with your advisor before listing the property for sale.**

If you have taken significant depreciation (especially accelerated depreciation from a cost segregation study), a sale triggers Section 1245 recapture on reclassified personal property (taxed at ordinary income rates, up to 37% federal in 2026) and unrecaptured Section 1250 gain on the building structure (taxed at a maximum 25% federal rate). Run a pre-sale tax model with your advisor that shows the net after-tax proceeds. A 1031 exchange (which defers gain by rolling into a replacement property) or installment sale structure may be worth considering. These are complex strategies that require advisor review before signing a purchase agreement.

## Timing And Deadlines

- **Year-round:** Maintain your time log continuously. Calculate the average period of customer use before accepting each new booking. Track personal use days as they occur.
- **Mid-year (suggested: July):** Run a mid-year check. Total your hours, confirm which material participation test you will meet, recalculate the average period of customer use, and update your year-end projection with your advisor. If you are behind on hours or the average is drifting toward 7 days, adjust now -- not in December.
- **December 31:** The last day of the tax year is the deadline for meeting all tests. Material participation and the average period of customer use are measured as of December 31. You cannot retroactively increase hours or adjust bookings after the year ends.
- **January 15 (estimated taxes):** If your STR produces significant net income (or if a cost segregation deduction changes your income picture dramatically), adjust your fourth-quarter estimated tax payment accordingly. Individuals generally must pay the fourth-quarter estimated tax by January 15 of the following year (January 15, 2027 for the 2026 tax year). File using Form 1040-ES.
- **April 15 (return due date for individuals):** Your Form 1040 with Schedule E and Form 4562 is due April 15, 2027 for the 2026 tax year (October 15, 2027 with a timely filed Form 4868 extension). If a lookback cost segregation study is being filed for the first time, Form 3115 must be attached to the timely filed return (including extension).
- **Form 4868 (extension):** Filing Form 4868 by April 15 gives you an automatic 6-month extension to October 15 to file your return. An extension to file is NOT an extension to pay. If you owe tax, you must pay the estimated amount owed by April 15 to avoid interest and a late-payment penalty.
- **No mid-year election exists for material participation:** You cannot elect into material participation status for a year if you did not actually spend the required hours. Meet the test or you do not.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity structure and passive activity analysis:**
The non-passive classification of an STR (based on the 7-day exception and material participation) is made at the individual taxpayer level, not at the entity level. If you hold the STR in a single-member LLC that is taxed as a disregarded entity, the tax analysis is the same as if you owned it personally. If you hold it in a multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership, the STR income and loss flow through to you on a Schedule K-1, and the passive/non-passive determination is made on your individual Form 1040. If you hold it in an S-corp, the same K-1 passthrough applies. Holding property in an LLC does not itself affect the passive activity classification. Confirm with your advisor.

**Self-employment tax:**
Rental income reported on Schedule E is NOT subject to self-employment tax (15.3% on net earnings up to $176,100, 2.9% Medicare tax above that amount -- confirm 2026 Social Security wage base with your advisor). However, if your STR activity is treated as a Schedule C business because you provide substantial hotel-like services, the net income becomes subject to self-employment tax. This is a significant cost. The determination of Schedule E vs. Schedule C is fact-specific and must be made by your advisor based on the services you provide.

**State income tax -- passive activity conformity:**
Most states follow the federal passive activity rules and will recognize your STR loss as non-passive if the federal tests are met. However, state conformity varies. Some states have their own passive activity regimes or limitations on rental losses that differ from federal rules. Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor before projecting net savings.

**State income tax -- bonus depreciation conformity:**
Many states do not conform to federal bonus depreciation. States including California, New York, and New Jersey have historically decoupled from federal bonus depreciation, requiring you to add back the federal bonus depreciation deduction on your state return and instead depreciate at the state's own schedule [confirm current state conformity with your advisor -- state laws change frequently]. This means a large federal bonus depreciation deduction on a cost segregation study can create an unexpected state tax bill in the year of the study. Always model federal and state taxes together.

**State and local short-term rental taxes (occupancy taxes, transient occupancy taxes, lodging taxes):**
Most states and many counties and cities impose occupancy taxes, transient occupancy taxes (TOT), hotel taxes, or similar charges on short-term rental income. These are separate from income tax and are not covered by the federal passive activity analysis. Your income tax strategy does not reduce these taxes. Confirm with your advisor and local tax authority what STR-specific taxes apply in the jurisdiction where your property is located and whether the rental platform collects and remits these on your behalf.

**Local STR licensing and zoning:**
Many municipalities require STR operators to obtain a permit or license before operating legally. Some jurisdictions cap the number of permitted STRs or restrict which properties qualify. Non-compliance can result in fines that are not tax deductible. Confirm local licensing requirements with a local attorney or your municipality before beginning operations.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) interaction:**
Non-passive STR income (from materially participating in the activity) is generally NOT subject to the 3.8% NIIT. Passive STR income (from years you did not meet material participation) IS subject to NIIT if your MAGI exceeds the thresholds. This is one more reason material participation documentation matters: in a year you fail the test, your net STR income becomes subject to both ordinary income tax and NIIT.

**At-risk rules interaction:**
The at-risk rules (IRC Section 465) may limit your STR loss even if it is non-passive. If you financed the property with significant non-recourse debt that does not qualify as qualified nonrecourse financing for real property, your deductible loss is capped at your at-risk amount. Your advisor will calculate this as part of return preparation.

**Current-year note on bonus depreciation:**
The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (P.L. 119-21, July 4, 2025) permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation under IRC Section 168(k) for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025. For property acquired before that date, the phase-down rates that applied under prior law govern the bonus depreciation calculation for the placed-in-service year. If you are doing a lookback cost segregation study on an STR purchased before January 19, 2025, your advisor must apply the correct bonus depreciation percentage for each component based on its original placed-in-service date, not the 100% rate.

**Excess business loss limitation (IRC Section 461(l)):**
For tax years 2026 and beyond, the excess business loss limitation may apply to noncorporate taxpayers. This rule caps the amount of business losses (including non-passive STR losses) that can offset non-business income. The 2026 excess business loss threshold is [confirm with your advisor -- the inflation-adjusted 2026 threshold amount was not separately confirmed against a canonical source at time of publication; governed by IRC Section 461(l) as adjusted by Rev. Proc. 2025-32]. Losses disallowed by the excess business loss rule are converted to net operating loss (NOL) carryforwards. If you anticipate very large STR losses, discuss the Section 461(l) limit with your advisor before finalizing your tax plan.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Not calculating the average period of customer use until after the year ends.**
The 7-day average is the foundation of the entire strategy. If you discover in January that your year-end average was 7.4 days, you cannot fix it retroactively. Monitor the average throughout the year, especially when considering longer bookings.

**2. Failing to document contractor hours.**
Material participation Test 3 requires you to work at least as many hours as your most active contractor. If your cleaner visits the property 15 times for 2 hours each (30 hours total) and you only logged 25 hours of your own time, you fail Test 3. Track contractor hours at the same time you track your own.

**3. Keeping a reconstructed (end-of-year) time log instead of a contemporaneous one.**
IRS auditors and Tax Court judges repeatedly reject time logs created from memory at year-end. If you log "12 hours per week, every week, year-round" without specific task descriptions, the entry will be disregarded. Specific, date-stamped, task-specific entries kept in real time are what survives an audit.

**4. Exceeding the personal use threshold without realizing it.**
A few extra vacation days at the property can push you over the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days and trigger the Section 280A vacation home limitation, which caps all your rental deductions. Count every personal use day as it happens; do not rely on memory at year-end.

**5. Assuming the STR exception and REPS are interchangeable.**
They are separate strategies with separate tests. The 7-day exception is NOT real estate professional status. Telling your advisor "I run my Airbnb" does not establish REPS. Conversely, if you do qualify for REPS, you do not need the STR exception -- but the tests and documentation requirements are completely different. Do not confuse them.

**6. Accepting a long-term booking that tips the average above 7 days.**
One 30-night or 60-night booking accepted during a slow season can push your annual average above 7 days if your short-stay booking volume is not sufficient. Once the average exceeds 7 days for the year, the exception is lost for the ENTIRE year -- not just for that booking's portion of the year. There is no partial-year fix.

**7. Failing to account for state tax on bonus depreciation before committing to a cost segregation study.**
A federal cost segregation deduction of $200,000 in a non-conforming state might generate a $15,000 to $20,000 state tax bill you were not expecting. The federal refund is real; the state bill is also real. Model both before signing the cost segregation engagement letter.

**8. Not matching Schedule E income/loss to the passive activity classification.**
If your STR is non-passive, it should NOT appear on Form 8582. Including a non-passive loss on Form 8582 limits it incorrectly. Conversely, if your STR is passive (because you failed material participation), it MUST go through Form 8582. The correct form routing matters; confirm with your advisor.

**9. Ignoring recapture when planning a sale.**
If you sell an STR that has been through a cost segregation study, the recapture tax on reclassified personal property (taxed at ordinary income rates) can be substantial. A property where you deducted $150,000 of personal property over 5 years and then sell will have up to $150,000 of ordinary income recapture recognized in the sale year. Build this into your sale planning, not as an afterthought.

**10. Treating the STR as fully non-passive in a year when you did not log hours.**
Failing to maintain a time log means you cannot prove material participation. Without proof, the IRS will treat the STR as a passive activity for that year, potentially converting a non-passive deduction into a suspended passive loss -- and, if you already deducted it against active income, resulting in a deficiency, penalties, and interest. Log every year.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and confirm the following with your advisor before acting:

1. **Before your first booking (or before purchasing an STR property):** Confirm the booking strategy that will maintain a 7-day or below average period of customer use, confirm your ability to materially participate, and model the first-year tax impact of the STR losses (with and without a cost segregation study) against your projected income.

2. **Before accepting any booking longer than 14 nights:** Recalculate the year-to-date average period of customer use including the proposed long booking. If the average would rise above 7 days, do not accept the booking until you have discussed the consequences with your advisor.

3. **Before ordering a cost segregation study:** Confirm that (a) the STR qualifies for the 7-day exception and you are materially participating, (b) the study fee is justified by the projected tax savings, (c) your state's treatment of bonus depreciation is understood, and (d) the at-risk amount and excess business loss limits have been reviewed.

4. **Before filing your return claiming non-passive STR losses:** Confirm with your advisor that the average period of customer use is documented and at or below 7 days, that material participation is documented with a contemporaneous log, that personal use days are within the Section 280A limits, and that Schedule E (not Form 8582) is the correct home for the loss.

5. **Before selling the property:** Run a full recapture analysis, including Section 1245 recapture on cost-segregated personal property and unrecaptured Section 1250 gain on the building. Evaluate whether a 1031 exchange, installment sale, or other structure is preferable to an outright sale. Do not sign a purchase agreement without this analysis.

6. **If you receive an IRS notice or are under examination:** Do not initiate a cost segregation study or file Form 3115 while under examination without your advisor's review of the timing implications. Do not make representations about non-passive STR status to the IRS before reviewing the facts with your advisor.

7. **If your personal use is approaching the 14-day or 10% threshold:** Alert your advisor before crossing it. Once you exceed the threshold for the year, the Section 280A vacation home limitation applies to the ENTIRE year's expenses -- you cannot undo it.

8. **If your net STR losses are very large:** Discuss the IRC Section 461(l) excess business loss limitation with your advisor before projecting the tax benefit. Large non-passive losses may be partially limited for noncorporate taxpayers.

## Before You Act

Do not file your return, claim non-passive STR losses, order a cost segregation study, change the passive/non-passive classification of your rental, or accept long bookings that affect your average without first confirming the specific facts with your advisor. The tax savings available through the short-term rental strategy are real and substantial -- but every element (the 7-day average period of customer use, material participation, the personal use limit, the entity structure, the state tax impact) must be confirmed against your actual facts for your actual tax year. A single element out of place -- an average period of customer use of 7.1 days, or 95 hours of participation instead of 105, or personal use that exceeds the Section 280A threshold -- can convert a non-passive deduction into a suspended passive loss or trigger penalties for an improper position. Confirm the numbers with your advisor before acting.
