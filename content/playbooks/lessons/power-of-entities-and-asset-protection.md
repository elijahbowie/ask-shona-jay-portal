# Power of Entities and Asset Protection

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Business entities -- such as limited liability companies (LLCs), S corporations (S-corps), and C corporations (C-corps) -- are legal structures that separate your personal finances from your business finances. Choosing the right entity and maintaining it properly accomplishes two goals: (1) it shields your personal assets (your home, savings, and investments) from business lawsuits and creditors, and (2) it changes how your business income is taxed, which can reduce the amount of self-employment and income tax you owe. This playbook walks you through how each major entity type works, how to choose among them, and how to implement your choice correctly so that both the tax savings and the legal protection hold up.

---

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is most useful for:

- **Sole proprietors** who are currently operating without any formal legal structure and are therefore personally exposed to every business liability.
- **Single-member LLC owners** who want to understand whether their current setup is providing maximum tax efficiency.
- **Business owners earning net profit of roughly $40,000 or more per year** from self-employment, because the tax savings from an S-corp election become meaningful at this level of income.
- **Business owners with significant personal assets** (real estate, investment accounts, retirement funds) who want legal separation between those assets and business risk.
- **Business owners with multiple income streams or business activities**, who may benefit from placing different activities in separate entities.
- **Real estate investors** who want to use LLCs to separate properties and limit exposure from any single property.

---

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **When your net business profit is below $40,000 annually.** The cost of running payroll and filing a separate corporate return for an S-corp election typically exceeds the FICA tax savings at lower profit levels. A sole proprietorship or single-member LLC taxed as a disregarded entity may be more cost-effective.
- **When you have no meaningful personal assets to protect.** If you have very limited savings and own no real estate, the asset-protection benefit is smaller. Legal protection still matters, but the urgency is lower.
- **When your business is a Specified Service Trade or Business (SSTB) near or above the QBI phase-out.** See the Key Terms and The Numbers sections; the Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction begins to phase out at certain income levels for certain service businesses.
- **When you cannot maintain corporate formalities.** An S-corp or C-corp that is not operated as a separate business -- meaning no separate bank accounts, no documentation of major decisions, and personal expenses mixed with business expenses -- risks having a court "pierce the corporate veil" (hold you personally liable as if the entity did not exist). If you are not willing to maintain the separation, the protection is illusory.
- **When your state imposes high annual LLC or corporate fees that offset the benefit.** Some states charge significant minimum taxes or franchise fees regardless of income. Confirm state costs with your advisor.
- **When you are a non-resident alien or a foreign corporation** seeking to own an S-corp. Foreign shareholders are not permitted in an S-corp.

## Key Terms

**Sole proprietorship:** A business owned and operated by one person with no formal legal structure. All income is reported on Schedule C of the owner's Form 1040. All net profit is subject to self-employment tax and income tax. There is no legal separation between the owner and the business: a business creditor can reach the owner's personal assets.

**LLC (Limited Liability Company):** A state-law business entity that provides limited liability protection to its owners (called "members"). An LLC with one member is called a single-member LLC; with two or more members, a multi-member LLC. The LLC itself is not a federal tax category -- its default federal tax treatment depends on how many owners it has (see "Default tax treatment" below). State law, not federal law, governs LLC formation and protection rules.

**Default tax treatment (LLC):** How the IRS treats an LLC that has not made a tax election. A single-member LLC is treated as a "disregarded entity" -- the IRS ignores it for tax purposes and taxes the owner like a sole proprietor (Schedule C, self-employment tax on all net profit). A multi-member LLC is treated as a partnership by default (files Form 1065, issues K-1s to each member, self-employment tax on active members' shares).

**S corporation (S-corp):** A domestic corporation or LLC that has elected to be taxed under Subchapter S of the Internal Revenue Code (IRC). S-corps are "pass-through" entities -- the business itself does not pay federal income tax; instead, income, losses, deductions, and credits flow through to shareholders' personal returns. The key tax advantage over a sole proprietorship: an S-corp shareholder who works in the business pays FICA taxes only on "reasonable compensation" (the required W-2 salary), not on the remaining profit distributions.

**C corporation (C-corp):** A corporation taxed under Subchapter C of the IRC. A C-corp is a separate taxpayer: the corporation pays its own income tax, and shareholders pay income tax again on dividends received (called "double taxation"). For most small business owners, C-corps create a less favorable tax outcome than S-corps or pass-throughs, though there are narrow planning scenarios where a C-corp is advantageous.

**Pass-through entity:** Any business entity (LLC taxed as a partnership, S-corp, or sole proprietorship) where income is not taxed at the entity level but instead "passes through" to the owners' personal returns and is taxed once at the owner's individual rate.

**Self-employment (SE) tax:** The tax that a sole proprietor, single-member LLC disregarded entity, or general partner pays on net earnings from self-employment. For 2026, the SE tax rate is 15.3% on net earnings up to $184,500 (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare), and 2.9% Medicare only on amounts above $184,500, plus an additional 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on net self-employment income above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). SE tax applies to 92.35% of net earnings (a rounding adjustment for the employer-equivalent deduction).

**FICA (Federal Insurance Contributions Act):** The payroll taxes that fund Social Security and Medicare. When you are an S-corp shareholder-employee, you pay FICA as an employee and your S-corp pays the matching employer share, rather than paying SE tax on all income. For 2026, the Social Security portion of FICA is 6.2% each for employee and employer (12.4% total) on wages up to $184,500. The Medicare portion is 1.45% each for employee and employer (2.9% total) with no wage cap.

**Reasonable compensation:** The wage the IRS requires an S-corp shareholder-employee to pay themselves before taking non-wage profit distributions. It must equal what a similarly qualified person would earn doing the same work for a comparable business. The IRS can reclassify distributions as wages -- and charge back payroll taxes, interest, and penalties -- if reasonable compensation is too low. Courts look at: duties and responsibilities, time devoted, training and experience, what comparable businesses pay, and the corporation's dividend history.

**Distribution (S-corp):** A payment from the S-corp to the shareholder beyond the shareholder's W-2 wage. Distributions are not subject to FICA or SE tax; they are treated as a return of the shareholder's investment basis to the extent of basis, and as capital gain when they exceed basis. This is the source of the S-corp payroll tax savings.

**Basis (S-corp):** A shareholder's tax investment in the corporation. Basis starts at the amount you paid for your shares, increases with income and additional contributions, and decreases with distributions and losses. You can only deduct S-corp losses up to your basis. Distributions in excess of basis are taxable as capital gains.

**Shareholder-employee:** An owner of an S-corp who also works in the business. If you own more than 2% of an S-corp and provide services to it, you are a shareholder-employee and must be paid reasonable compensation via W-2.

**Form W-2:** IRS Wage and Tax Statement. An S-corp must issue a W-2 to each shareholder-employee showing wages paid and taxes withheld. W-2s are due to employees by January 31 of the following year and must be filed with the Social Security Administration by the same date.

**Form 1120-S:** U.S. Income Tax Return for an S Corporation. The annual tax return an S-corp files with the IRS. For calendar-year S-corps, Form 1120-S is due March 15 of the following year, or the next business day when March 15 falls on a weekend or federal holiday. For the 2026 tax year, Form 1120-S covering 2026 income is due March 16, 2027, because March 15, 2027 is a Sunday.

**Schedule K-1 (Form 1120-S):** The document an S-corp sends each shareholder showing that shareholder's share of income, deductions, and credits for the year. Shareholders use the K-1 to report their S-corp income on their personal returns (Form 1040, Schedule E). K-1s must be furnished to shareholders by the same deadline as the Form 1120-S.

**Form 1065:** U.S. Return of Partnership Income. Filed by multi-member LLCs taxed as partnerships. Issues K-1s to each partner/member. Due March 15 for calendar-year partnerships (same weekend adjustment rules apply).

**Form 2553:** IRS form used by a corporation or eligible LLC to elect S-corp status under IRC Section 1362(a). Must be filed by the later of (a) March 15 of the year you want the election to take effect, or (b) two months and 15 days after the start of that tax year if the entity began operations during the year.

**Form 8832:** IRS form used by an LLC to elect to be classified as a corporation (C-corp) for federal tax purposes. An LLC that wants to be an S-corp must first either be a corporation under state law or file Form 8832 to be treated as a corporation, and then file Form 2553 to elect S-corp status. (Many tax advisors file both forms simultaneously; some states allow a single-step process.) The effective date on Form 8832 cannot be more than 75 days before the date it is filed.

**Charging order:** A state-law remedy available to a creditor of an LLC member. Instead of seizing the member's LLC interest outright, a creditor typically can only obtain a charging order -- a court order directing the LLC to pay any distributions that would otherwise go to the debtor-member directly to the creditor instead. In most states, the creditor does not become a member, cannot force distributions, and cannot vote or manage. This makes an LLC membership interest much less attractive to creditors than direct ownership of a business asset and provides practical asset protection even when a judgment exists against the member personally.

**Piercing the corporate veil:** A court's decision to hold the owner(s) personally liable for entity obligations despite the limited liability structure. Courts pierce the veil when owners fail to maintain the entity as a separate legal person: commingling personal and business funds, using the entity bank account for personal expenses, failing to maintain records, undercapitalizing the entity, or treating the entity as an alter ego. Proper maintenance of corporate formalities (separate accounts, documented decisions, adequate capitalization) prevents veil-piercing.

**QBI deduction (IRC Section 199A):** The Qualified Business Income deduction allows eligible taxpayers to deduct up to 20% of qualified business income from a pass-through trade or business (including S-corps, partnerships, and sole proprietorships) on their personal return. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, enacted July 4, 2025), this deduction is now permanent. For 2026, if your taxable income exceeds certain thresholds, the deduction may be limited or phased out for Specified Service Trades or Businesses (SSTBs). Per Rev. Proc. 2025-32, the SSTB phase-out begins at $201,750 (single filers) or $403,500 (married filing jointly) and is fully phased out at $276,750 (single filers) or $553,500 (married filing jointly). Non-SSTB pass-through businesses are subject to W-2 wage and capital limitations above those same thresholds but are not denied the deduction entirely. Use Form 8995 or 8995-A to compute the exact deduction for your situation.

**SSTB (Specified Service Trade or Business):** A trade or business in one of the following fields: health, law, accounting, actuarial science, performing arts, consulting, athletics, financial services, brokerage services, or any trade or business where the principal asset is the reputation or skill of one or more employees or owners. SSTBs are subject to additional limitations on the QBI deduction above certain income thresholds. Note: engineering and architecture are explicitly excluded from SSTB status.

**EIN (Employer Identification Number):** A nine-digit number the IRS assigns to a business entity. Required for opening a business bank account, running payroll, and filing corporate returns. Apply via Form SS-4 at IRS.gov.

**Form 941:** Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return. Used by employers (including S-corps) to report wages paid, income tax withheld, and Social Security and Medicare taxes. Due by the last day of the month following each calendar quarter: April 30 (Q1), July 31 (Q2), October 31 (Q3), January 31 (Q4 of prior year). If all taxes are deposited on time, the deadline extends 10 calendar days.

**Form 940:** Employer's Annual Federal Unemployment (FUTA) Tax Return. Due January 31 of the following year. Extended 10 days if all FUTA taxes were deposited on time.

**Operating agreement:** A written document that governs how an LLC is run: management structure, voting rights, profit and loss allocation, and what happens if a member wants to leave or dies. While not always required by state law, an operating agreement is essential for asset protection (it helps demonstrate that the LLC is a separate legal entity) and for clarifying the rights of members.

**Corporate minutes / written resolutions:** Documentation of decisions made by the shareholders or directors of a corporation (or the members of an LLC). Even a single-owner entity should document major decisions in writing (compensation decisions, dividend or distribution payments, large purchases, loans, officer appointments). Minutes and resolutions are evidence that the entity is being operated separately from the individual owner.

**Franchise tax / annual report:** A state-imposed fee or tax that LLCs and corporations must pay each year to remain in good standing. Amounts vary dramatically by state. Failure to pay or file can result in administrative dissolution of the entity, eliminating the liability protection it provides.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before taking any action:

1. **Your current entity structure:** Are you a sole proprietor, single-member LLC, multi-member LLC, S-corp, C-corp, or some other structure? If you have an LLC, what state was it formed in?
2. **Your net business profit for the most recent complete year and your projection for the current year.** This drives the entity-choice analysis.
3. **Your total household income and filing status (single, married filing jointly, etc.).** Needed to evaluate income tax brackets and whether QBI phase-outs apply.
4. **Whether you have employees (other than yourself).** Affects payroll complexity and cost-benefit of an S-corp election.
5. **The nature of your business activity.** Is it an SSTB (see Key Terms)? Does it involve real estate? Does it generate passive income or active income?
6. **Your state of residence and state of business operations.** State law governs LLC formation, charging order protection, and franchise tax obligations.
7. **Whether you already have an EIN for the business.** If not, you will need one to open business accounts and run payroll.
8. **Whether the business has multiple owners.** An S-corp is limited to 100 shareholders, all of whom must be U.S. citizens or residents. A multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership has no shareholder limit.
9. **Whether the business has or is likely to have outside investors.** Venture capital funds typically cannot hold S-corp stock; C-corps are standard for VC-backed companies.
10. **The business's assets.** What property does the business own (equipment, inventory, intellectual property, real estate)? This affects which structure best separates liabilities.
11. **Your tolerance for administrative overhead.** An S-corp requires payroll, a separate corporate return, and annual corporate formalities. Confirm you can and will maintain these.

## Required Documents And Records

**Before formation:**
- Your business financials (profit and loss statement for the past one to two years, or projections for a new business)
- Your personal Social Security Number (SSN) and any co-owners' SSNs
- The name you intend to use for the entity (confirm availability with your state's business registry)

**To form the entity:**
- State Articles of Incorporation (for a corporation) or Articles of Organization (for an LLC), filed with the state secretary of state or equivalent office
- Operating agreement (LLC) or bylaws plus shareholder agreement (corporation)
- EIN obtained from the IRS (Form SS-4 or online at IRS.gov; the EIN is issued immediately online)

**For S-corp election:**
- Form 2553, signed by all shareholders and by a corporate officer (retained copy plus IRS acceptance letter)
- Form 8832 if required (retain a copy with IRS acknowledgment)

**To maintain the entity (retain indefinitely or as long as the entity exists):**
- State formation documents (articles) and all subsequent amendments
- Operating agreement or bylaws (all versions)
- All annual reports and franchise tax filings and receipts
- EIN confirmation letter (CP-575 or 147C)
- IRS S-corp election acceptance letter

**Ongoing annual records (retain at minimum 7 years):**
- Corporate minutes or written member resolutions documenting all major decisions
- Shareholder-employee W-2s
- Form 1120-S (or Form 1065) filed each year with all attachments
- Schedule K-1s issued to each shareholder or member
- Payroll records: Forms 941 and 940 filed each quarter/year, payroll registers, payroll tax deposit confirmations
- Business bank account statements (separate from personal)
- Documentation of each distribution paid to shareholders, including the date and amount
- All contracts, leases, or major business agreements entered into in the entity's name

**Retention guidance:** The IRS generally has three years from the filing date to audit a return, but six years if income is understated by more than 25%, and no time limit if fraud is alleged. Retain all entity records for at least seven years after the return is filed. Retain formation documents and the S-corp election letter permanently.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Follow these steps in order. Each step identifies what you need going in and what you produce coming out.

### Step 1: Confirm Your Current Entity Status

**What to do:** Gather your most recent Form 1040, Schedule C, and any state business registration paperwork. Identify how you currently report your business income. If you have a Schedule C and no state-registered business, you are a sole proprietor. If you have a state LLC but file Schedule C, you are a single-member LLC taxed as a disregarded entity.

**Output:** A clear statement of your current entity type and tax classification.

### Step 2: Run the S-Corp Break-Even Analysis with Your Advisor

**What to do:** Work with your advisor to calculate whether an S-corp election saves you net money after accounting for (a) the payroll tax savings on profit above reasonable compensation, and (b) the added cost of payroll service fees and a separate S-corp tax return. Use the Entity Diagram download to visualize how money flows through each entity type. Use the Power of Entities Quick Reference Guide to see side-by-side comparisons of each entity's tax treatment.

**When an S-corp election is generally worth it:** Annual net profit consistently above approximately $40,000 to $50,000 is a commonly used rule of thumb. The exact break-even depends on your state, your payroll costs, and your return-preparation fees. Your advisor should model your specific numbers.

**Output:** A written or documented analysis showing the projected annual FICA savings versus the added administrative costs, by entity type.

### Step 3: Choose Your Entity Structure

**What to do:** Based on your profit level, number of owners, investment plans, and state rules, select one of the following paths:

- **Path A: Remain a sole proprietor.** Appropriate if your net profit is below the S-corp break-even, if you have no meaningful personal assets to protect, or if your business is winding down.
- **Path B: Form an LLC (taxed as disregarded entity or partnership).** Appropriate when you need asset protection but your profit does not yet justify an S-corp election. Simple to maintain; no payroll required for the owner.
- **Path C: Form an LLC or corporation and elect S-corp taxation.** Appropriate when net profit consistently clears the break-even threshold, you can commit to running payroll, and you are a U.S. citizen or resident. This is the most common tax-reduction move for profitable small business owners.
- **Path D: Form a C-corp.** Narrow use cases: VC-backed startups, businesses retaining earnings for reinvestment at the corporate rate, or specific fringe benefit structures. Not a typical first choice for the small business owner. Consult your advisor.

**Output:** A documented decision identifying your chosen entity structure.

### Step 4: Form the Entity with the State

**What to do:**

1. Check name availability through your state secretary of state's website.
2. File Articles of Organization (LLC) or Articles of Incorporation (corporation) with your state and pay the filing fee. Filing fees vary by state.
3. Draft and sign an operating agreement (LLC) or bylaws (corporation). Even if your state does not require it, this document is essential. Include: member/shareholder names and ownership percentages, management structure (member-managed or manager-managed for an LLC, or board of directors for a corp), voting procedures, and what happens if an owner exits.
4. Obtain an EIN from the IRS online at IRS.gov/EIN (free, immediate issuance).
5. Open a dedicated business bank account in the entity's name. Never use the business account for personal expenses.

**Output:** State-issued entity registration confirmation, signed operating agreement or bylaws, EIN confirmation letter, open business bank account.

### Step 5: File Form 2553 (S-Corp Election) if Applicable

**What to do:**

If you chose Path C (S-corp taxation):

1. Complete IRS Form 2553, Election by a Small Business Corporation. All shareholders must sign.
2. File by the deadline:
   - To be effective for the entire calendar year 2026: Form 2553 must have been filed by March 15, 2026. If you missed that deadline for 2026, the election will take effect for calendar year 2027, unless you qualify for late-election relief.
   - To be effective for a new entity formed during 2026: File Form 2553 within 2 months and 15 days of the date the entity was formed or began operations.
3. If you are converting an existing LLC, confirm with your advisor whether Form 8832 is also required. Some states treat this conversion differently.
4. Keep the IRS acceptance letter. You will need it if your S-corp status is ever questioned.

**Output:** Filed Form 2553 with confirmation of receipt from the IRS (the IRS typically sends CP261 Notice acknowledging the S-corp election within 60 days).

### Step 6: Set Up Payroll (S-Corp Owners Only)

**What to do:**

1. Choose a payroll service or work with your advisor to establish payroll. Common options include Gusto, ADP, QuickBooks Payroll, and Paychex. Compare costs.
2. Determine your reasonable compensation with your advisor before the first payroll run. Document the analysis in writing (see The Numbers and Advisor Review Gates sections).
3. Run payroll at least quarterly (monthly or semi-monthly is typical). Issue yourself a W-2 paycheck subject to FICA withholding.
4. Deposit payroll taxes (income tax withheld plus employee and employer FICA) to the IRS via EFTPS (Electronic Federal Tax Payment System) on the IRS's required schedule. Most new employers deposit monthly (by the 15th of the following month) until payroll history triggers a semiweekly schedule.
5. File Form 941 quarterly (due April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31).
6. File Form 940 annually by January 31.

**Output:** Active payroll, quarterly 941 filings on calendar, employer EFTPS enrollment, recurring payroll tax deposits.

### Step 7: File the Annual Entity Tax Return

**What to do:**

- S-corp: File Form 1120-S by March 15 (or next business day if March 15 falls on a weekend). Furnish Schedule K-1 to each shareholder by the same date. If you need more time, file Form 7004 for an automatic 6-month extension (extends the filing deadline to September 15, but does not extend the time to pay taxes).
- Partnership (multi-member LLC): File Form 1065 by March 15. Furnish K-1s to each partner by the same date. Form 7004 extends the filing deadline by 5 months.
- Disregarded entity / sole proprietor: Report income on Schedule C with your Form 1040, due April 15 (extended to October 15 with Form 4868).

**Output:** Annual entity return filed on time, K-1s issued.

### Step 8: Report S-Corp Income on Your Personal Return

**What to do:**

1. After receiving your Schedule K-1 from the S-corp, report your share of S-corp income on Schedule E (Supplemental Income and Loss), Part II of Form 1040.
2. Your W-2 wages from the S-corp are reported on the wages line of Form 1040 and subject to income tax like any wages.
3. If you are eligible for the QBI deduction, compute it on Form 8995 (simplified method) or Form 8995-A (complex situations or high-income filers). The S-corp will provide the QBI information on your K-1.
4. Track your S-corp basis each year. Ask your advisor to confirm your basis calculation before taking distributions that could exceed your basis.

**Output:** Completed Form 1040 with Schedule E, Form 8995 or 8995-A, and correct basis records.

### Step 9: Maintain Corporate Formalities Every Year

**What to do:**

1. File your state's annual report and pay the franchise tax or annual fee by the due date. Due dates and amounts vary by state.
2. Document major business decisions in writing (written resolutions or minutes): changes in ownership, compensation decisions, large purchases or loans, officer changes, distribution payments.
3. Keep all business income and expenses flowing through the business bank account only. Never pay personal bills from the entity account.
4. Review your reasonable compensation level each year with your advisor and update payroll if your role, hours, or profit have changed significantly.
5. Confirm with your advisor any changes to tax law that may affect your entity election (e.g., if QBI deduction rules or S-corp eligibility rules change).

**Output:** Current state filing, documented decisions, clean bank records, updated compensation documentation.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**S-corp eligibility is strict.** An S-corp must be a domestic corporation, have no more than 100 shareholders, have only one class of stock, and have only eligible shareholders (U.S. citizens or resident individuals, certain trusts, and estates). If any of these requirements is violated, the S-corp election terminates automatically and the entity reverts to C-corp status -- potentially triggering significant tax consequences without warning. Monitor eligibility every year.

**Reasonable compensation must be documented before the first distribution.** The IRS can reclassify distributions as wages and assess back payroll taxes, interest, and penalties at any time during the audit window (generally three to six years). Do not take distributions without first establishing and running payroll at a defensible reasonable compensation level. Courts have been consistently unsympathetic to S-corp owners who paid themselves $0 in salary while taking large distributions.

**The S-corp election does not eliminate all payroll tax.** The FICA savings apply only to profit above your reasonable compensation. If reasonable compensation consumes most of your profit, the savings are smaller. Model your specific situation.

**State tax treatment of S-corps varies.** Several states (including California, New York, New Jersey, and others) impose their own tax on S-corp income or have separate franchise taxes that partly offset the federal FICA savings. California, for example, levies a 1.5% franchise tax on net income with a minimum of $800 per year on S corporations (note: the 8.84% rate applies to C corporations, not S corporations; per the CA Franchise Tax Board, S-corps pay the lower 1.5% rate). Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor before making the election.

**An LLC provides asset protection from business liabilities -- not from your own malpractice or personal obligations.** If you are a professional (doctor, attorney, accountant) and commit malpractice while working through an LLC, your personal assets may still be reached under professional liability rules. For professional practices, a Professional LLC (PLLC) or Professional Corporation (PC) may be required by your state's licensing rules.

**Charging order protection varies dramatically by state.** Some states (Wyoming, Nevada, Delaware, and others) offer strong charging-order-only remedies that make it very difficult for creditors to access LLC assets or force distributions. Other states provide weaker protection. The state where your LLC was formed governs this protection, not the state where you live. Multi-state planning may be worth discussing with your advisor.

**Veil-piercing risk is real and ongoing.** An LLC or corporation that is operated as an alter ego of the owner offers no protection. The most common veil-piercing facts are: commingled funds, personal expenses paid from the entity, failure to maintain records, and failure to file annual reports. Maintaining formalities is not optional.

**The QBI deduction is permanent under the OBBBA (enacted July 4, 2025).** The deduction was previously scheduled to expire after December 31, 2025. It has been made permanent. However, the SSTB phase-out rules remain. For 2026, per Rev. Proc. 2025-32, the SSTB phase-out begins at $201,750 (single) or $403,500 (MFJ) and is complete at $276,750 (single) or $553,500 (MFJ). Income thresholds are adjusted for inflation each year; confirm the applicable thresholds for any year other than 2026 with your advisor and on Form 8995 or 8995-A.

**The S-corp re-election waiting period.** Under IRC Section 1362(g), if an S-corp election is terminated or revoked, the corporation generally cannot re-elect S-corp status for five taxable years without IRS consent. This five-year bar applies to re-electing after a termination or revocation -- it is not an automatic lock that takes effect the moment you make your initial S-corp election. IRS consent to re-elect before the five years expire is possible but not guaranteed. Think carefully before terminating or revoking an S-corp election.

**Form 8832 retroactivity is limited.** If you are filing Form 8832 to reclassify an existing LLC, the effective date can be no more than 75 days before the date of filing. You cannot retroactively change your entity classification for most of a tax year after the fact.

**The 60-month restriction after a Form 8832 election.** Once you use Form 8832 to change an LLC's tax classification, you generally cannot change it again for 60 months without IRS consent. Unlike the S-corp re-election rule (which applies only after a termination or revocation), the Form 8832 restriction runs from the date of the classification change itself.

**Bonus depreciation and Section 179 changes (OBBBA).** If your entity places qualified property in service after January 19, 2025, 100% bonus depreciation is available. Section 179 expensing increased to $2,500,000 for tax years beginning in 2025, with the phase-out beginning at $4,000,000 of qualifying property. These provisions interact with the QBI deduction calculation; consult your advisor.

**Current-year note:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act was enacted July 4, 2025, and several of its provisions took effect mid-year 2025 or beginning in 2026. IRS implementing guidance is being issued on a rolling basis through 2026. Verify the current status of any provision that is described as newly enacted before acting.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Forming an LLC but never opening a separate bank account.** An LLC with commingled funds provides minimal real-world protection because the commingling is direct evidence of alter-ego treatment.

2. **Electing S-corp status without setting up payroll.** The S-corp election only saves FICA taxes if you actually run payroll at reasonable compensation. An S-corp that has never issued a W-2 to its owner-operator is a red flag in an IRS audit and a near-certain loss on the reasonable compensation issue.

3. **Setting reasonable compensation too low.** Paying yourself $10,000 per year and distributing $200,000 will attract IRS attention. Use documented analysis (comparable salaries, role description, hours) to set compensation.

4. **Forgetting to file the S-corp return (Form 1120-S) or filing it late.** The penalty for late filing of Form 1120-S is $255 per shareholder per month (up to 12 months) for returns required to be filed in 2026, per the IRS Instructions for Form 1120-S. For a single-shareholder S-corp, that is $3,060 for a 12-month late return. For a five-shareholder S-corp, it is $15,300. File on time or file Form 7004 to extend.

5. **Missing the state annual report.** Administrative dissolution of an LLC or corporation eliminates the entity's legal protections retroactively in many states. Put the annual report due date on a recurring calendar reminder.

6. **Treating the entity as a sole proprietor after election.** Using the entity bank account as a personal checking account, failing to keep minutes, or signing contracts in your own name instead of the entity's name all undermine the legal separation.

7. **Ignoring basis before taking distributions.** Distributions in excess of your S-corp stock basis are taxable as capital gains in the year received. This is an unexpected tax hit that arises when an owner takes more out of an S-corp than the basis supports. Track basis with your advisor annually.

8. **Failing to update payroll when income grows.** If your S-corp profit doubles but your W-2 salary stays the same, the IRS may conclude your compensation was never reasonable. Review and adjust payroll at least annually.

9. **Assuming the S-corp election automatically filed itself.** The IRS must accept Form 2553 before S-corp status is effective. Confirm receipt of the CP261 Notice. If you never received confirmation, contact the IRS to verify the election is on record.

10. **Using a single-member LLC and assuming it provides the same protection as a multi-member LLC.** Some states provide stronger charging-order protection to multi-member LLCs than to single-member LLCs. Discuss this with your advisor if single-member LLC charging-order protection in your state is important to your planning.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before acting whenever you encounter any of the following:

1. **Before choosing an entity type or changing your current entity classification.** The analysis involves your profit level, state tax rules, business type, ownership structure, and personal financial situation. A wrong entity choice can be expensive and slow to unwind.

2. **Before filing Form 2553 or Form 8832.** Deadlines are strict, and errors on these forms -- or filing them out of sequence -- can result in the election not taking effect as intended, or in unintended C-corp status.

3. **Before setting your reasonable compensation for the first time.** The amount must be documented and defensible. Once you start paying yourself, changes attract scrutiny. Set it right from the beginning.

4. **If the IRS questions or reclassifies your S-corp distributions as wages.** This is an employment tax adjustment that can carry interest and penalties. Do not respond to IRS correspondence on this issue without advisor involvement.

5. **If your S-corp income will be above the QBI phase-out threshold for SSTBs.** The interaction between entity type, reasonable compensation, and the QBI deduction is complex at high income levels.

6. **If you plan to add a co-owner, bring in an investor, or transfer any ownership interest.** S-corp eligibility is easily violated by adding an ineligible shareholder. Any ownership change should be reviewed before it occurs.

7. **If your business will hold real estate.** Real estate held inside an operating S-corp creates complications (self-rental rules, passive activity rules, potential inability to use depreciation losses). A separate LLC may be preferable for real estate.

8. **If your state imposes a high franchise tax or entity-level tax on S-corps or LLCs.** The analysis must account for state-level costs, not just federal savings.

9. **If you are converting from one entity type to another.** Conversion triggers can include gain recognition, loss of S-corp basis, and unintended tax events depending on the method chosen.

10. **Before taking any distributions from an S-corp.** Confirm your current basis with your advisor to ensure the distribution does not exceed basis.

## Before You Act

Do not form an entity, make an S-corp election, file Form 2553 or Form 8832, set payroll, change your reasonable compensation, take an S-corp distribution, or restructure any existing entity without first confirming the following with your advisor:

1. **Your specific numbers** (projected profit, current-year income, state taxes) have been run through the break-even model.
2. **Your state's rules** regarding LLC formation, charging-order protection, franchise taxes, and S-corp taxation are factored into the analysis.
3. **Your S-corp eligibility** has been confirmed (shareholder count, shareholder type, one class of stock).
4. **Your reasonable compensation amount** is documented and defensible.
5. **Your basis** is current and any planned distribution will not exceed your basis.
6. **Any timing constraints** (Form 2553 deadline, Form 8832 retroactivity limit, state filing deadlines) have been identified.

The strategies described in this playbook are well-established and legal, but they require correct implementation to produce the intended savings and protection. A missed deadline or a skipped formality can eliminate the benefit entirely and in some cases create a worse position than doing nothing. The purpose of this playbook is to help you understand the strategy and prepare for a productive advisor conversation -- not to replace that conversation.
