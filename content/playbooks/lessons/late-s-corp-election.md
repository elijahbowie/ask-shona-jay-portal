# Late S-Corp Election

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

An S-corporation election is a choice you make with the IRS to have your LLC or corporation taxed as an S-corp instead of as a sole proprietorship or C-corporation. The primary benefit is that business profit above your reasonable salary (defined below) is not subject to self-employment tax. SE tax is computed on 92.35% of your net earnings: the Social Security portion (12.4%) applies only up to the $184,500 wage base for 2026, while the Medicare portion (2.9%) applies to all net SE earnings with no cap -- for a combined rate of 15.3% up to the cap and 2.9% above it. If you missed the original filing deadline, the IRS has a formal relief procedure under Revenue Procedure 2013-30 that allows most eligible businesses to file the election late, sometimes reaching back up to 3 years and 75 days, and still capture those tax savings. This playbook walks you through every step of that late-election process.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is relevant if you meet all of the following:

- You operate as an LLC or corporation (not a sole proprietorship with no entity).
- You have not yet made a valid S-corporation election, or you believe the election was missed.
- Your business generates net profit of at least $40,000 to $50,000 per year (below that threshold, the payroll costs and compliance burden typically outweigh the self-employment tax savings; confirm with your advisor).
- Your federal income tax return for the year you want the election to cover has NOT yet been filed -- or, if it has been filed, it was filed consistently reporting income as if the S-corp election were already in effect (see "Required Conditions" under Key Terms).
- The intended effective date of your election is within the last 3 years and 75 days (meaning the IRS lookback window has not yet closed).
- You have not been notified by the IRS of any problem with your S-corporation status within 6 months of filing the first S-corp return.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Your tax return was already filed and did NOT treat the entity as an S-corp.** If you filed as a sole proprietor (Schedule C) and your shareholders did not report income consistently with S-corp treatment, late election relief under Rev. Proc. 2013-30 is generally not available. A private letter ruling is possible but expensive: the reduced user fee (available only for taxpayers with gross income under $250,000) is $3,450, the next tier ($250,000 to $1,000,000 gross income) is $9,775, and the standard PLR user fee is $43,700 per the annual Rev. Proc. 2026-1 fee schedule [confirm the current fee schedule with your advisor].
- **The 3-year-and-75-day window has closed.** Count back from today to the January 1 of the year you wanted the election. If more than 3 years and 75 days have passed, this relief path is not available.
- **Your entity does not meet S-corp eligibility rules.** You cannot have more than 100 shareholders, you must have only one class of stock, and all shareholders must be U.S. individuals, certain trusts, or estates. Corporations and partnerships cannot be shareholders. Non-resident aliens cannot be shareholders.
- **You are a C-corporation or LLC that is subject to a shareholder agreement restricting a tax election.** Review any existing agreements before filing.
- **Your state imposes a gross receipts tax or other tax on S-corps that makes election unattractive.** California, for example, levies a 1.5% net income tax on S-corp income at the state level plus the $800 annual minimum franchise tax. Some states do not conform to federal S-corp status. Verify your state's treatment with your advisor before filing.
- **Your net profit is not high enough to justify the ongoing payroll compliance cost.** At profit levels below roughly $40,000 to $50,000, the cost of running payroll and filing Form 1120-S often exceeds the self-employment tax savings.

## Key Terms

**S-corporation (S-corp):** A tax classification, not a business type. An S-corp is a corporation or eligible LLC that has elected under Subchapter S of the Internal Revenue Code (IRC Section 1361 through 1379) to pass its income, losses, deductions, and credits through to its shareholders for federal tax purposes. The entity itself does not pay federal income tax; shareholders report their share on their personal returns.

**C-corporation:** The default tax treatment for a corporation. A C-corp pays corporate income tax on its profits, and shareholders pay income tax again on any dividends. Most small-business LLCs that have not elected S-corp or partnership treatment are taxed as either sole proprietorships (single-member) or partnerships (multi-member) by default, NOT as C-corps.

**Self-employment (SE) tax:** The tax that self-employed individuals owe on net business profit, computed on 92.35% of net SE earnings. The Social Security portion is 12.4% and applies only to net SE earnings (after the 92.35% adjustment) up to the $184,500 Social Security wage base for 2026 (this cap is also reduced by any W-2 wages you earn from other employment). The Medicare portion is 2.9% and applies to ALL net SE earnings with no cap. The combined rate is therefore 15.3% on net SE earnings up to the $184,500 wage base and 2.9% on net SE earnings above that amount. An additional 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax applies to net SE income above $200,000 (single filer) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). S-corp shareholders who perform services must pay SE tax (via payroll) only on their reasonable compensation, not on profit distributions.

**Reasonable compensation (also called reasonable salary):** The wage an S-corp owner who performs substantial services must pay themselves before taking profit distributions. The IRS requires this amount to reflect what a comparable employee would earn for the same work in the open market. There is no IRS-prescribed formula or fixed percentage; the standard is facts-and-circumstances. Underpaying reasonable compensation is an audit risk. Your advisor should help you document and set this amount.

**Profit distribution:** A payment from the S-corp to a shareholder that represents a share of business profit, separate from and on top of their salary. Distributions are NOT subject to payroll taxes (FICA). The tax advantage of S-corp status is that the portion of profit paid as a distribution avoids the SE tax that a sole proprietor would owe on that same income.

**FICA:** Federal Insurance Contributions Act. FICA taxes are the employer and employee shares of Social Security and Medicare taxes collected through the payroll system. An S-corp pays FICA on reasonable compensation (the salary), not on distributions.

**Form 2553 (Election by a Small Business Corporation):** The IRS form you file to elect S-corporation tax treatment. Must be signed by all shareholders of the entity. For a timely election, it must be filed by the 15th day of the 3rd month of the tax year the election is to take effect (March 15 for calendar-year entities, or March 16 when March 15 falls on a weekend, as it does in 2026). For a late election, you attach a specific statement to it.

**Revenue Procedure 2013-30 (Rev. Proc. 2013-30):** The IRS guidance document that establishes the simplified procedure for obtaining late S-corp election relief. It consolidates prior guidance and sets out the eligibility criteria, required statements, and filing mechanics. Referenced at IRS.gov and published in Internal Revenue Bulletin 2013-36.

**Required Conditions (for late relief under Rev. Proc. 2013-30):** To qualify, all of the following must be true: (1) the entity intended to be an S-corp on the intended effective date and was eligible; (2) the entity failed to qualify solely because Form 2553 was not timely filed; (3) all shareholders reported their income consistently with S-corp status for every year since the intended effective date; (4) the late Form 2553 is filed within 3 years and 75 days of the intended effective date; and (5) neither the entity nor any shareholder was notified by the IRS of a problem with S-corp status within 6 months of the date the first Form 1120-S was timely filed.

**Form 1120-S:** The U.S. Income Tax Return for an S Corporation. This is the annual federal tax return an S-corp files each year, due by March 15 for calendar-year S-corps (March 16 in 2026 when March 15 is a Sunday). It reports income, deductions, and credits, and generates Schedule K-1s for each shareholder.

**Schedule K-1:** The form an S-corp sends each shareholder showing their share of income, loss, deductions, and credits for the year. Shareholders report K-1 amounts on their personal Form 1040 returns.

**Schedule E:** The IRS form used by shareholders to report S-corp income from Schedule K-1 on their personal tax return (Page 2 of Schedule E). This is the correct form for S-corp income, as opposed to Schedule C, which is for sole proprietors. Consistent use of Schedule E for prior years is required for late election relief.

**Form 941:** Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return. Once you have S-corp status and begin paying yourself a salary, you must file Form 941 each quarter to report payroll tax withholdings and deposits. If a late election is granted retroactively, you may need to file retroactive Form 941s for the period covered.

**First-Time Penalty Abatement (FTA):** An IRS administrative relief program that waives certain penalties for taxpayers who have a history of good tax compliance (no penalties in the 3 prior tax years). For S-corps, the FTA can waive the per-shareholder-per-month late filing penalty on Form 1120-S. Governed by IRS administrative policy; not a statutory right. Can be requested by phone or by written submission using Form 843.

**Form 843 (Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement):** The IRS form used to formally request abatement of a penalty in writing. Used when you prefer not to call, or when a phone request is denied.

**Effective date of election:** The date from which the S-corp election takes effect. For a late election, this is typically January 1 of the earliest year for which you are seeking relief.

**CP261 Notice:** The IRS acceptance letter confirming that your S-corp election has been approved. Do not file Form 1120-S or the associated personal returns until you receive this letter. Processing typically takes 8 to 12 weeks after the IRS receives the faxed Form 2553.

**Penalty notice (CP162):** The IRS notice you may receive after filing Form 1120-S asserting a late filing penalty. This notice is expected and does not mean the election was denied. It is addressed through penalty abatement (see First-Time Penalty Abatement above).

**Lookback window:** The period during which a late election can be filed. Under Rev. Proc. 2013-30, the late Form 2553 must be filed within 3 years and 75 days of the intended effective date of the election.

**Private letter ruling (PLR):** A written IRS determination issued to a specific taxpayer addressing their unique situation. Taxpayers who do not qualify under Rev. Proc. 2013-30 (e.g., because the lookback window has closed) can request a PLR to seek late election relief, but PLRs require a user fee and can take many months to receive. The user fee tiers under Rev. Proc. 2026-1 are: $3,450 for taxpayers with gross income under $250,000 (reduced fee), $9,775 for gross income between $250,000 and $1,000,000, and $43,700 for the standard/ordinary PLR request. Confirm the current fee schedule with your advisor before filing, as fees are updated annually.

**Form 8832 (Entity Classification Election):** The form an LLC files to change its default tax classification. In most cases, an LLC filing Form 2553 to elect S-corp status does NOT need to also file Form 8832, because the IRS deems the 2553 filing to include an entity classification change. Confirm with your advisor whether your specific situation requires Form 8832.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather all of the following before starting the Form 2553 preparation:

1. **Legal name and EIN (Employer Identification Number) of the entity.** EIN is the nine-digit tax ID number assigned by the IRS (format: XX-XXXXXXX). If you do not have an EIN, you must obtain one at IRS.gov before filing Form 2553.
2. **Entity type.** Is it an LLC, corporation, or other eligible entity? What state was it formed in?
3. **Date of entity formation.** The date your LLC or corporation was legally formed (per state records). This determines when your tax year began.
4. **Intended effective date of the S-corp election.** This is January 1 of the earliest year for which you want S-corp treatment. Confirm this year is within the 3-year-and-75-day lookback window.
5. **Names, Social Security Numbers (SSNs), and ownership percentages of all shareholders.** All shareholders must sign Form 2553.
6. **Tax year end.** Most small businesses use December 31 (calendar year). If you use a fiscal year, confirm the specifics with your advisor.
7. **Confirmation of how prior-year returns were filed.** Were personal returns filed using Schedule E (S-corp treatment) or Schedule C (sole proprietor)? Were business returns filed as 1120-S? This determines whether you meet the consistency requirement for late relief.
8. **State of principal business operation.** Determines which IRS Service Center fax number to use and whether your state requires a separate state S-corp election.
9. **Advisor contact information.** Your advisor will prepare or review the Form 2553 package and the reasonable cause statement.

## Required Documents And Records

**Gather before filing:**

- Completed Form 2553 with all shareholder signatures and dates
- A written reasonable cause statement (see Step 4) explaining why the election was not filed timely
- Prior-year federal tax returns (personal and business) confirming consistent S-corp reporting, OR confirmation that returns have not yet been filed
- Entity formation documents (Articles of Organization or Articles of Incorporation) to confirm formation date and entity type
- Confirmation of EIN (Form SS-4 or IRS EIN confirmation letter)

**Create and keep after filing:**

| Document | Retention Period |
|---|---|
| Copy of filed Form 2553 with all attachments | Permanently |
| IRS fax confirmation or transmission report | Permanently |
| CP261 acceptance letter from the IRS | Permanently |
| Any penalty notice received (CP162 or similar) | Until abatement is resolved, then permanently |
| Correspondence related to penalty abatement (Form 843 or written letter) | Until resolved, then at least 7 years |
| All filed Form 1120-S returns | At least 7 years |
| Payroll records (Form 941s, W-2s) once payroll begins | At least 7 years |

**Retention note:** The IRS generally has 3 years from the date a return is filed to assess additional taxes, but that window extends to 6 years if more than 25% of gross income is omitted, and there is no limit if fraud is involved. Keeping S-corp election records permanently protects you if a future year's return is questioned.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

**Before you begin:** Complete the "Required Client Inputs" section above. Do not start filing until your advisor has confirmed you meet the eligibility conditions under Rev. Proc. 2013-30.

---

**Step 1: Confirm Eligibility**

Work through each condition with your advisor:

- [ ] Your entity is an LLC or corporation that was eligible to be an S-corp on the intended effective date.
- [ ] The intended effective date is within the last 3 years and 75 days (count back from today).
- [ ] The reason the election was missed is that Form 2553 was simply not filed (not because the entity was ineligible).
- [ ] All shareholders filed their personal tax returns treating the entity as an S-corp (Schedule E, not Schedule C) for every year since the intended effective date -- OR those returns have not yet been filed.
- [ ] Neither the entity nor any shareholder received an IRS notice questioning S-corp status within 6 months of filing the first Form 1120-S.
- [ ] The entity has no more than 100 shareholders, only one class of stock, and all shareholders are U.S. individuals, certain trusts, or estates.

If any box cannot be checked, stop and consult your advisor before proceeding. You may still have options (such as a PLR), but the simplified procedure will not apply.

---

**Step 2: Download and Prepare Form 2553**

Download the current Form 2553 from IRS.gov (search "Form 2553"). Fill out Part I completely:

- Box A: Name of corporation (legal name exactly as registered with your state)
- Box B: Employer Identification Number (EIN)
- Box C: Date incorporated or organized
- Box D: State of incorporation
- Box E: Effective date of election (enter January 1 of the earliest year for which you want S-corp treatment -- for example, "01/01/2024")
- Box F: Tax year (enter the month and day your tax year ends -- "12/31" for a calendar year)
- Box G: Enter the number of shares issued and outstanding on the effective date
- Box H: Name of officer or legal representative to contact

Every shareholder must sign in the shareholder signature section of Part I, including their name, address, Social Security Number, percentage of stock owned, date acquired, and the tax year end of the shareholder.

**Do not skip Part II** (Fiscal Year information) if you use a fiscal year rather than calendar year. Confirm with your advisor.

---

**Step 3: Add the Required Late-Election Statement**

This is the most critical step. At the very top margin of the first page of Form 2553, write or type this exact phrase:

> **FILED PURSUANT TO REV. PROC. 2013-30**

Do not alter or paraphrase this language. The IRS uses this notation to route the form to the correct process. If you are attaching Form 2553 to Form 1120-S (see Step 5B), you must also write this same phrase at the top margin of the first page of Form 1120-S.

---

**Step 4: Prepare a Reasonable Cause Statement**

Attach a signed, written statement to Form 2553 explaining why the S-corp election was not filed on time. This statement must be specific to your circumstances. It should include:

- The name and EIN of the entity
- A factual description of when and why the entity intended to be an S-corp
- The specific reason the Form 2553 was not filed by the deadline (for example: "The owner was not advised by their prior accountant that an affirmative election was required," or "The entity was formed and began operations but the election form was inadvertently omitted from the initial filing package")
- When the omission was discovered
- Steps taken to correct it promptly after discovery

The statement does not need to be long, but it must be factually accurate and signed by an authorized officer of the entity. Your advisor can draft this with you. Generic language ("we forgot") is less effective than specific factual detail.

---

**Step 5: Choose Your Filing Method**

There are two ways to submit a late Form 2553 under Rev. Proc. 2013-30:

**Method A: File Form 2553 standalone (before filing Form 1120-S)**

Use this method if you have not yet filed the Form 1120-S for any of the years covered by the election.

- Attach the completed Form 2553 (with the Rev. Proc. 2013-30 notation and the reasonable cause statement) as a package.
- Fax the package to the IRS Service Center for your state (see "Fax Numbers" in The Numbers section below). Do NOT mail if a fax number is available; fax creates a transmission record.
- Keep the fax confirmation page permanently.
- Wait for the CP261 acceptance letter before filing any Form 1120-S returns (typically 8 to 12 weeks).

**Method B: Attach Form 2553 to Form 1120-S**

Use this method if you are filing the Form 1120-S for the first year the S-corp election should have been in effect and you have not yet received the election approval.

- Attach Form 2553 (with the Rev. Proc. 2013-30 notation) to the front of the Form 1120-S.
- Write "INCLUDES LATE ELECTION(S) FILED PURSUANT TO REV. PROC. 2013-30" at the top margin of the first page of Form 1120-S.
- File the 1120-S by its due date (or extended due date) in the normal manner.
- Your advisor will determine whether this method is appropriate for your situation.

For most clients who have not yet filed any 1120-S, Method A is preferable because it lets you confirm approval before preparing the tax return.

---

**Step 6: Fax the Package to the IRS**

Fax the complete package (Form 2553 with the Rev. Proc. 2013-30 notation at the top, the reasonable cause statement, and any other required attachments) to the correct IRS Service Center fax number based on your state. See "The Numbers" section below for the fax numbers. Double-check the entire package before sending:

- All pages of Form 2553 are included
- All shareholders have signed and dated
- The Rev. Proc. 2013-30 notation is at the top of page 1
- The reasonable cause statement is attached and signed
- Fax cover sheet includes entity name, EIN, and total page count

Keep the fax confirmation page with a clear timestamp as your permanent proof of submission.

---

**Step 7: Wait for the CP261 Acceptance Letter**

The IRS typically takes 8 to 12 weeks to process the Form 2553 and issue the CP261 acceptance letter. Do not file Form 1120-S or finalize personal returns for the years covered by the election until you have the acceptance letter in hand (if using Method A). If the IRS sends a request for additional information, respond promptly and completely.

---

**Step 8: File Form 1120-S and Personal Returns**

Once you have the CP261 acceptance letter:

- Your advisor will prepare Form 1120-S for each year covered by the election.
- Each shareholder will receive a Schedule K-1 showing their share of income.
- Shareholders report K-1 income on Schedule E of their personal Form 1040 (not Schedule C).
- If any prior personal returns were filed incorrectly (using Schedule C instead of Schedule E), those may need to be amended using Form 1040-X. Your advisor will determine whether amended returns are necessary.

---

**Step 9: Set Up Payroll (Prospectively)**

The S-corp election only eliminates SE tax on the profit portion above reasonable compensation. Once the election is in effect going forward, you must:

- Set up a payroll system to pay yourself a reasonable salary (subject to payroll tax withholding and employer FICA)
- File Form 941 quarterly
- Issue yourself a W-2 at year end
- Pay the employer share of FICA on your salary

For the retroactive year(s) covered by the late election, your advisor will determine whether retroactive payroll or other adjustments are required. In some cases, no retroactive payroll is needed for a prior year if the tax returns are being filed in a specific way; confirm this with your advisor.

---

**Step 10: Respond to Any Penalty Notice**

After you file Form 1120-S, the IRS may send a penalty notice (often CP162) asserting a late filing penalty of $255 per shareholder per month (for returns required to be filed in 2026), up to 12 months. Note: the per-shareholder-per-month penalty amount and the minimum penalty are inflation-adjusted annually under IRC Section 6699; the $255 and $525 figures are specific to returns required to be filed in 2026 and must be re-checked for each filing year. This notice is expected and does not mean your election was rejected. Take these steps:

- Do NOT pay the penalty automatically.
- Contact your advisor immediately upon receiving the notice.
- Request First-Time Penalty Abatement (FTA) if you have a 3-year clean compliance history.
  - By phone: Call the number on the notice and say you are requesting First-Time Abatement for late filing of Form 1120-S; the IRS will check your account automatically.
  - By mail: Send Form 843 (Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement) plus a copy of the penalty notice to the address on the notice via certified mail. Allow 6 to 8 weeks for processing. Additional notices may arrive during that time; do not pay them while your abatement request is pending.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Entity type:** This relief procedure applies to corporations and eligible LLCs. A single-member LLC is treated as a disregarded entity (sole proprietorship) by default; a multi-member LLC is treated as a partnership by default. In either case, you can file Form 2553 directly to elect S-corp treatment; you do not also need to file Form 8832 in most situations. Confirm with your advisor.

**Payroll requirement going forward:** Once the S-corp election is in effect, you must run payroll and pay yourself reasonable compensation as a W-2 employee. Failing to pay any salary and taking only distributions is the most common IRS challenge to S-corp arrangements and can result in the IRS reclassifying distributions as wages, triggering back payroll taxes, penalties, and interest. Set up payroll before or immediately after the election is approved.

**Retroactive payroll:** For the prior year(s) covered by the late election, your advisor will determine whether you need to file retroactive Form 941s. This depends on the facts of your specific situation. Do not attempt retroactive payroll without advisor guidance.

**State conformity -- do not assume your state follows federal:** Each state makes its own rules about whether it recognizes federal S-corp elections.

- States that require a separate state S-corp election include New York and New Jersey (among others). In New York, the state S-corp election is made on Form CT-6, and the deadline is on or before the 15th day of the third month of the tax year to which the election applies (March 15 for calendar-year entities). An election made after that date is effective for the following tax year. Confirm the current CT-6 deadline with your advisor and the NY Department of Taxation and Finance.
- California conforms to the federal S-corp election (no separate filing required) but imposes a 1.5% net income tax on S-corp income and the $800 annual minimum franchise tax. These state-level costs reduce the net benefit of the election for California residents.
- Some states (for example, New Hampshire and Tennessee, historically) tax S-corp income differently at the state level. Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor.

**Consistency requirement -- all shareholders must have filed the same way:** If even one shareholder filed their personal return treating the entity as a sole proprietorship (Schedule C) while another filed as an S-corp (Schedule E), the consistency requirement is violated and the simplified relief procedure is not available. All shareholders must be on the same page. If prior returns need to be corrected, amended returns must be filed before or simultaneously with the Form 2553 filing.

**Current-year election vs. prior-year relief:** If you are in the current tax year (2026) and your timely deadline of March 16, 2026 has already passed, you can still use Rev. Proc. 2013-30 to request relief for 2026 -- provided you have not yet filed your 2025 personal return or 2025 Form 1120-S showing non-S-corp treatment. Confirm the specific year strategy with your advisor.

**TCJA and OBBBA interaction:** The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 and subsequent legislation affect the QBI (Qualified Business Income) deduction, which is separate from SE tax savings. S-corp income may qualify for the QBI deduction under IRC Section 199A. The interaction between SE tax savings from S-corp status and the QBI deduction is case-specific; confirm with your advisor that the overall strategy still makes sense after accounting for both.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Tweaking or paraphrasing the required Rev. Proc. 2013-30 language.** The exact phrase "FILED PURSUANT TO REV. PROC. 2013-30" must appear at the top margin of Form 2553. Any variation risks the IRS processing it as a standard election and rejecting it as late.

2. **Forgetting to have all shareholders sign Form 2553.** Every current shareholder must sign. A missing signature is grounds for rejection.

3. **Mailing instead of faxing.** The IRS accepts fax submissions and it creates a timestamped transmission record. If you mail the form and it is delayed or lost, you lose the documented submission date. Use fax unless your advisor has a specific reason to mail.

4. **Filing Form 1120-S before receiving the CP261 acceptance letter.** If you file the tax return before the election is approved, you may create confusion about the entity's status. Wait for the acceptance letter.

5. **Paying the penalty notice without attempting abatement.** Many clients pay the CP162 penalty assuming it is owed. In many cases, First-Time Penalty Abatement eliminates or reduces the penalty entirely. Always consult your advisor before paying.

6. **Not setting up payroll after the election is approved.** Paying yourself only distributions and no salary is the clearest audit trigger for an S-corp. Once the election is in effect, establish payroll promptly.

7. **Missing the state-level election deadline.** If your state requires a separate S-corp election, missing that deadline can leave you taxed as a C-corp or partnership at the state level even though you are an S-corp federally. Research your state's requirement as part of the same process.

8. **Filing a late election for a year that is outside the lookback window.** Count carefully. If the intended effective date was January 1, 2022, the lookback window closed approximately March 17, 2025. There is no exception to this deadline under Rev. Proc. 2013-30.

9. **Confusing the SE tax savings with the overall tax picture.** S-corp status reduces SE tax but does not reduce income tax. Business income still flows through to your personal return and is taxed at your individual income tax rate. Make sure your estimated tax payments account for the income tax portion.

10. **Skipping the reasonable cause statement or using generic language.** The IRS requires a specific statement explaining why the election was late. Vague or missing statements slow processing and may result in a denial.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and consult your advisor before proceeding if any of the following applies:

- [ ] You are not certain whether prior personal and business returns were filed consistently with S-corp treatment.
- [ ] You have more than one shareholder and you are not certain all have been reporting income consistently.
- [ ] You received any IRS correspondence questioning your entity's tax status within the last 12 months.
- [ ] You believe your state requires a separate S-corp election and you have not confirmed the deadline.
- [ ] The intended effective date is within 3 to 6 months of the lookback window closing (time is short; act quickly).
- [ ] Your net profit in the year(s) covered is below $40,000, making it unclear whether the election generates net savings after payroll costs.
- [ ] You are unsure whether your entity meets the eligibility requirements (shareholder count, class of stock, shareholder types).
- [ ] You have received a penalty notice and are considering whether to pay it or request abatement.
- [ ] You are trying to establish reasonable compensation and are not sure what amount the IRS would consider appropriate for your role and industry.
- [ ] You are considering amending prior-year returns as part of this process.

## Before You Act

Before you fax Form 2553, file Form 1120-S, amend any prior returns, set up payroll, request penalty abatement, or take any other action described in this playbook, confirm your specific facts with your advisor. The rules governing late S-corp election relief depend heavily on whether your prior returns were filed consistently with S-corp treatment, whether the lookback window is still open for each year you are targeting, and whether your state requires a separate action. A filing made without verifying these facts could fail to obtain the election, trigger additional penalties, or close off options that would otherwise be available. This page teaches the strategy and the process; your advisor applies it to your specific situation before you act.
