# Retirement Withdrawal Tax Buckets

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Your retirement savings live in three different "buckets," each with its own set of tax rules for withdrawals. Knowing which bucket to pull from, and in what order, can dramatically reduce the taxes you owe in retirement. This playbook teaches you how each bucket works, how the IRS taxes money coming out of each one, and how to sequence your withdrawals to keep more of what you saved.

---

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is for you if:

- You are approaching retirement (generally within 10 years of stopping full-time work) or are already retired, and
- You hold accounts in more than one of the three tax buckets described below, and
- You want to reduce your lifetime tax burden by being deliberate about which account you draw from each year.

Business owners who have built wealth across a mix of pre-tax retirement accounts, Roth accounts, and taxable investment accounts will get the most value from this strategy.

---

## When This Is Not A Fit

Do not rely on this playbook as your sole guide if:

- You have only one type of account (e.g., only a traditional 401(k)). There is nothing to sequence against.
- Your combined retirement income will be so low that you pay little or no income tax regardless of the source. The sequencing benefit is minimal.
- You have a pension or significant Social Security income that already fills your low tax brackets. Your room to "fill" low brackets with IRA withdrawals may be limited.
- You are under age 59 1/2 and need money now. Early withdrawal penalties and taxes make this strategy less effective; see the exceptions in Key Terms and Common Mistakes.
- You are subject to a required minimum distribution (RMD) larger than your spending needs. You may have less flexibility to choose your withdrawal source than this strategy assumes.

Always confirm applicability with your advisor before acting.

## Key Terms

**Tax bucket:** A category of retirement account defined by how and when the IRS taxes the money inside it. There are three buckets: pre-tax (taxable later), tax-free (Roth), and taxable brokerage.

**Pre-tax account (also called a traditional account):** A retirement account funded with money you did not pay income tax on when you earned it. Common examples are traditional IRAs, traditional 401(k)s, traditional 403(b)s, SEP-IRAs, and SIMPLE IRAs. Every dollar you withdraw from a pre-tax account is added to your ordinary income for that year and taxed at your marginal income tax rate.

**Traditional IRA (Individual Retirement Arrangement):** A pre-tax retirement savings account you open yourself, separate from an employer plan. Contributions may be tax-deductible depending on your income and whether you have a workplace plan (see The Numbers section).

**401(k) / 403(b):** Employer-sponsored retirement plans. A traditional (pre-tax) 401(k) works like a traditional IRA for withdrawal purposes: every dollar out is ordinary income.

**Roth account (tax-free bucket):** A retirement account funded with after-tax money. Contributions have already been taxed, so qualified withdrawals (see definition below) come out completely free of federal income tax, including the growth. Examples: Roth IRA, Roth 401(k), Roth 403(b).

**Qualified Roth distribution:** A withdrawal from a Roth account that is tax-free and penalty-free. To be qualified, two conditions must both be met: (1) at least 5 tax years must have passed since you first contributed to any Roth IRA (the "5-year rule"), and (2) you must be age 59 1/2 or older (or the distribution is due to death, disability, or a first-time home purchase up to a lifetime limit).

**Roth IRA:** A Roth account you open yourself. Unlike a Roth 401(k), a Roth IRA has no required minimum distributions during your lifetime, so you can leave the money to grow indefinitely. Your original contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty because you already paid tax on them.

**Taxable brokerage account (taxable bucket):** An investment account that is not sheltered inside a retirement plan. You use after-tax money to fund it. You pay tax each year on dividends and interest earned. When you sell an investment, you pay capital gains tax on the profit.

**Capital gain:** The profit you make when you sell an investment for more than you paid for it. Calculated as: sale price minus your cost basis (what you originally paid, plus any reinvested dividends that were already taxed).

**Short-term capital gain:** A gain on an asset held for one year or less. Taxed as ordinary income at the same rates as your wages or IRA withdrawals.

**Long-term capital gain:** A gain on an asset held for more than one year. Taxed at preferential rates (0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your income), which are lower than ordinary income rates for most people.

**Qualified dividend:** A dividend paid by a U.S. corporation (or qualifying foreign corporation) on stock you held long enough to meet IRS holding requirements. Taxed at long-term capital gains rates, not ordinary income rates.

**Ordinary income:** Income taxed at the standard graduated brackets (10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, or 37% for 2026). Traditional IRA and 401(k) withdrawals, wages, Social Security benefits (if income is above certain thresholds), and short-term capital gains are all ordinary income.

**Marginal tax rate (tax bracket):** The rate you pay on your next dollar of income. In 2026, the brackets are 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37% for ordinary income. You do not pay the same rate on all your income; each "layer" of income is taxed at its own bracket rate.

**Effective tax rate:** The average rate you pay across all your income, which is always lower than your marginal rate.

**Required Minimum Distribution (RMD):** A minimum amount the IRS forces you to withdraw each year from most pre-tax retirement accounts once you reach age 73. You cannot leave your pre-tax money in the account forever. Failure to take your RMD results in a 25% excise tax on the amount you should have withdrawn (reduced to 10% if corrected within the 2-year correction window). Roth IRAs are exempt from RMDs during your lifetime.

**RMD age:** The age at which RMDs must begin. Under current law (SECURE 2.0 Act, effective for 2023 and later), the required beginning date is April 1 of the year after you turn age 73.

**RMD calculation:** Divide the prior December 31 account balance by your life expectancy factor from the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table (Table III in IRS Publication 590-B). Your financial institution is required to report your RMD amount to you by January 31 each year.

**Roth conversion:** Moving money from a pre-tax account (traditional IRA or 401(k)) into a Roth account in the same tax year. The amount converted is added to your ordinary income for the year and taxed at your current marginal rate. After the conversion, that money grows tax-free and future qualified withdrawals are tax-free.

**Pro-rata rule:** An IRS rule that applies when you own both deductible (pre-tax) and nondeductible (after-tax basis) money in any traditional IRA. When you convert or withdraw, the IRS treats all your traditional IRAs as one pool, and taxes a proportionate share of each distribution based on the ratio of pre-tax money to total IRA value. This is calculated on Form 8606.

**Form 8606:** The IRS form used to report nondeductible IRA contributions, Roth conversions, and distributions from IRAs with a cost basis. Required whenever you make a nondeductible contribution or convert a traditional IRA to a Roth.

**Form 1099-R:** The tax form your financial institution sends you by January 31 each year for any distribution from a retirement account (IRA, 401(k), pension, annuity). You use this to report the withdrawal on your federal tax return.

**Schedule D (Form 1040) and Form 8949:** The IRS forms used to report capital gains and losses from your taxable brokerage account.

**MAGI (Modified Adjusted Gross Income):** Your adjusted gross income (AGI) modified by adding back certain deductions. The IRS uses MAGI to test eligibility for Roth IRA contributions, IRA deductibility, the NIIT, and the OBBB senior deduction phase-out. For most people, MAGI is close to or equal to AGI.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A 3.8% surtax on certain investment income (interest, dividends, capital gains, rental income) for taxpayers whose MAGI exceeds $200,000 (single or head of household), $250,000 (married filing jointly), or $125,000 (married filing separately). These thresholds are not adjusted for inflation.

**OBBB senior deduction (enhanced deduction for seniors):** An additional deduction enacted by the One Big Beautiful Bill (P.L. 119-21) available to taxpayers age 65 or older for tax years 2025 through 2028. The deduction is $6,000 per qualifying individual ($12,000 if both spouses on a joint return are age 65 or older). It is available whether you itemize or take the standard deduction -- it stacks on top of both. The deduction phases out for taxpayers with MAGI above $75,000 (single) or $150,000 (married filing jointly). Source: IRS.gov, "Check Your Eligibility for the New Enhanced Deduction for Seniors" (irs.gov/newsroom).

**Additional standard deduction for age 65 or blind:** A per-condition add-on to the base standard deduction under IRC section 63(f), separate from and in addition to the OBBB senior deduction above. For 2026: $1,650 per qualifying condition for married filers; $2,050 per qualifying condition for unmarried filers (single, head of household, qualifying surviving spouse). Each condition is counted independently -- a married couple where both spouses are 65 and both are blind would add $1,650 x 4 = $6,600 on top of the $32,200 base. Source: Rev. Proc. 2025-32.

**10% early withdrawal penalty:** An additional 10% tax on distributions before age 59 1/2, on top of any regular income tax. This penalty applies to distributions from pre-tax accounts (traditional IRA, 401(k), 403(b), etc.) and to non-qualified Roth earnings. It does NOT apply to withdrawals of your own Roth IRA contributions, which can be taken at any time tax-free and penalty-free because you already paid tax on them. Several exceptions apply (see Common Mistakes and Entity, Payroll, State, and Current-Year Caveats).

**SEPP / 72(t) distributions:** Substantially Equal Periodic Payments under Internal Revenue Code section 72(t)(2)(A)(iv). A method of taking penalty-free withdrawals from a retirement account before age 59 1/2 by committing to a fixed schedule of equal payments for the longer of 5 years or until you reach age 59 1/2. Three IRS-approved calculation methods exist (RMD method, fixed amortization, fixed annuitization). Modifying or stopping the schedule before the required period ends triggers retroactive penalties plus interest on all prior years.

**Standard deduction:** A flat dollar amount that reduces your taxable income automatically if you do not itemize deductions. For 2026: $16,100 for single filers; $32,200 for married filing jointly. Taxpayers age 65 or older (or blind) receive an additional amount on top of this base -- see "Additional standard deduction for age 65 or blind" above.

**Taxable income:** Your income after subtracting the standard deduction (or itemized deductions) and other above-the-line adjustments. Capital gains rates and ordinary income brackets apply to taxable income, not gross income.

**Social Security taxation threshold:** A portion of your Social Security benefits becomes taxable when your "combined income" (AGI plus nontaxable interest plus half of Social Security) exceeds $25,000 (single) or $32,000 (married filing jointly). Up to 85% of your Social Security benefit can become taxable at higher income levels. These are fixed statutory amounts under IRC section 86 and have not been adjusted for inflation since they were set in the 1980s and 1990s; they are not expected to change absent new legislation. Source: IRS Publication 915, Social Security and Equivalent Railroad Retirement Benefits.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before working through this playbook:

1. **Account inventory:** A list of every retirement and investment account you own, with account type (traditional IRA, Roth IRA, 401(k), Roth 401(k), taxable brokerage, etc.), approximate current balance, and financial institution.
2. **Pre-tax account basis:** If you have ever made nondeductible (after-tax) contributions to a traditional IRA, you need Form 8606 from prior years and the total basis amount tracked.
3. **Roth account start date:** The calendar year of your very first Roth IRA contribution or conversion, to determine whether your 5-year clock has started and when it completes.
4. **Estimated retirement income:** Expected income from Social Security, pensions, part-time work, rental income, and any other sources.
5. **Estimated annual spending:** How much you need to withdraw each year to cover living expenses.
6. **Current-year taxable income estimate:** Wages, business income, or other taxable income for the current year, so you can project your remaining room in each bracket.
7. **Filing status:** Single, married filing jointly, head of household, etc.
8. **State of residence:** State income tax rules on retirement withdrawals vary widely. Some states exempt IRA withdrawals; others do not.
9. **Age:** Yours and, if married, your spouse's. Determines RMD start dates, penalty exceptions, eligibility for the additional standard deduction for age 65+, and eligibility for the OBBB senior deduction.

## Required Documents And Records

**Before you start withdrawals, collect:**

- Most recent account statements for all retirement and investment accounts (to verify balances as of December 31 of the prior year for RMD calculations).
- Prior-year Form 8606 (nondeductible IRA contributions) if you have any after-tax basis in a traditional IRA. Keep every 8606 you have ever filed permanently.
- Social Security benefit statement (SSA-1099 or the SSA.gov "my Social Security" estimate).
- Prior-year tax returns (at least 3 years) to verify your income history and any carryforward items.
- Roth account opening documentation showing the year of your first contribution.

**Records to create and keep after each transaction:**

| Document | Retention Period |
|---|---|
| Form 1099-R (received from your financial institution for each withdrawal) | Keep 7 years after filing the return |
| Form 8606 (filed with your return for any Roth conversion or nondeductible IRA withdrawal) | Keep permanently |
| Schedule D and Form 8949 (capital gains from taxable accounts) | Keep 7 years after filing the return |
| Written record of each RMD amount taken and date | Keep 7 years |
| Roth conversion paperwork from your financial institution | Keep permanently (proves the 5-year clock start date) |
| Basis records for taxable account investments (purchase price, date acquired, reinvested dividends) | Keep for as long as you own the investment plus 7 years after you sell |

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Map Your Buckets (Do This Once, Update Annually)

**Step 1. List every account by bucket.**

Create a table with three columns: Pre-Tax Accounts, Tax-Free (Roth) Accounts, Taxable Brokerage Accounts. Fill in each account, its institution, and its current balance. Use Section 1 of the attached Retirement Withdrawal Tax Buckets Implementation Worksheet for this.

**Step 2. Identify your pre-tax basis (if any).**

Locate your most recent Form 8606. If you have made any nondeductible contributions to a traditional IRA, your cumulative basis is shown on line 14 of that form. Record this number -- it reduces the taxable portion of future withdrawals and conversions.

**Step 3. Confirm your Roth 5-year start date.**

Find the calendar year of your first Roth IRA contribution (or first conversion if you have never contributed directly). The 5-year clock runs for 5 consecutive tax years starting January 1 of that year. For example, if you first contributed in 2022, your 5-year clock completes January 1, 2027.

**Step 4. Estimate this year's income from non-discretionary sources.**

Add up Social Security, pension payments, rental income, wages, or any other income you will receive whether or not you withdraw from retirement accounts. This is your "floor income" before any bucket withdrawals.

---

### Phase 2: Calculate Your Bracket Room (Do This Each Year)

**Step 5. Find your current-year standard deduction -- including all applicable add-ons.**

For 2026, the base standard deduction is $16,100 (single) or $32,200 (married filing jointly). If you or your spouse are age 65 or older or blind, add the applicable amount per qualifying condition:

- Married filers: add $1,650 per qualifying condition (per Rev. Proc. 2025-32)
- Unmarried filers (single, head of household): add $2,050 per qualifying condition

If you (and/or your spouse) are age 65 or older, also check whether the OBBB senior deduction applies: an additional $6,000 per qualifying person ($12,000 if both MFJ spouses are 65+), available for tax years 2025-2028, provided your MAGI does not exceed $75,000 (single) or $150,000 (MFJ). This stacks on top of the standard deduction and the age-65 add-on. The total deduction pool available before you hit taxable income can be substantially larger than the base standard deduction alone for the typical retiree reader.

**Step 6. Find the top of your target bracket.**

Choose the highest bracket rate you are comfortable paying on retirement withdrawals. Most people target the 12% bracket or the 22% bracket, depending on their income level. For 2026 (taxable income thresholds):

- Top of 12% bracket: $50,400 (single) / $100,800 (married filing jointly)
- Top of 22% bracket: $105,700 (single) / $211,400 (married filing jointly)

**Step 7. Calculate remaining bracket room.**

Remaining room = Top of target bracket - (floor income - total deductions).

Example (married filing jointly, both spouses age 68, targeting 12% bracket, MAGI well under $150,000):

- Floor income: $40,000 (Social Security partially taxable + pension)
- Base standard deduction (MFJ): $32,200
- Age-65 additional standard deduction: $1,650 x 2 conditions (both spouses age 65+) = $3,300
- OBBB senior deduction: $12,000 (both spouses age 65+, MAGI under $150,000)
- Total deductions: $32,200 + $3,300 + $12,000 = $47,500
- Tentative taxable income from floor: $40,000 - $47,500 = $0 (floor income is entirely offset by deductions)
- Top of 12% bracket: $100,800
- Remaining room in 12% bracket: $100,800 - $0 = $100,800

This means the couple in this example could withdraw up to approximately $100,800 from their pre-tax accounts without any of it being taxed above 12%. Compare this to the $93,000 room that would result from using only the base standard deduction -- the age-related add-ons add roughly $8,000 of additional withdrawal capacity in this example.

**Important caveat:** The OBBB senior deduction phases out once MAGI exceeds $75,000 (single) or $150,000 (MFJ). Because Roth conversions and additional pre-tax withdrawals increase your MAGI, a large conversion can simultaneously consume bracket room and erode the OBBB deduction itself -- a compounding effect that must be modeled carefully. Similarly, crossing these MAGI thresholds can increase Social Security taxation and trigger IRMAA surcharges. Model the full combined effect with your advisor before executing large withdrawals or conversions. (This is a simplified illustration; your actual calculation must account for the full Social Security taxation formula and any other income. Use the worksheet and confirm with your advisor.)

**Step 8. Check for 0% capital gains room.**

The 0% long-term capital gains rate applies if your taxable income stays at or below $49,450 (single) or $98,900 (married filing jointly) for 2026. If your taxable income -- including IRA withdrawals -- stays below these thresholds, you can realize gains in your taxable brokerage account at 0% federal tax.

---

### Phase 3: Design Your Annual Withdrawal Strategy

**Step 9. Cover essential spending with required sources first.**

RMDs are not optional. Calculate your RMD for each pre-tax account (prior December 31 balance divided by your Uniform Lifetime Table factor from IRS Publication 590-B). Your RMD counts as ordinary income. If your RMD plus floor income already meets your spending needs, you may not need additional withdrawals.

**Step 10. Fill low brackets with pre-tax withdrawals or Roth conversions.**

If your floor income plus RMDs leave room inside the 12% (or 22%) bracket and you are not yet at RMD age or your RMD is smaller than your bracket room:

- **Option A -- Take additional traditional IRA / 401(k) withdrawals** to cover spending needs. Each dollar is ordinary income taxed at your current marginal rate.
- **Option B -- Perform a Roth conversion** up to the top of your target bracket. You pay ordinary income tax now, move that money into the tax-free bucket, and it never generates RMDs or taxable income again. This is most powerful in the years between retirement and age 73 (before RMDs begin), when income is often lower.

The choice between Option A and B depends on your projected future tax rates, your RMD projections, your estate goals, and your current cash flow. This is a key advisor review gate (see Advisor Review Gates section).

**Step 11. Harvest capital gains in the taxable bucket if you have 0% room.**

If your taxable income is at or below the 0% capital gains threshold ($49,450 single / $98,900 MFJ for 2026), you can sell appreciated investments in your taxable brokerage account and recognize the gain at 0% federal tax. Then repurchase the same or similar securities to "step up" your cost basis. This is sometimes called "tax-gain harvesting." Be aware of wash-sale rules (which prohibit buying a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after a sale at a loss; they do not technically apply to gains, but confirm with your advisor before executing).

**Step 12. Use Roth and taxable accounts for spending above bracket limits.**

If you need more money than your pre-tax withdrawals and Roth conversions allow within your target bracket, pull the remainder from:
- Your taxable brokerage account (pay capital gains rates, which are lower than ordinary income for long-term gains), or
- Your Roth IRA (qualified distributions are tax-free; non-qualified withdrawals of contributions only are also tax-free, but earnings may be taxable).

**Step 13. Record every transaction.**

For each withdrawal or conversion, note the account, dollar amount, date, and intended tax treatment. File Form 8606 with your tax return for any Roth conversion. Retain your Form 1099-R from each institution.

---

### Phase 4: Annual Review

**Step 14. Repeat Steps 4 through 13 each year.**

Income, account balances, tax law, and spending needs change. Recalculate bracket room every year in the fall (October -- November) before year-end so you have time to execute conversions, take gains, or withdraw before December 31.

**Step 15. Recalculate RMDs each December 31.**

Each year your RMD is based on the balance as of December 31 of the prior year. Use the current-year IRS Uniform Lifetime Table factor for your age (from IRS Publication 590-B or your financial institution's calculation). Take the RMD by December 31 each year (the very first RMD can be delayed to April 1 of the following year, but doing so means two RMDs in one year, which can push you into a higher bracket).

## Timing And Deadlines

| Event | Deadline |
|---|---|
| First RMD (if you turn 73 in 2026) | April 1, 2027 (but consider taking it by Dec 31, 2026 to avoid two RMDs in 2027) |
| All subsequent RMDs | December 31 of each year |
| Roth conversion to count for 2026 | December 31, 2026 (conversions are irrevocable within the tax year; recharacterization of conversions was eliminated by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act and is no longer allowed) |
| IRA contribution for 2026 | April 15, 2027 (the tax filing deadline, not including extensions) |
| File Form 8606 for 2026 Roth conversions or nondeductible contributions | With your 2026 Form 1040, due April 15, 2027 (October 15, 2027 with extension) |
| Annual bracket-room planning window | October through December of each tax year |
| Form 1099-R received from financial institution | By January 31, 2027 |

**SEPP / 72(t) timing note:** If you begin SEPP payments, they must continue without modification for the longer of 5 years or until age 59 1/2. Any modification before the required end date triggers penalties retroactively on all prior payments plus interest. Do not start without advisor guidance.

### Standard Deduction (2026)

**Base amounts:**

- Single: $16,100
- Married filing jointly: $32,200
- Head of household: $24,150

Source: Same IRS newsroom release above; Rev. Proc. 2025-32.

**Additional standard deduction for age 65 or blind (IRC section 63(f)):**

Added per qualifying condition on top of the base amounts above. A "qualifying condition" is: taxpayer is age 65 or older, taxpayer is blind, spouse (on a joint return) is age 65 or older, spouse (on a joint return) is blind. Each condition is independent and each adds the applicable amount.

- Married filers: $1,650 per qualifying condition
- Unmarried filers (single, head of household, qualifying surviving spouse): $2,050 per qualifying condition

Examples for 2026: A single filer who is 67 and not blind adds $2,050 (total standard deduction: $18,150). A married couple where both spouses are 65+ and neither is blind adds $1,650 x 2 = $3,300 (total standard deduction: $35,500). If one of those spouses is also blind, add another $1,650 (total: $37,150).

Source: Rev. Proc. 2025-32, IRC section 63(f).

**OBBB enhanced senior deduction (tax years 2025-2028):**

In addition to the standard deduction and the age-65 add-on above, taxpayers age 65 or older may claim an additional deduction of $6,000 per qualifying person ($12,000 if both spouses on a joint return are age 65 or older). Available to both itemizers and non-itemizers. Phases out for taxpayers with MAGI exceeding $75,000 (single) or $150,000 (married filing jointly). Applies for tax years 2025 through 2028 only.

Source: One Big Beautiful Bill (P.L. 119-21); IRS.gov, "Check Your Eligibility for the New Enhanced Deduction for Seniors" (https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/check-your-eligibility-for-the-new-enhanced-deduction-for-seniors); IRS.gov, "New and Enhanced Deductions for Individuals" (https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/new-and-enhanced-deductions-for-individuals).

Short-term capital gains are taxed at ordinary income rates using the brackets above.

### Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

3.8% surtax on net investment income (interest, dividends, capital gains, rents, royalties, passive business income) for taxpayers with MAGI above:

- Single / Head of household: $200,000
- Married filing jointly: $250,000
- Married filing separately: $125,000

These thresholds are not inflation-adjusted. Source: IRS.gov, "Questions and Answers on the Net Investment Income Tax" (IRC section 1411): https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/questions-and-answers-on-the-net-investment-income-tax

### IRA Contribution Limits (2026)

- Under age 50: $7,500
- Age 50 or older: $8,600 (includes $1,100 catch-up contribution)

Source: IRS.gov, "401(k) limit increases to $24,500 for 2026, IRA limit increases to $7,500" (irs.gov/newsroom).

### 401(k) Contribution Limits (2026)

- Employee elective deferrals (under age 50): $24,500
- Catch-up (age 50-59 and 64+): $8,000 (total $32,500)
- Special catch-up (ages 60-63, SECURE 2.0 Act): $11,250

Source: Same IRS newsroom release above.

### Roth IRA Income Phase-Out Thresholds (2026)

Your ability to contribute directly to a Roth IRA phases out if your MAGI exceeds:

| Filing Status | Phase-Out Begins | Contribution Eliminated At |
|---|---|---|
| Single / Head of Household | $153,000 | $168,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $242,000 | $252,000 |
| Married Filing Separately | $0 | $10,000 |

If your income is above these limits, you cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA but may be able to do a "backdoor" Roth conversion (contribute nondeductibly to a traditional IRA, then convert). Note the pro-rata rule if you have other pre-tax IRA balances. Source: IRS.gov, "401(k) limit increases to $24,500 for 2026" and Notice 2025-67.

### Traditional IRA Deductibility Phase-Out Thresholds (2026)

If you are covered by a workplace retirement plan:

| Filing Status | Phase-Out Begins | Deduction Eliminated At |
|---|---|---|
| Single | $81,000 | $91,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly (you are covered) | $129,000 | $149,000 |
| Married Filing Jointly (spouse is covered, you are not) | $242,000 | $252,000 |

If you cannot deduct a traditional IRA contribution, you can still make a nondeductible contribution (use Form 8606) and convert it to a Roth IRA (subject to the pro-rata rule). Source: IRS.gov, "401(k) limit increases to $24,500 for 2026."

### RMD Age and Deadlines

- RMD start age: 73 (per SECURE 2.0 Act, effective for those reaching age 73 in 2023 or later)
- First RMD deadline: April 1 of the year after you turn 73
- All subsequent RMDs: December 31 each year
- RMD formula: Prior December 31 account balance divided by your Uniform Lifetime Table factor (IRS Publication 590-B, Table III)
- Penalty for missing an RMD: 25% excise tax on the amount not taken (reduced to 10% if corrected within the 2-year correction window, per SECURE 2.0 Act)

Source: IRS.gov, Publication 590-B (2025), "Distributions from Individual Retirement Arrangements"; IRS.gov, "Retirement topics -- Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs)."

### Early Withdrawal Penalty

10% additional tax on distributions before age 59 1/2 from traditional IRAs and 401(k)s. Applies in addition to regular income tax on the withdrawal. Source: IRS.gov, Publication 590-B.

Key exceptions (no 10% penalty) include:
- Age 59 1/2 or older.
- Death or disability.
- SEPP (72(t)) substantially equal periodic payments.
- Unreimbursed medical expenses exceeding 7.5% of AGI (applies to both IRAs and employer plans such as 401(k)/403(b)).
- Health insurance premiums while unemployed (IRA only).
- Qualified higher education expenses (IRA only).
- First-time home purchase up to $10,000 lifetime (IRA only).
- Separation from service at age 55 or older (employer plans only, not IRAs).
- Qualified disaster recovery distributions up to $22,000 (both IRAs and employer plans).

Source: IRS.gov, "Retirement topics -- Exceptions to tax on early distributions."

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**S-corporation and business owners:** If you pay yourself a salary from your business, your W-2 wages count as ordinary income in the year they are paid. High wages can reduce or eliminate your remaining room in low tax brackets, making the sequencing strategy less impactful in years with large compensation. Plan business compensation decisions and retirement withdrawals together each fall.

**Self-employed with SEP-IRA or solo 401(k):** Contributions to a SEP-IRA or solo 401(k) reduce your adjusted gross income and create larger room in low brackets for future years. However, those accounts are also pre-tax and will generate RMDs. Consider how adding more to pre-tax accounts now affects your projected RMD burden at age 73+.

**State income tax on retirement distributions:** Rules vary significantly by state. Some states (e.g., Florida, Texas, Nevada) impose no income tax at all. Others exempt some or all retirement income. Others tax IRA and 401(k) withdrawals at the full state rate. Roth distributions that are tax-free federally may still be taxed by some states. Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor or your state's Department of Revenue before finalizing a withdrawal strategy.

**Social Security taxation:** IRA and 401(k) withdrawals increase your Adjusted Gross Income and can cause more of your Social Security benefits to become taxable (up to 85% of benefits at higher incomes). Roth distributions generally do not count toward this "combined income" calculation, which is a key advantage of drawing from the Roth bucket in higher-spending years.

**TCJA sunset and 2026:** The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA, Pub. L. 115-97) was enacted in December 2017. Its individual income tax rate provisions were always scheduled to sunset after tax year 2025, meaning they would have reverted to pre-2018 rates (higher rates, more brackets) starting in 2026 absent further legislation. The "One Big Beautiful Bill" (P.L. 119-21), enacted in 2025, made the TCJA individual rate structure permanent and extended it beyond 2025. The brackets and thresholds shown in this playbook reflect that extension, as confirmed by IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32. Confirm with your advisor that no further legislative changes have occurred before executing large conversions or withdrawals.

**Medicare premium surcharges (IRMAA):** If your MAGI exceeds certain thresholds, you pay higher Medicare Part B and Part D premiums with a 2-year lookback (e.g., your 2026 income affects 2028 premiums). Large Roth conversions can trigger IRMAA surcharges even if they stay within ordinary income brackets. [Confirm current IRMAA income thresholds with your advisor -- these change annually and were not verified against a public source for 2026 at the time this was written.]

**OBBB senior deduction and Roth conversion interaction:** The OBBB senior deduction phases out at MAGI above $75,000 (single) / $150,000 (MFJ). Because Roth conversions and additional pre-tax withdrawals increase MAGI, a larger-than-planned conversion can push you above the phase-out threshold and reduce or eliminate the senior deduction. This is a compounding effect: the conversion adds income and simultaneously reduces your deduction, increasing taxable income on both ends. Model this interaction explicitly when sizing any conversion. The same MAGI increase can also trigger or worsen Social Security taxation and IRMAA surcharges, so all three effects should be evaluated together.

**Inherited IRAs:** Rules for inherited IRAs changed under the SECURE Act and SECURE 2.0. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must empty the account within 10 years. This playbook covers your own accounts only. Inherited IRA strategy requires separate advisor guidance.

**Roth conversion reversals are no longer allowed:** Since 2018, you cannot "recharacterize" (reverse) a Roth conversion back to a traditional IRA. Once you convert, it is permanent for that tax year. Size conversions carefully and confirm with your advisor before executing.

**Pro-rata rule risk:** If you have pre-tax money in any traditional IRA (including a SEP-IRA or SIMPLE IRA) and you try to do a backdoor Roth contribution, the pro-rata rule applies. You cannot selectively convert only your nondeductible (after-tax basis) dollars. The taxable portion is determined by the ratio of pre-tax money to total IRA value across all your traditional IRAs. This can make the backdoor Roth strategy less attractive or entirely ineffective. Discuss with your advisor before attempting.

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Taking RMDs late or missing them.**
Missing an RMD triggers a 25% penalty on the amount that should have been taken. Set a recurring reminder each December to verify you have taken every required RMD from every pre-tax account.

**Mistake 2: Doing large Roth conversions in a high-income year.**
Converting a large amount in a year when wages, business income, or a one-time gain already pushes you into the 32% or 35% bracket means paying unnecessary tax. Conversions are most powerful in years when your ordinary income is low.

**Mistake 3: Forgetting that RMDs cannot be converted.**
The IRS does not allow you to treat your RMD as a Roth conversion. You must take the RMD as a taxable distribution first, then decide whether to convert additional amounts beyond the RMD.

**Mistake 4: Triggering two RMDs in one year.**
If you delay your first RMD to the April 1 deadline, you must still take your second RMD by December 31 of that same year. This doubles up ordinary income and can push you into a higher bracket or increase Social Security taxation.

**Mistake 5: Ignoring the pro-rata rule when doing a backdoor Roth.**
If you have a large pre-tax IRA balance, the pro-rata rule will make most of any conversion taxable, defeating the purpose. Consider rolling pre-tax IRAs into your employer 401(k) (if the plan accepts rollovers) before executing a backdoor Roth.

**Mistake 6: Withdrawing from the Roth too early.**
Taking earnings out of a Roth IRA before the 5-year clock expires and before age 59 1/2 means those earnings are taxable and subject to the 10% penalty. Your contributions can come out tax-free at any time, but earnings cannot until both conditions are met.

**Mistake 7: Starting SEPP / 72(t) without fully understanding the commitment.**
SEPP payments must continue for the longer of 5 years or until age 59 1/2 without modification. Changing jobs, moving accounts, or miscalculating the required payment amount can trigger retroactive penalties plus interest on every year of payments made so far.

**Mistake 8: Overlooking NIIT on large capital gains years.**
Selling a large position in your taxable brokerage account in a year when you also do a Roth conversion can push your MAGI above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (MFJ) -- or $125,000 if you file married separately -- and trigger the 3.8% NIIT on investment income. Model the combined effect before executing.

**Mistake 9: Assuming state tax matches federal tax.**
Even if a Roth distribution is completely federal-tax-free, your state may tax it. Verify your state's rules.

**Mistake 10: Ignoring the OBBB senior deduction when sizing conversions.**
If you are age 65 or older and your MAGI is near $75,000 (single) or $150,000 (MFJ), a Roth conversion that pushes you over that threshold can phase out the $6,000 (or $12,000) OBBB senior deduction, creating a higher effective tax cost than the bracket rate alone suggests. Always model the OBBB phase-out alongside Social Security taxation and IRMAA when sizing conversions in your 60s and 70s.

## Advisor Review Gates

Do not execute any of the following actions without first reviewing the specifics with your advisor:

1. **Starting your first Roth conversion.** The tax impact depends on your full income picture, state taxes, IRMAA risk, pro-rata exposure, and estate goals. The bracket math in this playbook is illustrative; your numbers must be modeled precisely.

2. **Sizing any Roth conversion above $10,000.** Even if you understand the concept, the exact dollar amount to convert in a given year requires knowing your complete tax return projection, including all income sources, deductions, the Social Security taxation formula, the OBBB senior deduction phase-out (if you are age 65+), and IRMAA thresholds.

3. **Taking your first RMD.** Confirm the correct required beginning date, the correct account balance(s) to use, the correct life expectancy factor for your age, and whether you have multiple accounts that each require a separate calculation.

4. **Beginning SEPP / 72(t) payments.** This is an irreversible multi-year commitment. The calculation method, the permitted interest rate, and the account structure must be set up correctly from the start.

5. **Executing a backdoor Roth contribution** if you have any pre-tax IRA balance. The pro-rata rule must be assessed first.

6. **Any withdrawal strategy in the year you turn 59 1/2, 63, 65, 70, or 73.** Each of these ages triggers rule changes (penalty exception, special catch-up, Medicare, Social Security, RMD). Your strategy should be updated at each milestone. Turning 65 also makes you newly eligible for the age-65 additional standard deduction and the OBBB senior deduction (through 2028).

7. **Any year you anticipate a large one-time income event** (business sale, real estate sale, large bonus, inheritance). These events can change your bracket room dramatically and may make or ruin a planned conversion.

8. **If you move to a different state.** State tax treatment of retirement distributions changes your net-of-tax outcome and may change the optimal withdrawal sequence.

## Before You Act

This playbook is educational. It is not a personalized tax plan. The numbers, brackets, and thresholds shown here apply to federal taxes for the 2026 tax year under current law and were verified against IRS canonical sources at the time of writing. However:

- Tax law changes. Confirm all figures with your advisor before acting, especially if Congress has passed legislation since this page was last updated.
- Your state may impose additional taxes that change the math entirely.
- Your specific income, deductions, Social Security situation, Medicare premiums, and estate goals all affect which actions make sense for you.
- Every withdrawal, conversion, or election in retirement is consequential and in many cases irrevocable.

**Do not take a distribution, execute a Roth conversion, begin SEPP payments, or change any retirement account elections until you have reviewed your specific numbers with your advisor.**
