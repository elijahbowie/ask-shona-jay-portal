# Social Security and Retirement Income Tax Planning

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Once you begin receiving Social Security benefits or drawing down retirement accounts, the amount of federal income tax you owe is not fixed -- it is shaped by when and how much income you take and in what order. This playbook covers four connected strategies: (1) understanding and managing the taxation of your Social Security benefits, (2) timing Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) from your traditional IRAs and 401(k) plans, (3) using Roth conversions to move pre-tax retirement savings into a tax-free account during lower-income years, and (4) using Qualified Charitable Distributions (QCDs) to satisfy your RMD obligation with pre-tax IRA dollars and skip the income inclusion entirely. Done well, these strategies can meaningfully reduce your lifetime federal tax bill and protect your Medicare premiums.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is most relevant to you if:

- You are receiving, or are within a few years of receiving, Social Security retirement benefits (paid out starting as early as age 62, with full benefits beginning at your full retirement age as set by SSA).
- You have a traditional IRA (Individual Retirement Arrangement -- a personal retirement account funded with pre-tax or deductible dollars, where growth is tax-deferred and withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income), a 401(k) (an employer-sponsored retirement savings plan funded with pre-tax dollars), a SEP-IRA (a Simplified Employee Pension IRA used by self-employed individuals and small business owners), or a SIMPLE IRA (a Savings Incentive Match Plan for Employees IRA available in small businesses).
- You are age 59.5 or older and eligible to take penalty-free distributions from retirement accounts.
- You are approaching or have passed age 73, the age at which Required Minimum Distributions must begin from most pre-tax retirement accounts.
- You are currently in a relatively lower federal income tax bracket than you expect to be in future years (or than you were in your peak earning years).
- You make charitable donations and have IRA assets.

## When This Is Not A Fit

The strategies in this playbook do NOT apply, or apply in limited ways, in these situations:

- **Your only retirement income is a pension or Social Security and your total provisional income is well below $25,000 (single) or $32,000 (married filing jointly).** At those income levels, Social Security benefits are entirely tax-free and there is little tax to manage.
- **All of your retirement assets are in Roth accounts already.** Roth IRAs and Roth 401(k) accounts are not subject to RMDs during the original owner's lifetime (under current law post-SECURE 2.0), and qualified Roth distributions are tax-free, so most of this planning does not apply.
- **You need every dollar from your retirement accounts immediately to cover living expenses and cannot afford to leave money in traditional IRAs or pay Roth conversion taxes.** Roth conversions and RMD timing strategies require that you can cover tax bills from non-retirement funds or surplus cash flow.
- **You are under age 59.5 and would face the 10 percent early withdrawal penalty.** While exceptions to the penalty exist, they are beyond the scope of this playbook.
- **You are still in your peak earning years and in a high tax bracket.** Roth conversions are most advantageous when done in lower-bracket years. Converting in a very high-income year often accelerates tax at a higher rate than waiting.
- **Your traditional IRA contains only non-deductible (after-tax) contributions.** The pro-rata rule (see Key Terms) will affect the tax treatment of any conversion.

## Key Terms

**Social Security benefits:** Monthly payments from the Social Security Administration (SSA) based on your lifetime earnings record. Benefits can begin as early as age 62 (at a permanently reduced amount) or as late as age 70 (at an increased amount). Benefits are subject to federal income tax if your provisional income exceeds the base amounts described in this playbook.

**Provisional income:** Also called "combined income" by the IRS. The figure used to determine how much of your Social Security benefit is taxable. It is calculated as: your adjusted gross income (AGI) PLUS tax-exempt interest income (such as interest from municipal bonds) PLUS one-half of your Social Security benefits for the year. This is the key number to manage for Social Security tax planning.

**Adjusted gross income (AGI):** Your total gross income for the year minus specific above-the-line deductions (such as IRA contributions, student loan interest, and self-employment tax). Found on Form 1040, line 11. AGI is used to compute provisional income.

**Base amount:** The provisional income threshold below which none of your Social Security benefits are taxable. The base amount is $25,000 for single filers and $32,000 for married filing jointly filers. These amounts are set by statute and are NOT adjusted for inflation.

**First-tier threshold:** The upper boundary of the first tier. For single filers, provisional income between $25,000 and $34,000 triggers taxation of up to 50 percent of benefits. For married filers, it is between $32,000 and $44,000. These thresholds are also set by statute and do not adjust for inflation.

**Second-tier threshold:** The income level above which up to 85 percent of your Social Security benefits are taxable. For single filers, this is provisional income above $34,000. For married filing jointly, it is above $44,000. "Up to 85 percent" means 85 percent is the maximum -- the exact amount taxable is calculated on the worksheet in IRS Publication 915 or Form 1040 instructions.

**Traditional IRA:** A retirement account where contributions may be tax-deductible (reducing taxable income in the year of contribution), the money grows tax-deferred, and withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. Required Minimum Distributions must begin at age 73.

**Roth IRA:** A retirement account where contributions are made with after-tax dollars (no deduction), the money grows tax-free, and qualified distributions (taken after age 59.5 and after the account has been open at least five years) are completely tax-free. Under current law, the original Roth IRA owner has no Required Minimum Distributions during their lifetime.

**Roth conversion:** The process of moving money from a traditional IRA (or other pre-tax retirement account) to a Roth IRA. The amount converted is added to your ordinary taxable income in the year of conversion and taxed at your marginal rate. Once in the Roth IRA, the money grows tax-free and qualified distributions are tax-free.

**Required Minimum Distribution (RMD):** The minimum dollar amount you must withdraw from your pre-tax retirement accounts each year once you reach the required beginning age. Failure to take the full RMD results in a 25 percent excise tax on the amount not distributed (reduced to 10 percent if you correct the shortfall within two years). Roth IRAs owned by the original account holder are NOT subject to RMDs during that person's lifetime.

**Required beginning date:** The deadline by which you must take your first RMD. For most people, this is April 1 of the year following the year you turn 73. However, delaying the first RMD to April 1 means you must also take your second RMD (for the year you turn 74) by December 31 of the same calendar year, resulting in two RMDs in one year.

**Life expectancy factor:** A number published by the IRS in actuarial tables (found in IRS Publication 590-B) that is used to calculate your annual RMD amount. Your RMD for any year is your prior December 31 account balance divided by your life expectancy factor for your age in that year. The Uniform Lifetime Table is used by most account owners; a Joint and Last Survivor Table is available if your sole beneficiary is a spouse more than 10 years younger.

**Qualified Charitable Distribution (QCD):** A direct transfer of funds from a traditional IRA (or Roth IRA) to an eligible charity. The amount transferred is excluded from your taxable income -- you do NOT include it in your AGI, and the charity never reports it as a taxable gift. QCDs also count toward your RMD for the year, so they can satisfy all or part of your RMD obligation without that amount being included in your income. You must be at least age 70.5 when the distribution is made.

**IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount):** A Medicare surcharge added to your monthly Part B (outpatient/physician coverage) and Part D (prescription drug) premiums if your Modified Adjusted Gross Income (MAGI) from two years prior exceeds certain thresholds. For example, your 2026 Medicare premiums are based on your 2024 tax return income. Managing your income in the two years before Medicare enrollment and in each year of enrollment can prevent or reduce IRMAA surcharges.

**MAGI for IRMAA:** Modified Adjusted Gross Income as used by Medicare. It is generally your AGI plus any tax-exempt interest income. Note that Social Security benefits included in your AGI count toward this MAGI, which means managing your Social Security taxability also affects your Medicare costs.

**Form SSA-1099:** The annual statement mailed by the Social Security Administration by the end of January each year showing the total Social Security benefits you received during the prior calendar year. Box 5 on this form shows the net amount of benefits, which is the figure you use in the provisional income calculation.

**Form 1099-R:** The annual statement issued by your IRA custodian or retirement plan administrator showing the total distributions you received from your retirement accounts during the year.

**Form 8606:** The IRS form used to report Roth conversions and track the basis (after-tax dollars) in traditional IRAs. Required whenever you convert any amount from a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA.

**Ordinary income tax rates:** The tax rates that apply to wages, salaries, retirement account withdrawals, and most other income. For 2026, these rates are 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37%, applied in brackets. Roth conversions and traditional IRA withdrawals are taxed at these rates.

**Pro-rata rule:** A tax calculation that applies when you have any non-deductible (after-tax) contributions in any of your traditional IRAs. If you convert to a Roth IRA and you have a mix of pre-tax and after-tax money across all your traditional IRAs, you cannot choose to convert only the after-tax portion. Instead, each dollar converted is treated as a proportional mix of pre-tax and after-tax money, which affects how much of the conversion is taxable. Your advisor or tax software will calculate this.

**Marginal tax rate:** The tax rate that applies to each additional dollar of income. For example, if a Roth conversion of $20,000 pushes your income from the 22% bracket into the 24% bracket, the portion in the 22% bracket is taxed at 22% and the portion in the 24% bracket is taxed at 24%. Planning a Roth conversion means identifying how much income you can convert before crossing into the next bracket.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before starting your planning:

1. **Your most recent Form SSA-1099** showing total Social Security benefits received during the year (Box 5).
2. **Your current-year estimated AGI** before Social Security benefits are considered. This includes all wages, pension income, interest, dividends, capital gains, rental income, and any retirement account distributions taken so far.
3. **Your filing status** for the current tax year (single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, or head of household).
4. **The prior December 31 balance** of each traditional IRA, 401(k), SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, or other pre-tax retirement account you own. You will need one balance per account. This is available on your December 31 account statement or from your account custodian.
5. **Your age as of December 31 of the current tax year**, or your birthdate, to determine your life expectancy factor for RMD purposes.
6. **Whether your spouse is more than 10 years younger than you and is your sole designated beneficiary** on all retirement accounts -- this determines which life expectancy table to use.
7. **The total basis (after-tax, non-deductible contributions) in all your traditional IRAs**, if any. This is tracked on Form 8606 from prior year returns.
8. **Your two-year-prior tax return (Form 1040)** to understand what MAGI figure Social Security is currently using to determine your Medicare premiums, and to anticipate next year's IRMAA impact of current decisions.
9. **Your projected future income needs** -- a rough sense of whether you expect higher, lower, or similar income in coming years helps determine when Roth conversions are most favorable.
10. **Charitable giving intentions for the year**, including which organizations you plan to donate to, to evaluate whether a QCD strategy is appropriate.

## Required Documents And Records

**Documents to collect and keep:**

- Form SSA-1099 for the current tax year (issued by SSA by end of January of the following year)
- Form 1099-R from each IRA custodian and retirement plan showing all distributions taken during the year
- Completed Form 8606 (for any year in which you make a Roth conversion or have non-deductible IRA contributions), retained indefinitely until all related Roth IRA funds are distributed and the basis is exhausted
- Your December 31 account statements for all traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, 401(k), 403(b), and 457 accounts -- these statements establish the prior year-end balance used to calculate the following year's RMD
- Life expectancy factor from the applicable IRS table (found in IRS Publication 590-B Appendix B): record the factor used each year for each account
- Written acknowledgment from each charity for any QCD: the charity must confirm in writing that no goods or services were provided in exchange for the contribution. Keep this for your records.
- IRA custodian confirmation that a QCD was transferred directly to the charity (not paid to you first and then donated)
- Prior-year Form 1040 and Schedule 1 showing prior Roth conversion history

**Retention guidance:** Retain records supporting each Roth conversion for as long as any funds from that conversion remain in the Roth IRA, plus at least three years after you close the account. Retain Form 8606 filings indefinitely until all traditional IRA basis has been fully distributed and the basis has reached zero. Retain RMD calculation worksheets for at least three years after the tax return for that year is due.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Work through these steps in order. If any step reveals uncertainty about your specific facts, stop and consult your advisor before continuing.

**Step 1: Calculate your provisional income for Social Security taxation.**

Using your current-year estimated figures:
- Start with your estimated AGI for the year (all income except Social Security benefits).
- Add any tax-exempt interest income (such as interest from municipal bonds or bond funds shown in Box 8 of Form 1099-INT).
- Add one-half of the total Social Security benefits shown in Box 5 of your Form SSA-1099.
- The result is your provisional income.

Compare it to the applicable base amount and tier thresholds for your filing status (see "The Numbers" section below). This tells you whether your benefits are currently entirely non-taxable, partially taxable (up to 50%), or subject to the higher tier (up to 85%).

**Step 2: Identify opportunities to reduce provisional income.**

If your provisional income is close to or above a threshold, look for income types you can defer, reduce, or recharacterize:
- Capital gains realizations (selling investments): consider whether the timing can shift to a different year.
- Interest income in taxable accounts: note that moving taxable bonds into tax-deferred accounts before retirement can reduce interest income but requires advance planning.
- Roth IRA distributions: qualified Roth distributions do NOT count in AGI and do NOT count in provisional income. This is a key advantage over traditional IRA distributions.
- Non-Roth retirement account withdrawals: these count in AGI and therefore increase provisional income. Reducing discretionary traditional IRA withdrawals (to only what you need, beyond any RMD) can keep provisional income lower.

**Step 3: Calculate your Required Minimum Distribution for each pre-tax account.**

For each traditional IRA, SEP-IRA, SIMPLE IRA, 401(k), 403(b), or other pre-tax retirement account:
- Take the account balance as of December 31 of the prior year.
- Look up your life expectancy factor in IRS Publication 590-B, Appendix B, Table III (Uniform Lifetime Table), using your age as of your birthday in the current year.
- Divide the prior December 31 balance by the life expectancy factor. The result is your RMD for that account for the current year.

Note: You may aggregate RMDs from multiple traditional IRAs and take the total from any one or more of those IRAs in any combination, as long as the total amount withdrawn equals or exceeds the combined RMD. However, RMDs from 401(k), 403(b), or 457 plans generally cannot be aggregated with IRA RMDs and must be taken separately from each plan.

**Step 4: Determine whether to satisfy any portion of your RMD with a Qualified Charitable Distribution.**

If you are age 70.5 or older and plan to make charitable donations this year:
- Identify eligible charities (501(c)(3) public charities -- generally any charity that qualifies for a charitable contribution deduction on your tax return; DAFs and supporting organizations are NOT eligible for QCDs).
- Determine how much of your planned donation could come directly from your IRA.
- Contact your IRA custodian and request a direct transfer (check payable to the charity, not to you) for the desired amount.
- The QCD satisfies that dollar amount of your RMD for the year without the distribution being included in your taxable income or provisional income.
- The maximum QCD per person per year is $111,000 for 2026 (per IRS Notice 2025-67).

**Step 5: Take any remaining RMD balance not covered by the QCD.**

After the QCD amount, if your total RMD exceeds the QCD amount, you must still withdraw the remaining balance from your pre-tax accounts. This remaining amount is included in your taxable income.
- Withdraw the remaining RMD amount by December 31 of the current year.
- Your custodian will issue a Form 1099-R showing the full distribution.
- On your Form 1040, report the QCD amount on the taxable amount line with "QCD" noted to indicate the excluded portion.

**Step 6: Evaluate whether to do a Roth conversion this year.**

Roth conversions make the most sense when your current marginal rate is lower than you expect in the future. To evaluate:
- Start with your current provisional income after all RMDs, QCDs, and other income sources.
- Find your current tax bracket in "The Numbers" section below.
- Calculate how much additional income (the conversion amount) you can add before reaching the top of your current bracket or triggering the next Social Security taxation tier.
- Decide on a conversion amount that keeps you from crossing into a higher bracket, triggering IRMAA, or pushing more Social Security benefits into taxability.
- Be sure you can pay the resulting tax bill from non-IRA funds; paying the Roth conversion tax from the converted amount reduces the benefit.

**Step 7: Execute the Roth conversion.**

- Contact your IRA custodian and request a conversion of the chosen amount from your traditional IRA to your Roth IRA (both can be at the same institution).
- If you have basis (after-tax contributions) in any traditional IRA, inform your advisor before converting -- the pro-rata rule will apply to determine the taxable portion.
- The converted amount will be reported on a Form 1099-R (code 2 for conversions before age 59.5, or code 7 for distributions at or after age 59.5).
- You must file Form 8606 for any year in which you make a Roth conversion. Your tax preparer will complete this form. There is a $50 penalty per missed Form 8606.

**Step 8: Adjust your estimated tax payments or withholding.**

Roth conversions and RMD withdrawals increase your taxable income in the year they occur. To avoid an underpayment penalty:
- Review whether your current withholding (from pensions, Social Security, or IRA distributions -- you may elect withholding on IRA distributions on Form W-4R) or quarterly estimated tax payments (Form 1040-ES) will cover the additional tax due.
- Increase withholding or pay additional estimated tax as needed. For IRA distributions, you can elect withholding of any percentage. For quarterly estimated tax, payments are due April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year.

**Step 9: Track and report all items on your tax return.**

At tax time, your tax preparer will need:
- Form SSA-1099: to calculate the taxable portion of Social Security using the Publication 915 worksheet (or Form 1040 instructions worksheet).
- All Forms 1099-R: to report all IRA and retirement plan distributions including the Roth conversion.
- Form 8606: to report the Roth conversion and track IRA basis.
- Documentation of any QCD (custodian transfer confirmation and charity acknowledgment).
- Your RMD calculations for each account to confirm the RMD obligation was satisfied.

**Step 10: Conduct annual review and plan for next year.**

Retirement income tax planning is an annual process, not a one-time event:
- Review account balances each December 31 (or as they are reported) to calculate next year's RMDs.
- Revisit whether a Roth conversion is appropriate given your current income, tax law changes, and expected future needs.
- Evaluate whether QCDs are the best use of your charitable giving dollars for the coming year.
- Confirm with your advisor how the prior year's decisions will affect your Medicare premiums two years out (IRMAA is a two-year lookback).

## Timing And Deadlines

**RMD deadlines:**
- Your first RMD: must be taken by April 1 of the year following the year you turn 73. Delaying to April 1 is permitted but means two RMDs in that one calendar year (one for the year you turned 73, taken by April 1, and one for the current year taken by December 31).
- All subsequent RMDs: must be taken by December 31 of each year.
- If you miss an RMD, the excise tax is 25 percent of the amount not distributed. If you correct the shortfall within two years (the "correction window"), the tax is reduced to 10 percent. You may also apply to the IRS for a penalty waiver.

**QCD timing:**
- The QCD must be completed (the transfer must be received by the charity) by December 31 of the tax year for which you want it to count. Do not wait until the last week of December -- custodians need processing time.
- You must be at least age 70.5 on the date the distribution is made (not just during the calendar year).
- Allow at least two to four weeks for a QCD transfer to be processed and received by the charity.

**Roth conversion timing:**
- Roth conversions can be done at any time during the calendar year; however, they must be completed by December 31 of the year for which you want to claim the conversion (unlike traditional IRA contributions, which can be made through the April filing deadline).
- There is no longer a provision to "undo" (re-characterize) a Roth conversion after the fact -- a conversion is permanent.
- Consider completing Roth conversions before mid-December to allow processing time and to confirm the conversion has settled before year-end.

**Social Security benefits taxation:**
- Your Social Security provisional income is calculated annually based on the full calendar year's income. There is no mid-year adjustment. You manage this by managing your total income for the full year.

**Estimated tax payments for 2026:**
- April 15, 2026 (first quarter)
- June 15, 2026 (second quarter)
- September 15, 2026 (third quarter)
- January 15, 2027 (fourth quarter)

**Annual review cadence:**
- October or November: optimal time to project full-year income, decide on Roth conversion amounts, plan charitable QCDs, and adjust estimated tax payments -- all while there is still time to act before December 31.
- January: collect all year-end account statements to calculate the following year's RMDs.
- January 31: IRS deadline for custodians to mail Forms 1099-R to account holders. The Social Security Administration separately mails Form SSA-1099 by the end of January each year.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**Business owners with pass-through income:**
If you also own a business with pass-through income (S corporation, partnership, or sole proprietorship), that net business income counts in your AGI and therefore counts in your provisional income calculation for Social Security. If your business has a strong year, it can push more Social Security benefits into taxability even if your investment and retirement account distributions stay flat.

**Self-employed individuals:**
Self-employed individuals who are receiving Social Security benefits and continuing to earn self-employment income must include that net self-employment income (after the deductible self-employment tax deduction) in AGI. This income is subject to self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare taxes) separately from income tax, and it counts toward provisional income.

**State income tax treatment:**
State income tax treatment of Social Security benefits, RMDs, and Roth conversions varies widely. Some states exempt all Social Security benefits from state income tax. Some states exempt a portion. Some states conform fully to federal treatment. Several states impose state income tax on retirement income that is entirely excluded at the federal level. Roth conversions that are taxable federally may also be taxable at the state level. Confirm the treatment in your state of residence with your advisor before planning.

**Inherited IRAs and the 10-year rule:**
If you inherited a traditional IRA from someone who died after December 31, 2019, different RMD rules apply under the SECURE Act. Most non-spouse beneficiaries must now empty the inherited IRA within 10 years. Inherited IRA RMD rules are NOT covered by this playbook -- if you have inherited retirement accounts, discuss those separately with your advisor.

**Still-working exception:**
If you are still employed and contributing to a 401(k) at age 73 or older, some employer plans allow you to delay RMDs from that specific employer's plan until you retire. This exception does NOT apply to traditional IRAs or to 401(k)s from former employers. Confirm with your plan administrator whether your current employer plan allows this delay.

**Medicare IRMAA two-year lookback:**
Your 2026 Medicare premiums are based on your 2024 adjusted gross income (MAGI). A large Roth conversion in 2024 could be driving a higher 2026 Medicare premium. Conversely, a large conversion you are considering in 2026 will affect your 2028 Medicare premiums. Always project at least two years forward when planning Roth conversions.

**IRMAA appeal (life-changing events):**
If your income dropped significantly in the last two years due to a qualifying life-changing event (retirement, death of spouse, divorce, loss of income-producing property, reduction of pension income, or receipt of employer settlement), you may appeal your IRMAA determination using Form SSA-44 and request that SSA use a more recent year's income. Discuss this with your advisor if a prior high-income year is driving current Medicare surcharges.

**Charitable deduction note for QCDs:**
Because a QCD is excluded from your income entirely, you cannot also claim a charitable contribution deduction for the same amount on Schedule A. The benefit of the QCD is the income exclusion, not a separate deduction. For most retirees who take the standard deduction anyway, the QCD produces a better result than a cash donation because it reduces AGI (and therefore provisional income) rather than adding to itemized deductions that may not exceed the standard deduction.

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Taking voluntary IRA distributions on top of the RMD without checking the provisional income impact.**
Many retirees take extra traditional IRA withdrawals for home repairs, travel, or gifts without realizing that each dollar withdrawn increases their provisional income and can trigger taxation on Social Security benefits that were previously tax-free. Before taking any voluntary traditional IRA withdrawal, run a provisional income calculation to see whether it crosses a Social Security taxation tier.

**Mistake 2: Counting Roth IRA distributions as income.**
Qualified Roth IRA distributions are NOT included in AGI and do NOT count toward provisional income. Retirees who have both traditional and Roth accounts can use Roth distributions to cover living expenses without affecting Social Security taxability. Not taking advantage of this creates an unnecessary tax burden.

**Mistake 3: Missing the RMD deadline and paying the 25 percent excise tax.**
The December 31 deadline for RMDs is firm, and missing it carries a steep penalty. Set a calendar reminder no later than November 1 each year to confirm you have taken or will take the required amount. Do not wait until December 31 to initiate a distribution -- processing delays with custodians are common in December.

**Mistake 4: Making the QCD payable to yourself and then donating it.**
A QCD must be a direct transfer from the IRA custodian to the charity. If the check is made out to you and you then donate it, it does NOT qualify as a QCD -- the distribution is taxable to you and you may claim a charitable deduction separately (subject to all normal charitable deduction rules). Request the QCD be made payable directly to the charity.

**Mistake 5: Converting to a Roth IRA in a high-income year without checking bracket impact.**
A Roth conversion that pushes your income from the 22% bracket into the 32% bracket on the converted portion costs you 32 cents per dollar converted at that margin, which may not make sense given your expected future rate. Run the bracket math before converting. Also check whether the conversion will push more Social Security benefits into taxability, adding a further hidden cost.

**Mistake 6: Ignoring IRMAA when planning Roth conversions.**
A Roth conversion that crosses an IRMAA threshold adds significant Medicare costs for the two following years. For married filers, crossing from below $218,000 to just above $218,000 in MAGI can add $81.20 per month per person in Part B surcharges plus Part D surcharges. Project your IRMAA exposure before finalizing a conversion amount.

**Mistake 7: Not filing Form 8606 after a Roth conversion.**
Form 8606 is required any time you make a Roth conversion. Missing this form creates record-keeping problems that can cause double taxation in the future (because the IRS cannot confirm the conversion was already taxed). The $50 failure-to-file penalty is minor compared to the long-term record-keeping damage.

**Mistake 8: Using IRA funds to pay the conversion tax.**
If you convert $50,000 but use $11,000 from the IRA itself to pay the tax (by withholding from the distribution), only $39,000 actually lands in the Roth IRA. The $11,000 is treated as a distribution, not a conversion, and if you are under 59.5, it may trigger the 10 percent early withdrawal penalty. Pay Roth conversion taxes from non-IRA funds to maximize the amount that moves into the tax-free Roth account.

**Mistake 9: Converting more than one year's worth of low-bracket room.**
Roth conversions work best when done gradually over multiple years to "fill up" the lower brackets each year. Converting five years' worth of pre-tax money in one year to "get it over with" can push all of that income into higher brackets, paying more tax than if the same amount had been converted gradually.

**Mistake 10: Forgetting that the Social Security income thresholds are not inflation-adjusted.**
The $25,000 and $34,000 thresholds for single filers, and the $32,000 and $44,000 thresholds for married filers, have not changed since 1993. As general price levels rise and retirement income grows, more retirees cross these thresholds over time. Planning to keep provisional income below the thresholds requires annual review, not a one-time fix.

## Advisor Review Gates

You must review the following decisions with your advisor before acting. Do not proceed based on this page alone.

1. **First-year RMD: delay to April 1 or take by December 31?** Delaying your first RMD to April 1 of the following year is permitted but results in two RMDs in that calendar year. Depending on your income situation, taking two RMDs in one year may push you into a higher bracket or trigger IRMAA. Your advisor should model both scenarios (December 31 vs. April 1) before you decide.

2. **Roth conversion amount and timing:** The right conversion amount depends on your current bracket, expected future bracket, IRMAA exposure, state tax, Social Security taxation impact, and how many years of conversion opportunity you have. This calculation involves multiple interacting variables and should not be done based on bracket tables alone.

3. **Pro-rata calculation if you have IRA basis:** If any of your traditional IRAs contain non-deductible contributions (after-tax dollars tracked on Form 8606), the pro-rata rule will determine what portion of any conversion is taxable. Confirm the basis amount and the pro-rata calculation with your advisor or tax preparer before converting.

4. **QCD eligibility of your chosen charities:** Not all nonprofits qualify for QCDs. Donor-advised funds (DAFs) and private foundations do NOT qualify. Supporting organizations may or may not qualify. Confirm the specific organization is an eligible 501(c)(3) public charity before requesting the distribution.

5. **One-time QCD to a split-interest entity:** The one-time election to transfer up to $55,000 (2026; per IRS Notice 2025-67) to a charitable remainder trust or charitable gift annuity funded by a QCD is irrevocable and complex. Do not make this election without legal and tax review.

6. **State income tax on Roth conversions and RMDs:** If your state imposes income tax on retirement distributions, your advisor should include state tax in the conversion cost calculation, not just federal.

7. **Inherited IRA RMD obligations:** If you have any inherited IRAs, your RMD obligations and timing rules are different from those for your own accounts. Discuss these separately with your advisor before taking any distributions from inherited accounts.

8. **Social Security benefit timing (not yet claiming):** If you have not yet claimed Social Security benefits, the decision of when to begin (age 62 through 70) affects your benefit amount for life and interacts with your income tax plan. That timing decision is beyond the scope of this playbook and requires personalized analysis by your advisor.

9. **IRMAA appeal eligibility:** If a prior year's high income is driving a current Medicare surcharge you believe does not reflect your current situation, discuss with your advisor whether a life-changing event appeal under Form SSA-44 is appropriate.
