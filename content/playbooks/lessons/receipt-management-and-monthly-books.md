# Receipt Management and Monthly Books

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Receipt management and monthly bookkeeping are the foundation every other tax strategy rests on. Without organized, contemporaneous records you cannot prove the deductions you claim, and without closing your books each month you have no reliable picture of your tax position until it is too late to act on it. This playbook shows you exactly how to capture, store, and categorize every business transaction each month so that your tax return is defensible, your estimated tax payments are accurate, and your advisor has clean data to work from.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is relevant to every business owner who files a U.S. federal tax return with business income or expenses, including:

- **Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs** (taxed as sole proprietors) reporting on Schedule C (IRS Form 1040, Schedule C -- Profit or Loss from Business).
- **S-corporation owner-employees** who need clean books at the entity level to support payroll, distributions, and annual returns (Form 1120-S).
- **C-corporation owners** who need clean entity books for Form 1120.
- **Partnership and multi-member LLC members** who need clean entity books for Form 1065.
- **Any business owner** who has received an IRS notice, gone through an audit, or been told by their advisor that their records were inadequate.

If you have a business and you claim deductions, this playbook applies to you.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **You have a dedicated bookkeeper or accountant who closes your books monthly and you have confirmed your system is audit-ready.** Review this playbook once to verify your system meets IRS requirements, then skip to the Completion Checklist.
- **Your business has zero deductible expenses.** If you are not claiming any business deductions, receipt management is less critical (though income records are always required).
- **You are an employee (W-2 only) with no business income or side income.** Standard employment records are handled by your employer; this playbook covers business-owner recordkeeping.

## Key Terms

**Schedule C:** IRS Form 1040, Schedule C, Profit or Loss from Business (Sole Proprietorship). Sole proprietors and single-member LLC owners use this form to report business income and deductible expenses on their personal tax return.

**Gross receipts:** The total income your business receives before subtracting any expenses. Includes cash, checks, credit card payments, electronic transfers, barter, and any other form of payment you receive for goods or services.

**Deductible business expense:** A cost that is "ordinary" (common and accepted in your trade or business) and "necessary" (helpful and appropriate for your business) under Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 162. Deductible expenses reduce your taxable business income dollar for dollar.

**Contemporaneous record:** A record created at or near the time the expense occurs, not reconstructed from memory weeks or months later. The IRS gives significantly more weight to contemporaneous records than to records recreated later.

**Documentary evidence:** Physical or electronic proof of an expense -- a receipt, invoice, canceled check, bank statement, or credit card statement -- that shows the amount, date, vendor, and description of what was purchased. Required for most business expenses under IRS rules.

**Substantiation:** The process of proving a deduction is legitimate by presenting adequate records. Under IRC Section 274(d), travel, meals, vehicle use, and gifts require records showing the amount, date, place, and business purpose of each expense. Without adequate substantiation, a deduction can be disallowed entirely.

**The $75 receipt rule:** Under IRS Publication 463, documentary evidence (a receipt, canceled check, or bill) is required for every business expense of $75 or more, and for all lodging expenses regardless of amount. For expenses under $75 (other than lodging), a receipt is not required by IRS rules -- but a written log entry recording the amount, date, vendor, and business purpose is still the best practice.

**Business purpose:** A brief description of why the expense was necessary for your business -- for example, "client meeting to discuss Q3 marketing strategy" or "software subscription for invoicing." This must be recorded for meals, travel, gifts, and vehicle expenses, and is good practice for every deduction.

**Chart of accounts:** The master list of categories (called "accounts") your bookkeeping system uses to sort income and expenses. Common expense accounts include Advertising, Meals (50% deductible), Office Supplies, Professional Fees, Rent, Travel, and Vehicle Expenses.

**Cash-basis accounting:** A bookkeeping method in which income is recorded when you actually receive payment and expenses are recorded when you actually pay them. Most small businesses use cash-basis accounting. The alternative is accrual-basis accounting, in which income and expenses are recorded when earned or incurred, regardless of when cash changes hands.

**Accrual-basis accounting:** A bookkeeping method in which income is recorded when it is earned (the invoice is sent) and expenses are recorded when they are incurred (the bill arrives), regardless of when cash actually moves. Required for some larger businesses; check with your advisor if your annual gross receipts exceed a threshold.

**Bank reconciliation:** The process of comparing your bookkeeping records to your actual bank and credit card statements to confirm every transaction is accounted for and no errors exist. Done monthly, it catches missed expenses, duplicate entries, and fraud.

**Period of limitations (statute of limitations):** The window of time during which the IRS can audit your return and assess additional tax. For most returns, this is three years from the filing date. It extends to six years if you understated income by more than 25% of gross income shown on your return. It is unlimited if you file a fraudulent return or no return at all.

**Self-employment tax:** The tax that self-employed individuals pay to fund Social Security and Medicare, in place of the payroll taxes an employer would otherwise withhold. The rate is 15.3% (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare) on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings, up to the Social Security wage base. Self-employment tax is calculated on Schedule SE.

**Estimated tax payments:** Quarterly prepayments of income tax and self-employment tax that self-employed individuals and business owners are generally required to make throughout the year because no employer withholds tax for them. Filed on Form 1040-ES. See "Timing and Deadlines" for due dates.

**Safe harbor (estimated tax):** A rule that protects you from the underpayment penalty even if your actual tax bill turns out to be higher than expected, provided you paid at least (a) 90% of your current-year tax liability, or (b) 100% of the prior year's tax liability (110% if your prior-year adjusted gross income exceeded $150,000).

**Form 1099-NEC / 1099-MISC:** IRS information returns that clients or customers who paid you $600 or more during the year are required to send you. "NEC" stands for Nonemployee Compensation. These forms report income that must be included in your gross receipts.

**Mileage log:** A contemporaneous written record of each business vehicle trip, showing the date, starting point, destination, business purpose, and odometer readings (or miles driven). Required to substantiate vehicle deductions.

**Standard mileage rate:** An IRS-published per-mile rate used as a simple alternative to tracking actual vehicle operating costs. For 2026, the business standard mileage rate is 72.5 cents per mile.

**Rev. Proc. 97-22:** The IRS revenue procedure (official IRS guidance) that authorizes taxpayers to store tax records electronically rather than as paper originals. Digital scans, photos, or electronic copies of receipts meet IRS requirements as long as they are legible, complete, organized, and retrievable.

**Schedule SE:** IRS Schedule SE, Self-Employment Tax. Attached to Form 1040, it calculates the self-employment tax owed on net earnings from self-employment.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these items before implementing the system:

1. **Entity type and tax classification:** Sole proprietorship, single-member LLC (taxed as sole proprietor), S-corp, C-corp, or partnership. Confirm this affects which tax forms your business files.
2. **Business bank account(s):** Account numbers and login credentials for every bank account used for business transactions. You need a dedicated business checking account -- if you are commingling business and personal funds in one account, open a separate business account before proceeding.
3. **Business credit card(s):** If you use a credit card for business expenses, it should be a dedicated business card. List all cards used.
4. **Current bookkeeping tool (if any):** QuickBooks, Wave, FreshBooks, spreadsheet, or none.
5. **Prior year tax return:** Useful to identify the expense categories your business has historically used.
6. **Chart of accounts from your accountant or advisor:** Ask your advisor for the expense categories they want you to use so your books map directly to your tax return.
7. **List of recurring monthly expenses:** Subscriptions, rent, insurance, loan payments -- any expense that recurs so you can set up automatic categorization.
8. **Pending 1099s received:** Any Form 1099-NEC or 1099-MISC you have received for income you earned.

## Required Documents And Records

### Income Records (keep every source)

- Cash register tapes, point-of-sale reports, or sales logs
- Copies of all invoices you issued to clients or customers
- Bank deposit slips confirming payments received
- Credit card processing reports (Stripe, Square, PayPal, etc.)
- Forms 1099-NEC and 1099-MISC received from clients who paid you $600 or more
- Records of barter or in-kind payments received (record at fair market value)

### Expense Records

- Receipts and invoices for all expenses of $75 or more (required by IRS)
- Receipts and invoices for lodging expenses regardless of amount (required by IRS)
- Bank statements and credit card statements covering all business accounts
- Canceled checks or electronic payment confirmations
- Mileage log for all business vehicle trips (date, origin, destination, purpose, miles)
- Documentation of business purpose for all meals, travel, gifts, and vehicle expenses

### Employment and Payroll Records (if you have employees or pay yourself a salary as an S-corp owner)

- Payroll registers for every pay period
- Copies of Forms W-2 issued to employees
- Copies of Forms 941 (quarterly payroll tax returns) and Form 940 (annual federal unemployment return)
- Employment tax deposit records

### Asset Records

- Purchase receipts and invoices for any equipment, furniture, vehicles, or other business property
- Records of the date placed in service and the business use percentage
- Depreciation schedules (your bookkeeper or advisor maintains these)

### How Long to Keep Records

The IRS retention rules are based on the period of limitations (how long the IRS can audit you):

| Situation | Keep records for |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Standard: you reported all income and no special situations apply | 3 years from the date you filed the return (or the due date, whichever is later) |
| You underreported income by more than 25% of gross income shown on the return | 6 years |
| You filed a fraudulent return, or you did not file a return | Indefinitely |
| Bad debt deduction or worthless securities claim | 7 years |
| Employment tax records | At least 4 years after the date the tax becomes due or is paid, whichever is later |
| Property records (equipment, real estate) | Until the period of limitations expires for the year you sell or dispose of the property |

**Best practice recommendation:** Keep all business tax records and supporting documents for a minimum of 7 years to cover all scenarios, then consult your advisor before discarding anything.

### Electronic Storage

Under IRS Rev. Proc. 97-22, digital images of receipts and documents are acceptable substitutes for paper originals, provided:
- The image is a complete, legible reproduction of the original
- Records are organized and can be located and retrieved on request (for example, by date or vendor)
- The system has reasonable controls to prevent records from being altered after storage
- You can produce records in readable format if the IRS requests them

You are not required to keep paper originals once a compliant digital copy exists.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Set Up Your System (One-Time, Do This First)

**Step 1: Open a dedicated business bank account (if you have not already done so).**
Use an account that is 100% for business transactions. Never pay personal expenses from this account. Never deposit personal funds into this account (other than initial capital contributions). This separation is the single most important step -- it makes reconciliation fast and makes a potential audit far less painful.

**Step 2: Get a dedicated business credit card.**
Apply for a business credit card to be used exclusively for business expenses. If you already have one, confirm it is not used for personal purchases. Using a dedicated card creates an automatic, categorized record of every expense.

**Step 3: Choose a bookkeeping tool.**
Select one of the following and set it up before month-end:
- **Cloud bookkeeping software** (QuickBooks Online, Wave, FreshBooks, Xero): Connects directly to your bank and credit card accounts and imports transactions automatically. Recommended for most business owners.
- **Spreadsheet:** Acceptable if your transaction volume is low (under 50 transactions per month). Ask your advisor for a template that maps to your Schedule C or entity return categories.

Confirm your choice with your advisor, who may have a preferred system or template that reduces the cleanup they need to do at tax time.

**Step 4: Set up your chart of accounts.**
Work with your advisor to create or confirm the expense categories in your bookkeeping system. At minimum, you need separate accounts for:
- Gross revenue (broken out by income type if relevant)
- Advertising and marketing
- Auto and vehicle (or mileage)
- Bank fees and merchant processing fees
- Education and training
- Equipment and supplies (under $2,500 per item -- items above this threshold may need to be capitalized and depreciated; confirm with your advisor)
- Home office (if applicable)
- Insurance (business)
- Legal and professional services
- Meals (business meals are 50% deductible -- keep this category separate from other expenses)
- Office supplies
- Rent (business space)
- Software and subscriptions
- Travel (airfare, hotel, ground transportation -- separate from meals)
- Utilities (business portion)
- Wages (if you have employees)

**Step 5: Connect your bank and credit card accounts to your bookkeeping software.**
Most cloud bookkeeping tools allow you to connect accounts securely so transactions import automatically. Do this as part of setup.

**Step 6: Set up a receipt capture system.**
Choose how you will capture receipts at the moment of purchase. Options:
- **Smartphone photo:** Take a photo of every paper receipt immediately after a purchase. Store in a dedicated folder (Google Drive, Dropbox, or within your bookkeeping software's receipt feature).
- **Bookkeeping software receipt capture:** QuickBooks Online, Wave, and others have mobile apps that let you photograph and attach receipts directly to transactions.
- **Email receipts:** For online purchases, set up a dedicated business email folder for all order confirmations and electronic receipts.
The method does not matter; consistency does. Establish a habit of capturing the receipt before you leave the counter or close the browser tab.

**Step 7: Create a mileage log.**
If you use a vehicle for business, start a mileage log today. Every business trip requires:
- Date
- Starting location
- Destination
- Business purpose (brief description: "client meeting," "supply run," etc.)
- Miles driven (odometer start and end, or total miles)

You can use a physical log book, a smartphone app (MileIQ, Everlance, etc.), or a spreadsheet. Record each trip the same day you drive it. Reconstructing mileage from memory is unreliable and will not survive an audit.

---

### Phase 2: Monthly Closing Routine (Do This Every Month)

Complete the following steps within the first 10 business days of the following month -- for example, close January's books by February 10.

**Step 8: Download and import all bank and credit card statements.**
If your bookkeeping software is connected to your accounts, confirm that all transactions for the prior month have imported. If you use a spreadsheet, download statements in CSV or PDF format and enter transactions manually.

**Step 9: Categorize every transaction.**
Assign each income transaction to a revenue account and each expense transaction to the correct expense account. Rules:
- Assign meals to the Meals (50% deductible) account, NOT to Entertainment (entertainment expenses are generally not deductible for federal income tax purposes under current law -- confirm the current rules for your situation with your advisor).
- Assign vehicle expenses using either the standard mileage method (72.5 cents per mile for 2026, from your mileage log) or actual expenses (fuel, repairs, insurance, depreciation). You cannot use both methods for the same vehicle in the same year. Your advisor can help you choose.
- For mixed-use expenses (cell phone, home internet): record the full payment and note the business-use percentage. Your advisor will apply the correct percentage at year-end.

**Step 10: Attach receipts to transactions.**
For every expense of $75 or more (and all lodging expenses regardless of amount), confirm that a receipt or invoice is attached to or stored in a location keyed to that transaction. If a receipt is missing, try to obtain a duplicate from the vendor before the month ends. Note any expenses where a receipt is not available.

For expenses under $75 (other than lodging), a receipt is not legally required, but record the date, vendor, amount, and business purpose in your log or bookkeeping software.

**Step 11: Add the business purpose note to meals, travel, and gifts.**
For every meal, travel expense, or business gift, add a note directly in the transaction record that states:
- Who was present (for meals: names and business relationship)
- The business purpose (what was discussed or accomplished)

The IRS requires this information to substantiate these deductions under IRC Section 274(d). Without it, the deduction can be denied even if you have a receipt.

**Step 12: Reconcile your bank accounts.**
Compare your bookkeeping records to the actual bank and credit card statements line by line:
- Every transaction on the statement should appear in your books.
- Every transaction in your books should appear on the statement.
- Beginning and ending balances should match.

Resolve every discrepancy before moving to the next step. Common issues: duplicate entries, missing entries, bank fees you forgot to record, a personal expense that accidentally ran through the business account (remove it and reclassify it as an owner's draw).

**Step 13: Update your mileage log total.**
Add up the business miles for the month and record the total in your bookkeeping system under Auto/Vehicle or Mileage.

**Step 14: Review your Profit and Loss report.**
Run a Profit and Loss report (also called an Income Statement) for the month in your bookkeeping software. Review:
- Are gross revenues in line with what you invoiced and collected?
- Are any expense categories unusually high or low? Investigate before assuming it is correct.
- Do the numbers match what you expected based on how the month felt?

This review is your early warning system. If something looks wrong, it is much easier to fix now than at tax time.

**Step 15: Estimate your quarterly tax liability and confirm your estimated payment is on track.**
After closing each month, divide your estimated annual net profit by four to get a rough quarterly taxable income figure. Apply your estimated effective tax rate (your advisor can help you calculate this) plus self-employment tax (approximately 14.13% of net self-employment income, which is 15.3% applied to 92.35% of net earnings) to estimate what you owe per quarter. Confirm that your upcoming estimated payment covers the obligation.

---

### Phase 3: Quarterly Actions

**Step 16: Make your estimated tax payment on time.**
See "Timing and Deadlines" for the 2026 due dates. Pay via EFTPS (Electronic Federal Tax Payment System at eftps.gov) or IRS Direct Pay (directpay.irs.gov). Keep a copy of the payment confirmation.

**Step 17: Run a quarterly Profit and Loss report.**
Compare the quarter's results to the prior quarter and to the same quarter last year if available. Share this report with your advisor at the quarterly check-in.

**Step 18: Check for any new vendor relationships requiring a Form 1099.**
If you paid any individual or unincorporated business $600 or more during the quarter for services (not goods), you may need to issue them a Form 1099-NEC by January 31 of the following year (or the next business day if January 31 falls on a weekend -- for 2026 payments, the adjusted deadline is February 1, 2027). Collect a Form W-9 from each such vendor now, while you can still reach them easily. Do not wait until January.

---

### Phase 4: Year-End Actions

**Step 19: Close the December books by January 10.**
Follow steps 8 through 14 for December. This is the most important month to close on time.

**Step 20: Compile and organize all 12 months of records.**
Create a year-end folder (physical or digital) containing:
- All 12 reconciled monthly bank and credit card statements
- The full-year Profit and Loss report
- The full-year Balance Sheet (a snapshot of what the business owns and owes)
- Your mileage log for the year
- All receipts and invoices for expenses of $75 or more
- All 1099-NEC and 1099-MISC received
- Copies of all W-9s collected from vendors

**Step 21: Issue Forms 1099-NEC to required recipients by February 1, 2027.**
If you paid any non-employee individual or unincorporated entity $600 or more in the calendar year for services, you are required to send them a Form 1099-NEC and file a copy with the IRS. The statutory deadline is January 31; because January 31, 2027 falls on a Sunday, the deadline shifts to Monday, February 1, 2027 for both paper and electronic filing. Note: no extension is available for Form 1099-NEC. Work with your advisor on this step; failure to file required 1099s can result in penalties.

**Step 22: Deliver your year-end package to your advisor.**
Provide your clean, reconciled books, all supporting documents, and your year-end folder to your advisor with enough lead time before your tax return due date (April 15, 2027 for 2026 individual returns, or October 15, 2027 if you file for an extension).

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

### Entity Caveats

**Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (taxed as sole proprietor):** All income and expenses flow to your Schedule C. Your books only need to be accurate enough to complete that schedule. You are not required to use accrual accounting unless your average annual gross receipts exceed a threshold [confirm the current gross receipts threshold for mandatory accrual accounting with your advisor -- the figure is subject to change and must be verified for your entity type].

**S-corporations:** The entity files Form 1120-S. Books must be closed at the entity level, not just in your personal records. Owner-employee compensation (salary) must run through payroll, separate from distributions. Your bookkeeping must track basis (how much you have invested in the company) because losses are only deductible to the extent of your basis. Work with your advisor on basis tracking.

**C-corporations:** File Form 1120. Entertainment expenses are not deductible for C-corps (same federal rules as other entities). Meals remain 50% deductible. Dividend distributions to shareholders are not deductible by the corporation (this is the "double taxation" issue specific to C-corps -- income is taxed at the corporate level and again when distributed as dividends).

**Partnerships and multi-member LLCs:** File Form 1065. Each partner's share of income and loss flows to their personal return on Schedule K-1. Clean partnership books are essential for accurate K-1s.

### Payroll Caveats

If you are an S-corp owner who pays yourself a salary, your business must:
- Run payroll and withhold federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare
- Deposit payroll taxes on time (penalties for late deposits are significant)
- File quarterly Form 941 (Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return)
- File annual Form 940 (Employer's Annual Federal Unemployment Tax Return)
- Issue Form W-2 to yourself by January 31 (or next business day if January 31 falls on a weekend)

These payroll records are part of your books and must be retained for at least 4 years.

### State Caveats

- Many states impose their own income taxes on business income. Your bookkeeping system must track income and expenses in a way that supports both federal and state returns.
- Some states have their own receipt-retention or recordkeeping requirements that differ from federal rules. Check with your advisor for requirements in every state where you have nexus (a taxable presence).
- State sales tax: If your business collects sales tax, you must keep records of all sales and tax collected separately from income. This is a separate compliance obligation from income tax recordkeeping.
- Some states require businesses to retain records for longer periods than the federal minimum. California, for example, has its own statute of limitations rules. Confirm retention periods with your advisor if you operate in multiple states.

### Current-Year Caveats

- **Meals deduction:** Business meals are 50% deductible for 2026. Entertainment expenses (tickets to sporting events, concerts, etc.) are generally not deductible under current federal law, even if business is discussed. This rule reflects the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 as extended and modified; confirm whether any legislative changes have affected meal and entertainment deductions for your 2026 filing with your advisor.
- **Employer-provided meals:** Beginning in 2026, business expense deductions for certain employer-provided meals (meals excludable from employee income or qualifying as de minimis fringes) may be disallowed. If your business provides food to employees on premises, confirm the current rules with your advisor.
- **Standard mileage rate:** The 72.5 cents per mile rate applies to miles driven on or after January 1, 2026, per IRS Notice 2026-10. If you drove business miles in 2025 at a different rate, use the 2025 rate for those miles.
- **Bonus depreciation:** Equipment purchases may qualify for bonus depreciation or Section 179 expensing. The percentage for bonus depreciation is subject to phase-down schedules under current law; confirm the applicable percentage with your advisor before purchasing equipment and expecting to deduct it in full in the year of purchase.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Mixing personal and business finances.** Running personal expenses through a business account (or vice versa) creates a bookkeeping nightmare and can cause the IRS to challenge the legitimacy of all business deductions. Keep accounts strictly separate.

**2. Waiting until tax time to organize receipts.** Receipts become lost, faded, or forgotten. By the time you dig for them in April, you cannot remember the business purpose of a meal from 14 months ago. Capture receipts daily; close books monthly.

**3. Not recording the business purpose for meals and travel.** A receipt for a restaurant is not enough. IRS substantiation rules for meals and entertainment (IRC Section 274(d)) require a record of who was there, their business relationship, and what business was discussed. Without this note, the deduction can be denied even with a perfect receipt.

**4. Using the bank statement as the only record.** A bank statement proves a payment was made; it does not prove it was a business expense. You need both the statement AND the supporting documentation (receipt, invoice, contract) for deductibility.

**5. Forgetting mileage.** Vehicle deductions are consistently one of the most missed deductions for self-employed individuals. If you do not log trips at the time of the drive, you cannot reliably reconstruct them. Start the log on Day 1.

**6. Ignoring the $75 threshold in the wrong direction.** Some business owners apply the "under $75, no receipt needed" rule too broadly. It means you are not legally required to have a receipt; it does not mean you should not track the expense. Always record the amount, vendor, date, and business purpose even for small purchases.

**7. Not reconciling monthly.** Skipping reconciliation lets errors compound. A single unreconciled month can cascade into a year of inaccurate records.

**8. Deducting 100% of meals.** Meals are 50% deductible. If your bookkeeping software does not split the deduction automatically, your books will overstate deductions, leading to errors on your tax return.

**9. Missing estimated tax payments or underpaying.** Clean books let you estimate your tax liability accurately. Without monthly books, you are guessing -- and guessing wrong triggers penalties.

**10. Failing to collect Form W-9 from vendors before paying them.** If you pay a vendor $600 or more and cannot issue a 1099 because you never got their tax ID number, you may be liable for backup withholding or penalties. Collect W-9s before you pay.

## Advisor Review Gates

Contact your advisor before acting if any of the following apply:

1. **You are starting a new entity or changed entity type this year.** The bookkeeping structure and deductible expense categories differ by entity type, and your advisor must set up the chart of accounts correctly from the start.

2. **You want to switch bookkeeping methods (cash to accrual or vice versa).** Changing accounting methods typically requires IRS approval (Form 3115, Application for Change in Accounting Method). Do not change methods without advisor guidance.

3. **Your gross receipts exceeded $[confirm with your advisor the current threshold for mandatory accrual accounting] for the year.** You may be required to use accrual accounting.

4. **You are considering purchasing equipment or vehicles that you want to deduct in the current year.** Section 179 elections and bonus depreciation rules require specific steps and limitations; your advisor must confirm eligibility and the correct treatment before you file.

5. **You receive an IRS notice or audit letter.** Stop immediately. Do not respond on your own. Your records must be pulled and reviewed by your advisor before any response is sent.

6. **You discover a category of expenses you have been taking that you are not sure are deductible.** Do not assume; ask before filing.

7. **You missed an estimated tax payment or believe you significantly underpaid.** There may be options to minimize the penalty; your advisor can calculate this.

8. **You operate in multiple states.** State nexus, apportionment, and local tax obligations require state-specific advice that goes beyond this playbook.

9. **Your business had a net loss this year.** Loss deductibility at-risk rules, passive activity rules, and net operating loss (NOL) rules are complex and require advisor review before you claim a loss on your return.

## Using The Download(s)

### Receipt Management and Monthly Books Implementation Worksheet

**What it is:** A structured monthly worksheet you fill out to guide yourself through the closing process, track your receipt capture status, and record your mileage log totals and estimated tax calculations.

**Where in the workflow to use it:**

- **At setup (Month 1 only):** Use the "System Setup" section to check off each one-time setup step (Steps 1-7 above) and record your account numbers, bookkeeping tool choice, and chart of accounts.
- **Every month (Steps 8-15):** Work through the Monthly Closing Checklist section of the worksheet before considering the month closed. Mark each step done and note any exceptions (missing receipts, unresolved reconciliation items).
- **Every quarter:** Use the Quarterly Summary section to record your Profit and Loss totals by quarter, your estimated tax payment amount and confirmation number, and any year-to-date notes for your advisor.
- **At year-end (Steps 19-22):** Use the Year-End Checklist section to confirm all 12 months are closed, all 1099s are issued, and your year-end package is ready for your advisor.

**How to fill it in:** Each section has labeled fields and checkboxes. Fill in the date, your bookkeeping software name, and account totals as you work through the steps. Keep one worksheet per month/quarter, or use the annual version your advisor provides. Bring the completed worksheet to every advisor meeting.

## Completion Checklist

Work through this list before considering implementation complete.

### One-Time Setup
- [ ] Dedicated business bank account opened and no personal transactions running through it
- [ ] Dedicated business credit card in use (no personal charges)
- [ ] Bookkeeping tool selected and connected to bank and credit card accounts
- [ ] Chart of accounts confirmed with advisor
- [ ] Receipt capture method established (app, photo folder, or software feature)
- [ ] Mileage log started (paper, app, or spreadsheet)
- [ ] Form W-9 collection process established for new vendors before payment

### Monthly (Repeat Every Month)
- [ ] All transactions for the month imported or entered
- [ ] Every transaction categorized to the correct account
- [ ] Receipts attached (or documented) for all expenses of $75 or more and all lodging
- [ ] Business purpose notes added to all meals, travel, and gift transactions
- [ ] Bank and credit card accounts reconciled -- beginning and ending balances match statements
- [ ] Mileage log totaled and entered for the month
- [ ] Profit and Loss report reviewed
- [ ] Estimated quarterly tax position reviewed

### Quarterly
- [ ] Estimated tax payment made on time (see Timing and Deadlines)
- [ ] Payment confirmation number saved
- [ ] Quarterly Profit and Loss report run and filed
- [ ] New vendor W-9s collected
- [ ] Advisor quarterly check-in completed with updated books

### Year-End
- [ ] December books closed by January 10
- [ ] Full-year Profit and Loss report and Balance Sheet pulled and saved
- [ ] All mileage log entries totaled for the year
- [ ] All 1099-NEC forms issued to applicable recipients by February 1, 2027 (January 31, 2027 falls on Sunday; deadline shifts to next business day)
- [ ] Copies of all 1099s filed with IRS (or e-filed) by applicable deadline
- [ ] Year-end document package delivered to advisor
- [ ] Prior year's records moved to archive folder labeled with the tax year and retention expiration date

## Before You Act

Before you change your bookkeeping method, switch from one mileage calculation method to another, begin claiming a new category of expense, purchase assets you plan to depreciate or expense, or make any significant change to how you report your business income and expenses, confirm your specific facts with your advisor.

This playbook teaches you a compliant, audit-ready receipt management and monthly bookkeeping system. It does not replace the review of your specific situation, entity type, state obligations, and tax year by a qualified professional. An error in your books compounds across every month and every return it affects -- getting the system right from the start, with your advisor's input, is worth the investment.
