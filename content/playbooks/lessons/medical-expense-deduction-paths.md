# Medical Expense Deduction Paths

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

The federal tax code offers four distinct paths to deduct or recover medical expenses: (1) itemizing out-of-pocket costs on Schedule A above a 7.5%-of-income floor, (2) deducting health insurance premiums above the line as a self-employed person, (3) reimbursing medical costs through an employer-sponsored arrangement tax-free, and (4) using pre-tax accounts such as HSAs and FSAs. Most business owners can access more than one path simultaneously, but each path has its own eligibility rules, forms, and dollar limits. This playbook maps all four paths end-to-end so you can implement each one that fits your situation and avoid leaving deductions unclaimed.

## Who This Applies To

This playbook is for you if one or more of the following are true:

- You pay out-of-pocket medical, dental, or vision expenses that are not fully covered by insurance.
- You are self-employed (sole proprietor, single-member LLC, S-corporation shareholder owning more than 2% of shares, or partner in a partnership) and you pay your own health insurance premiums.
- You own or operate a business that could establish or already has a Health Reimbursement Arrangement (HRA) for employees, including yourself as an owner-employee.
- You want to understand whether itemizing medical expenses on Schedule A will save you more tax than taking the standard deduction.
- You pay long-term care insurance premiums and want to know how much is deductible.
- You are evaluating whether a Health FSA or HSA should change how you route your medical spending.

## When This Is Not A Fit

This playbook is NOT a fit -- or requires advisor sign-off before acting -- in these situations:

- **Your medical expenses are below the 7.5% AGI floor and you will take the standard deduction.** If you have, for example, $3,000 in unreimbursed expenses and an AGI of $80,000, your floor is $6,000 and you cannot deduct anything on Schedule A.
- **All your medical expenses are already reimbursed by insurance, an HRA, or an FSA.** Reimbursed amounts cannot also be deducted. Double-dipping is a common audit trigger.
- **You are an S-corporation shareholder owning more than 2% of shares and the health insurance premiums are NOT correctly reported in Box 1 of your W-2.** Without the W-2 reporting step, you lose the above-the-line deduction entirely. Fix the payroll setup before claiming the deduction.
- **You or your spouse were eligible for subsidized employer coverage (a group plan offered through an employer other than your own business).** Eligibility alone -- not just actual enrollment -- can eliminate the self-employed health insurance deduction for any month that subsidized coverage was available to you.
- **You are enrolled in Medicare.** You cannot contribute to an HSA after Medicare enrollment begins, though you may still deduct Medicare premiums as self-employed health insurance under specific rules.
- **You expect to take the standard deduction and have no self-employment income and no employer-sponsored medical reimbursement structure.** If all three apply, the tax savings from this playbook are likely zero until circumstances change.

## Key Terms

**Adjusted Gross Income (AGI):** Your total gross income minus specific above-the-line deductions. AGI appears on Line 11 of Form 1040. Your AGI is the denominator in the 7.5% Schedule A floor calculation; a lower AGI means a lower floor and a larger deductible amount.

**Above-the-Line Deduction:** A deduction subtracted from gross income before AGI is calculated. You get the benefit of an above-the-line deduction whether or not you itemize. Examples: the self-employed health insurance deduction (Schedule 1, Line 17) and HSA contributions (Schedule 1, Line 13).

**Schedule A (Form 1040):** The IRS form where you list itemized deductions, including qualified medical and dental expenses. You only benefit from filing Schedule A if your total itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction for your filing status.

**Itemized Deduction:** A specific expense listed on Schedule A instead of claiming the flat standard deduction. For medical expenses, only the amount above 7.5% of AGI is deductible.

**Standard Deduction (2026):** The flat amount the IRS allows if you choose not to itemize. For tax year 2026: $16,100 for single filers and married filing separately, $32,200 for married filing jointly and qualifying surviving spouses, $24,150 for heads of household.

**IRC Section 213(d):** The Internal Revenue Code section that defines what counts as a "medical expense" for both Schedule A and HSA withdrawals. Covered costs include diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease and any cost affecting a bodily structure or function.

**Qualified Medical Expense:** Any cost that meets the IRC Section 213(d) definition and is not otherwise reimbursed. Examples: doctor visits, hospital care, prescription drugs, insulin, dental treatment, vision care (glasses, contacts, exams), mental health services, hearing aids, wheelchair or crutches, addiction treatment, smoking-cessation programs, and long-term care services. Non-examples: cosmetic surgery (unless reconstructive following disease or injury), gym memberships, general vitamins, non-prescription drugs (except insulin), and controlled substances illegal under federal law.

**Self-Employed Health Insurance Deduction:** A 100% above-the-line deduction under IRC Section 162(l) for health, dental, vision, and qualified long-term care insurance premiums paid by a self-employed individual for themselves, their spouse, and dependents. Reported on Form 7206 and claimed on Schedule 1, Line 17. This deduction is limited to your net self-employment income or W-2 wages from your S-corporation; you cannot use it to create a loss.

**Health Reimbursement Arrangement (HRA):** An employer-funded account that reimburses employees for qualified medical expenses and, in some cases, health insurance premiums. The employer's contributions are deductible as a business expense; reimbursements are tax-free to the employee. The three main HRA types relevant to small business owners are: Integrated HRA (paired with group health insurance), Qualified Small Employer HRA (QSEHRA), and Individual Coverage HRA (ICHRA).

**Qualified Small Employer HRA (QSEHRA):** An HRA available to employers with fewer than 50 full-time equivalent employees who do not offer group health insurance to any employees. The employer sets an annual reimbursement cap within IRS limits. Employees use QSEHRA funds to pay for individual health insurance premiums and qualified out-of-pocket expenses, and the reimbursement is tax-free to the employee if they have minimum essential coverage.

**Individual Coverage HRA (ICHRA):** An HRA available to employers of any size. Employees must be enrolled in individual health coverage (not group coverage). There is no statutory dollar cap; the employer sets the limit. ICHRAs can be offered to different classes of employees at different benefit levels.

**Health Flexible Spending Account (FSA):** An employer-sponsored account funded by employee pre-tax salary reductions. The employee elects a contribution amount, which is set aside pre-tax and used to pay qualified medical expenses. Unlike an HSA, an FSA generally follows a "use it or lose it" rule each plan year (subject to a carryover exception). A general-purpose FSA disqualifies the employee from contributing to an HSA for the same period. A limited-purpose FSA covering only dental and vision may be paired with an HSA.

**Health Savings Account (HSA):** A tax-advantaged account paired with a High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP). Contributions are above-the-line deductions (or excluded from income if made through payroll), growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified expenses are tax-free. For detailed HSA rules, contribution limits, and HDHP thresholds, see the companion playbook "HSA / Medical Deduction Decision Tree."

**Long-Term Care (LTC) Insurance:** Insurance that covers nursing home, assisted living, in-home care, and related services. Premiums on a "qualified" LTC insurance contract are treated as medical expenses under IRC Section 213(d)(10) up to an age-based annual cap. Only premiums on a policy that meets the definition in IRC Section 7702B qualify.

**IRC Section 162(l):** The Internal Revenue Code section that authorizes the self-employed health insurance deduction. The deduction cannot exceed the taxpayer's earned income from the business for which the health plan is established.

**Form 7206:** The IRS form used by self-employed taxpayers (sole proprietors, partners, and S-corporation shareholders) to calculate the self-employed health insurance deduction. The result flows to Schedule 1 (Form 1040), Line 17.

**Form 1040, Schedule 1, Line 17:** Where the self-employed health insurance deduction is reported, reducing your AGI before Schedule A or any other itemized calculation.

**Schedule A, Line 1:** Where you enter total qualifying medical expenses before the AGI floor reduction. Line 4 shows the amount actually deductible after the 7.5% floor.

**Net Self-Employment Income:** Your profit from self-employment after deducting one-half of self-employment tax (Schedule SE). This is the ceiling on the self-employed health insurance deduction. If you are an S-corporation shareholder, the relevant ceiling is your W-2 wages from that S-corporation.

**W-2, Box 1:** Wages subject to federal income tax withholding. For greater-than-2% S-corporation shareholders, health insurance premiums paid by the S-corporation must be added to Box 1 wages. This creates the income that the Form 7206 deduction then offsets.

**Explanation of Benefits (EOB):** A document from your insurance company explaining what was billed, what the insurer paid, and what you owe. EOBs are your primary source documentation for unreimbursed medical expense amounts.

**Carryover (FSA):** FSA plans may allow up to $680 in unused 2026 funds to carry over to the 2027 plan year instead of being forfeited. Not all employers elect this option. Confirm with your plan documents.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather the following before starting the attached worksheet:

1. **Your 2026 AGI estimate.** Start with Line 11 of your most recent Form 1040. Adjust for expected income changes in 2026. Your AGI drives the 7.5% floor on Schedule A and the ceiling on the self-employed health insurance deduction.
2. **A complete list of all medical, dental, and vision expenses paid in 2026**, including dates paid, provider names, amounts, and whether each was reimbursed by insurance, an HRA, an HSA, or an FSA. Unreimbursed amounts are the only ones potentially deductible.
3. **Health insurance premium statements for 2026** showing total premiums paid for medical, dental, vision, and qualified long-term care coverage. If you are self-employed, separate the premiums you paid personally from any paid through an employer plan.
4. **Your business entity type** (sole proprietor/Schedule C, single-member LLC taxed as sole proprietor, S-corporation, C-corporation, partnership) and, if an S-corporation, confirmation of whether health insurance premiums are included in Box 1 of your Form W-2.
5. **Confirmation of whether your business currently offers an HRA** (QSEHRA, ICHRA, or integrated HRA) and, if so, the annual dollar limit per employee, the plan documents, and substantiation records for any reimbursements made.
6. **Your filing status** for 2026 (single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, head of household).
7. **Your age and your spouse's age** as of December 31, 2026, if you pay long-term care insurance premiums.
8. **State of residence.** Several states (Alabama, California, New Jersey, Wisconsin) do not conform to federal HSA treatment; other states have their own medical deduction rules. Your advisor must evaluate state-level impact separately.

## Required Documents And Records

**Keep all of the following for at least three years after the filing deadline for the year in question (five to seven years if a substantial understatement is at issue):**

- Receipts, invoices, or Explanation of Benefits (EOB) for every unreimbursed medical expense you intend to deduct on Schedule A. For each record, note: date, provider, service or item, total billed, insurance payment, and net amount you paid out of pocket.
- Insurance premium invoices or billing statements showing premiums paid in 2026 for medical, dental, vision, and long-term care coverage.
- Form W-2 showing Box 1 wages and, for S-corporation shareholders, confirmation that health insurance premiums are included.
- HRA plan document, employee election form, and substantiation receipts for every HRA reimbursement made or received in 2026.
- FSA election documents, year-end FSA account statement, and receipts for all FSA-reimbursed expenses.
- Mileage log if you drove to receive medical care (date, destination, medical purpose, and miles driven).
- Long-term care insurance policy or premium statement confirming the policy is a "qualified" policy under IRC Section 7702B.
- Schedule A workpaper showing your calculation of the 7.5% AGI floor and the deductible excess.
- Form 7206 (completed or draft) if you claimed the self-employed health insurance deduction.

**Retention guidance:** If you deduct a medical expense in 2026 that you later receive reimbursement for in 2027 (an insurance payment arrives after year-end), you may need to report the reimbursement as income in 2027. Keep records connecting the original deduction to the later reimbursement.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Work through the paths in the order that matches your situation. Most business owners should start with Path 2 (self-employed health insurance deduction) because it provides the largest benefit per dollar and requires no itemizing floor. Then evaluate Path 3 (HRA) if you have employees or an entity that can sponsor a plan. Then evaluate Paths 1 and 4.

---

### Path 1: Schedule A Itemized Medical Expense Deduction

This path applies to unreimbursed out-of-pocket expenses (copays, prescriptions, dental, vision, therapy, etc.) that you paid in 2026 with after-tax money and that were NOT already reimbursed by insurance, an HRA, an HSA, or an FSA.

**Step 1.1 -- Compile your list of unreimbursed qualified expenses.**

Gather all receipts and EOBs for the calendar year 2026. For each expense, confirm:
- It meets the IRC Section 213(d) definition (see Key Terms and The Numbers sections).
- It was paid in 2026 (cash-basis taxpayers deduct in the year paid, not billed).
- It was NOT reimbursed or paid by an HSA, FSA, or HRA. Any reimbursed amount must be excluded.

**Step 1.2 -- Separate expenses that are deductible elsewhere.**

Remove from your list:
- Health insurance premiums if you are deducting them as a self-employed health insurance deduction on Schedule 1 (Path 2 below). These premiums cannot also appear on Schedule A.
- Expenses paid from an HSA or FSA (already tax-free; listing them on Schedule A is double-dipping).

**Step 1.3 -- Calculate your 7.5% floor.**

Multiply your estimated 2026 AGI by 7.5% (or 0.075). Only medical expenses above this dollar amount can be deducted.

Example: AGI of $150,000. Floor = $150,000 x 0.075 = $11,250. If your qualifying expenses total $16,000, your deductible amount is $16,000 minus $11,250 = $4,750.

**Step 1.4 -- Determine whether itemizing beats the standard deduction.**

Add your Schedule A medical deduction to your other potential itemized deductions (mortgage interest, state and local taxes up to the $40,000 OBBB cap for 2026 -- confirm with your advisor as this cap may be subject to phase-in or phase-out adjustments, charitable contributions, etc.). Compare that total to the standard deduction for your filing status (see The Numbers section). If itemized deductions are higher, file Schedule A. If not, the standard deduction wins and Path 1 produces no current-year benefit.

**Step 1.5 -- Complete Schedule A.**

- Enter total qualifying medical expenses on Schedule A, Line 1.
- The IRS pre-prints the 7.5% calculation on Lines 2 and 3. Line 4 shows your deductible medical expense.
- Report the total itemized deductions on Form 1040, Line 12a and check the "itemized" box.

**Step 1.6 -- Do NOT deduct the same dollar twice.**

If you receive a reimbursement in 2027 for an expense you deducted on your 2026 Schedule A, you must report that reimbursement as taxable income in 2027 under the "tax benefit rule." Flag this for your advisor at the start of 2027.

### Path 2: Self-Employed Health Insurance Deduction (Above the Line)

This path applies if you are self-employed (sole proprietor, partner, or S-corporation shareholder owning more than 2% of shares) and you pay health, dental, vision, or qualifying long-term care insurance premiums for yourself, your spouse, or dependents.

**Step 2.1 -- Confirm eligibility for each month.**

You cannot take this deduction for any month during which you (or your spouse) were eligible to participate in a subsidized employer health plan through another employer. "Eligible" means the plan was available to you, whether or not you enrolled. If this applied to part of the year, you may only claim the deduction for months you were ineligible for subsidized coverage. Confirm month-by-month with your advisor if coverage changed during 2026.

**Step 2.2 -- Confirm your entity structure and payroll treatment.**

- **Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C):** The insurance policy can be in your name or your business's name. Your deduction equals the premiums paid. Your ceiling is your net profit from Schedule C (after the deduction for one-half of self-employment tax on Schedule SE).
- **Partners in a partnership:** Health insurance premiums must be paid by the partnership and treated as guaranteed payments, or you paid them yourself and were reimbursed. They appear as income on your Schedule K-1 and are then deducted via Form 7206.
- **S-corporation shareholders (more than 2%):** Your S-corporation must either (a) pay the premiums directly, or (b) reimburse you for premiums you paid, and in either case, the amount must be added to your Box 1 wages on Form W-2. The premium amount should NOT appear in Boxes 3 and 5 (Social Security and Medicare wages). Once reported in Box 1, you then claim the deduction on Form 7206 to offset the income that was added. If your W-2 does not include the premium in Box 1, you cannot claim the above-the-line deduction. Work with your payroll processor to add the correct amount to wages for the fourth quarter (or earlier) if this has not been done.

**Step 2.3 -- Calculate the deductible amount using Form 7206.**

Complete Form 7206. The form walks you through:
- Total premiums paid for qualifying coverage.
- Reduction for any self-employed health insurance deduction claimed in a prior year that affects this year's calculation (rare, applies to certain overlapping situations).
- The net self-employment income or S-corporation W-2 wage ceiling.
- The calculated deduction amount.

The result from Form 7206 flows to Schedule 1 (Form 1040), Line 17.

**Step 2.4 -- Understand the interaction with Schedule A.**

Your self-employed health insurance deduction (premiums) reduces your AGI on Schedule 1. A lower AGI raises the 7.5% floor for Schedule A. This interaction means every dollar you deduct via Path 2 slightly reduces the benefit of Path 1. The net result still favors claiming both whenever both paths apply, because the above-the-line deduction (Path 2) saves taxes at your full marginal rate with no floor, while Path 1 only captures excess above 7.5% of AGI.

**Step 2.5 -- Long-term care premiums under Path 2.**

If you pay premiums on a qualified long-term care insurance policy, those premiums are treated as health insurance premiums for the self-employed health insurance deduction, subject to the age-based annual cap in The Numbers section. Include only the deductible portion in your Form 7206 calculation.

### Path 3: Health Reimbursement Arrangement (HRA)

This path applies if your business can sponsor an HRA to reimburse employees (including yourself as an owner-employee, depending on entity type) for medical expenses and/or insurance premiums tax-free.

**Note on owner eligibility:**
- **C-corporation owner-employees** can participate in a company HRA on the same terms as any employee. This is the most favorable structure for owner medical expense reimbursement.
- **S-corporation shareholders owning more than 2%** are treated as self-employed for fringe benefit purposes. An HRA reimbursement flows to their Box 1 wages as income; it is then deductible via Form 7206 (Path 2). This is less tax-advantaged than C-corporation treatment but still preferable to after-tax spending.
- **Sole proprietors and partners** cannot participate in their own business's HRA as an employee; these structures do not create an employer-employee relationship for the owner.

**Step 3.1 -- Choose the right HRA type for your business.**

| HRA Type | Who Can Use It | Key Limits (2026) | Owner Eligible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| QSEHRA | Fewer than 50 full-time equivalent employees; no group health plan offered to any employee | $6,450 (self-only), $13,100 (family) per year | Only C-corp owners as employees |
| ICHRA | Any employer size; employee must have individual coverage | No statutory cap; employer sets limit | Only C-corp owners as employees |
| Integrated HRA | Any employer; employer also offers a group health plan | No separate cap; must be offered with group plan | Only C-corp owners as employees |

**Step 3.2 -- Establish the plan before the reimbursement period starts.**

An HRA must be established in writing before the period to which it applies. A QSEHRA requires written notice to all eligible employees at least 90 days before the beginning of the plan year (or 90 days before an employee becomes eligible, whichever is later). An ICHRA also requires advance notice. You cannot retroactively establish an HRA for expenses already incurred and paid.

**Step 3.3 -- Require substantiation before reimbursing.**

Before reimbursing any expense from the HRA, collect:
- A receipt, invoice, or EOB showing the date, provider, nature of service or item, and amount.
- Employee certification that the expense is a qualified medical expense and has not been reimbursed from any other source.

**Step 3.4 -- Reimburse and record.**

Process the reimbursement through payroll or a separate check. Record the amount in your payroll or accounting records. The employer's reimbursement is a deductible business expense (under IRC Sections 105 and 106). The employee (if eligible) receives it tax-free.

**Step 3.5 -- File required reports.**

For QSEHRA: report the annual reimbursement amount for each employee in Box 12 of Form W-2 using Code FF. This tells the employee and the IRS what QSEHRA funds were available, which affects the employee's Premium Tax Credit (PTC) calculation if they bought coverage on an ACA exchange.

---

### Path 4: Pre-Tax Accounts (FSA and HSA)

For HSA rules, HDHP requirements, contribution limits, and the step-by-step HSA implementation process, see the companion playbook "HSA / Medical Deduction Decision Tree." This path summary covers FSA-only situations and the interaction with Paths 1 and 2.

**Step 4.1 -- Elect the FSA amount during your employer's open enrollment.**

If your employer offers a Health FSA, elect the amount you expect to spend on qualified medical expenses in 2026, up to the $3,400 employee limit. Do NOT elect more than you expect to use, since unused amounts beyond the $680 carryover are forfeited.

**Step 4.2 -- Pay medical expenses through the FSA.**

Use your FSA debit card or submit claims with receipts. Expenses must be incurred during the plan year (and any grace period) to be reimbursable. Keep all receipts; your FSA administrator may require substantiation.

**Step 4.3 -- Exclude FSA-reimbursed expenses from Schedule A.**

Any expense paid by the FSA cannot be deducted on Schedule A. This is the same rule as HSA reimbursements. Only track unreimbursed amounts as potential Schedule A deductions.

**Step 4.4 -- Do not use an FSA and an HSA for general medical expenses simultaneously.**

A general-purpose Health FSA makes you ineligible to contribute to an HSA. If your employer offers a limited-purpose FSA (covering only dental and vision), you may contribute to both. Confirm the FSA type in your plan documents before contributing to an HSA.

**Recurring rhythm:**

- **Monthly:** Save receipts and EOBs for every medical expense paid. Note whether it was paid out of pocket, from an HSA, from an FSA, or reimbursed by an HRA or insurance. A single tracking spreadsheet (or the attached worksheet) prevents confusion at year-end.
- **Quarterly:** Estimate whether your out-of-pocket expenses are tracking toward exceeding the 7.5% AGI floor. If you are close, consider whether accelerating or deferring elective procedures before December 31 would push you over the threshold.
- **Year-end (October through December):** Decide whether to bunch medical procedures into one calendar year to clear the 7.5% floor. Review your FSA balance and incur or submit any remaining eligible expenses before the plan year closes. Confirm your S-corporation's W-2 will include health insurance premiums in Box 1.
- **Filing season (January through April 15):** Gather all EOBs, receipts, insurance premium statements, Form W-2, and FSA/HSA year-end statements. Complete Form 7206, Schedule A, and Form 8889 (HSA). Confirm your Schedule A itemized total against the standard deduction before filing.

## The Numbers

All figures below are for tax year 2026 unless otherwise noted. Each figure is verified against the canonical source cited.

### Schedule A Medical Expense Floor

- **7.5% of AGI** is the floor. Only the amount of qualifying medical expenses above this threshold is deductible on Schedule A.
- This threshold is permanent law (Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021, Public Law 116-260). It was not changed by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, 2025).
- Reported on Schedule A (Form 1040). Line 1 = total expenses. Line 4 = deductible amount after floor.

Source: IRS Topic No. 502; IRS Publication 502 (2025 edition); IRS newsroom OBBBA announcement (2026).

### Standard Deduction (2026) -- Itemizing only helps if your total exceeds these amounts.

| Filing Status | Standard Deduction |
|---|---|
| Single | $16,100 |
| Married Filing Jointly | $32,200 |
| Married Filing Separately | $16,100 |
| Head of Household | $24,150 |

Source: IRS Newsroom -- 2026 Inflation Adjustments (IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-61).

### Self-Employed Health Insurance Deduction

- **100% of premiums** for medical, dental, vision, and qualifying long-term care coverage are deductible, subject to the net self-employment income (or S-corporation W-2 wage) ceiling.
- No dollar cap on premiums themselves; the ceiling is your net earned income from the business.
- Reported on Form 7206, then Schedule 1 (Form 1040), Line 17.
- Cannot be claimed for any month the taxpayer (or spouse) was eligible for a subsidized employer group health plan.

Source: IRC Section 162(l); IRS Form 7206 and Instructions (2025); IRS S-Corporation Compensation and Medical Insurance Issues.

### QSEHRA Limits (2026)

| Coverage | Annual Reimbursement Cap |
|---|---|
| Self-only | $6,450 |
| Family | $13,100 |

These limits are indexed annually. The employer may set a limit below these caps.

Source: IRS Publication 15-B (2026), Employer's Tax Guide to Fringe Benefits.

### Health FSA Limit (2026)

- **$3,400** maximum employee salary reduction contribution per plan year.
- **$680** maximum carryover to the 2027 plan year (if the employer elects the carryover option).

Source: IRS Newsroom -- 2026 Inflation Adjustments; IRS Publication 15-B (2026).

### Medical Mileage Rate (2026)

- **20.5 cents per mile** for travel to and from qualifying medical care.
- Keep a mileage log with: date, origin and destination, medical purpose, and miles.
- Parking fees and tolls paid for medical transportation are deductible separately at actual cost.

Source: IRS Notice 2026-10.

### Lodging for Out-of-Town Medical Care

- Up to **$50 per night, per person** (up to $100 per night for a patient plus one accompanying caregiver) for lodging away from home that is primarily for medical care.
- The care must be provided by a licensed medical professional at a medical facility. Meals are not deductible.

Source: IRS Publication 502 (2025 edition), IRC Section 213(d)(2).

### Long-Term Care Insurance Premium Caps (2026) -- Per insured person, per year.

The amount you may include as a medical expense for long-term care insurance premiums is capped by the insured person's age at year-end:

| Age at December 31, 2026 | Maximum Deductible Premium |
|---|---|
| 40 or younger | $500 |
| 41 through 50 | $930 |
| 51 through 60 | $1,860 |
| 61 through 70 | $4,960 |
| 71 or older | $6,200 |

If married, you calculate a separate cap for each spouse based on each spouse's individual age. The policy must be a "qualified" long-term care insurance contract under IRC Section 7702B.

Source: IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32, Section 4.27 (limits for taxable years beginning in 2026); confirmed via IRS Internal Revenue Bulletin 2025-45.

### HSA Contribution Limits and HDHP Thresholds (2026)

See the companion playbook "HSA / Medical Deduction Decision Tree" for the full HSA table. Summary:
- HSA self-only: $4,400; family: $8,750; age-55+ catch-up: $1,000 additional.
- HDHP minimum deductible: $1,700 (self-only), $3,400 (family).
- HDHP out-of-pocket maximum: $8,500 (self-only), $17,000 (family).

Source: IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-19.

### Penalties for Misuse

| Situation | Consequence |
|---|---|
| Deducting an expense on Schedule A that was also reimbursed (double-dipping) | Understatement of income; potential audit, accuracy-related penalty (20% of underpayment), and back taxes plus interest |
| HRA reimbursement without substantiation | Amount may be treated as taxable wages; payroll tax deficiency |
| S-corp health insurance NOT in Box 1 of W-2 and deducted anyway | Deduction disallowed; deficiency assessment |

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**C-corporation owners** have the most favorable path for medical expenses. The C-corporation can establish an HRA or provide group health insurance with no dollar cap related to the owner's wages, and the owner-employee receives all benefits tax-free as an employee. The corporation deducts costs as a business expense.

**S-corporation shareholders owning more than 2%** cannot receive most fringe benefits tax-free. Health insurance premiums paid by the S-corporation are added to Box 1 wages, creating income that is then offset by the Form 7206 deduction. Net result: no Social Security or Medicare (FICA) tax on the premiums, and federal income tax is a wash (added to income, then deducted). The benefit versus paying premiums out of pocket is that the S-corp route avoids the self-employment tax equivalent (FICA on wages), which can represent 15.3% savings on the premium dollars. A QSEHRA or ICHRA reimbursement to a greater-than-2% shareholder also flows through Box 1 wages.

**Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs (Schedule C):** Premiums are deductible above the line via Form 7206. You cannot create or benefit from your own HRA as an employee. Access to pre-tax FSA or HRA benefits requires employing a spouse under a legitimate employment arrangement with proper wages and a bona fide job, which your advisor must evaluate separately.

**Partners in a partnership:** Similar to sole proprietors for premium deductions. The partnership must either pay the premiums or reimburse the partner, and the amounts are treated as guaranteed payments (ordinary income to the partner), then offset by the Form 7206 deduction.

**Payroll timing for S-corporations:** The health insurance premium must be included in Box 1 wages before you file your tax return. If it was omitted, a corrected W-2 (W-2c) may be needed. Work with your payroll provider before January 31, 2027.

**State tax conformity:** States set their own rules for medical deductions. A non-exhaustive list of common variations:
- Several states have their own AGI floor (some higher than 7.5%, some lower).
- California, New Jersey, Alabama, and Wisconsin do not conform to federal HSA treatment.
- Some states do not allow QSEHRA reimbursements to be excluded from state income.

Your advisor must review your specific state before you assume the federal treatment carries through.

**One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), effective 2026:** The OBBBA did not change the 7.5% AGI floor, the definition of qualified medical expenses, or the long-term care premium caps. It did expand HSA eligibility (bronze and catastrophic ACA plans, Direct Primary Care arrangements, permanent telehealth safe harbor). The OBBBA introduced a new limitation on itemized deductions for taxpayers in the 37% bracket (a formulaic reduction of 2/37 of the lesser of deductions or excess taxable income above the top bracket); this can slightly reduce the Schedule A benefit for very high earners. Confirm the impact with your advisor if your taxable income exceeds the 37% bracket threshold.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Trying to deduct on Schedule A expenses that were already paid by an HSA, FSA, or HRA.** Any expense reimbursed from a tax-favored account is not deductible again. Run your expense log through the attached worksheet's reimbursement filter before totaling Schedule A.

**2. Including health insurance premiums on Schedule A when they are already deducted on Schedule 1 via Form 7206.** You cannot deduct the same premiums both above the line and on Schedule A. The premiums should go to Form 7206 first; only unreimbursed out-of-pocket costs (copays, prescriptions, etc.) go to Schedule A.

**3. Forgetting to run the standard deduction comparison.** Even with $15,000 in qualifying medical expenses, Schedule A may not help if your total itemized deductions are below your standard deduction. Always run the comparison in Step 1.4 before filing Schedule A.

**4. Failing to add S-corporation health insurance premiums to Box 1 wages before deducting them.** The deduction on Form 7206 is only available if the premiums were included in your W-2 income first. Deducting without the Box 1 addition is an error the IRS will catch.

**5. Establishing a QSEHRA after the plan year has started without advance notice.** A QSEHRA cannot reimburse expenses incurred before the plan was established. The 90-day advance notice requirement is not optional.

**6. Reimbursing expenses through an HRA without keeping substantiation records.** Unsubstantiated HRA reimbursements are treated as taxable compensation. For each reimbursement, keep the substantiation receipt before issuing payment.

**7. Deducting cosmetic procedures.** Elective cosmetic surgery is not a qualified medical expense unless it corrects a deformity from a congenital abnormality, accident, or disfiguring disease. Deducting it is a common audit trigger.

**8. Failing to apply the long-term care premium age cap.** You can deduct LTC premiums as a medical expense, but only up to the age-based annual cap. Deducting the full premium if it exceeds the cap overstates your medical expense deduction.

**9. Ignoring state conformity for HRA reimbursements.** Some states include QSEHRA or ICHRA reimbursements in state taxable income even though they are federally tax-free. Your advisor needs to check the state rules for your state before you establish or receive HRA benefits.

**10. Not timing elective medical procedures to bunch deductions.** The 7.5% AGI floor is a high hurdle. If you expect to be close to the floor, shifting an elective dental procedure, elective surgery, or vision expense from January 2027 into December 2026 can push your total over the threshold and unlock a deduction. Conversely, if you cannot clear the floor either year, spreading expenses across years costs nothing. Your advisor can model this before you schedule procedures.

**11. Forgetting that a mid-year insurance reimbursement received in 2027 for a 2026 Schedule A deduction becomes taxable income.** Track whether any 2026 deducted expenses were later reimbursed. If yes, report the reimbursement as income in the year received, up to the tax benefit you received.

## Advisor Review Gates

Review these situations with your advisor BEFORE acting:

1. **You are an S-corporation owner and health insurance premiums are NOT currently in Box 1 of your W-2.** You need a corrected payroll and W-2 setup before the above-the-line deduction is available. Do not file Form 7206 using premiums that were not properly included in wages.

2. **You want to establish a QSEHRA or ICHRA.** These plans require a formal written plan document, timely employee notices, and ongoing substantiation procedures. An error in setup eliminates the tax-free status of all reimbursements. Have your advisor or a benefits attorney review the plan document before the first reimbursement.

3. **You are considering bunching medical expenses into 2026 to clear the 7.5% AGI floor.** Your advisor can model whether the additional after-tax benefit of the deduction justifies accelerating spending, and whether 2026 or 2027 is the better year to bunch based on income projections.

4. **You or your spouse were eligible for employer-sponsored health insurance at any point in 2026.** Eligibility (not enrollment) for a subsidized group plan can eliminate the self-employed health insurance deduction for that month. Your advisor must evaluate the month-by-month facts.

5. **You pay long-term care insurance premiums.** Confirm the policy is a "qualified" contract under IRC Section 7702B and verify which age-based cap applies. Deducting above the cap or for a non-qualified policy is an error.

6. **Your taxable income exceeds the 37% federal income tax bracket threshold.** The OBBBA's new formulaic itemized deduction limitation may reduce your Schedule A benefit. Your advisor should calculate the net deductible amount after the limitation.

7. **You have employees and want to offer QSEHRA benefits to some but not others.** QSEHRA must generally be offered on the same terms to all eligible employees (with limited permitted exclusions). Selective benefit offerings can disqualify the entire arrangement.

8. **You received a 2026 HRA or FSA reimbursement and also want to deduct the same expense on Schedule A.** Bring both records to your advisor to confirm there is no overlap before filing.

9. **You deducted medical expenses on a prior year's Schedule A and then received an insurance reimbursement in 2026.** You may have a tax benefit rule inclusion to report for 2026. Confirm the amount and treatment with your advisor.
