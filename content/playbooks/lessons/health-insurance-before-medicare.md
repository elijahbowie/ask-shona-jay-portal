# Health Insurance Before Medicare

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Self-employed business owners who retire or step back from a day job before age 65 face a coverage gap: Medicare does not begin until age 65, but employer-sponsored health insurance ends when employment ends. This playbook shows you how to bridge that gap using two tax-advantaged tools: (1) the self-employed health insurance deduction under IRC Section 162(l), which lets you deduct 100% of qualifying premiums directly from your gross income, and (2) a Health Savings Account (HSA) paired with a qualifying high-deductible health plan, which lets you contribute pre-tax dollars and withdraw them tax-free for medical costs. Together these strategies can substantially reduce the out-of-pocket cost of coverage during the years before Medicare eligibility.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is most valuable for:

- **Self-employed individuals with net profit:** Sole proprietors filing Schedule C, farmers filing Schedule F, and partners in a partnership with net earnings from self-employment who are paying for health insurance out of pocket and do not have access to a subsidized employer health plan through a spouse or another job.
- **S-corporation owner-employees who own more than 2% of the stock:** You can deduct premiums the corporation pays on your behalf (reported on your W-2 as wages) using the self-employed health insurance deduction, subject to the rules in the S-Corp section below.
- **Business owners who retired early (before age 65):** Anyone who left a salaried position before Medicare eligibility who now runs a self-employed or pass-through business and needs to cover health insurance privately.
- **Owners with sufficient earned income:** The deduction cannot exceed your net profit (or W-2 wages, for S-corp shareholders) from the business under which the plan is established. You need enough business income to absorb the deduction.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Eligible for a subsidized employer plan:** If you or your spouse is eligible -- even if not actually enrolled -- in a subsidized health plan offered by an employer, you cannot claim the self-employed health insurance deduction for any month you were eligible for that plan. Eligibility, not enrollment, is the disqualifier.
- **No net profit:** If your self-employed business shows a net loss or zero profit, the earned income cap reduces your deduction to zero for that year. The deduction cannot create or increase a loss.
- **Medicare-enrolled individuals for certain months:** Once you are enrolled in Medicare (Parts A, B, C, or D), you cannot include premiums for those Medicare-covered months in calculating your deduction if you are also contributing to an HSA during those months. (You may still deduct Medicare premiums under IRC 162(l); the HSA contribution restriction is a separate rule addressed below.)
- **Sole proprietors and partners who want to use an accountable plan for health insurance:** Health insurance for self-employed individuals is NOT reimbursed under an accountable plan. The self-employed health insurance deduction on Schedule 1 is the correct mechanism. Do not conflate these two strategies.
- **C-corporation employees (non-owner):** A C-corp employee whose employer pays or reimburses health insurance premiums through an employer group health plan uses a different structure. The self-employed health insurance deduction under IRC 162(l) applies only to those who are self-employed within the meaning of IRC Section 401(c).

## Key Terms

**Self-employed health insurance deduction:** A deduction under IRC Section 162(l) that allows self-employed individuals to deduct 100% of the premiums they paid for qualifying health, dental, vision, and long-term care insurance from their gross income, reported on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), Line 17. This is an above-the-line deduction, meaning it reduces Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) and is available whether or not you itemize deductions.

**Above-the-line deduction:** A deduction subtracted from gross income to arrive at Adjusted Gross Income (AGI). These deductions are more valuable than itemized deductions because they reduce AGI, which in turn can reduce phase-outs, thresholds, and taxes that depend on AGI (such as the 7.5%-of-AGI floor for itemized medical deductions).

**Adjusted Gross Income (AGI):** Your total income from all sources minus certain specific "above-the-line" deductions. AGI is the number on the bottom of page 1 of Form 1040 and is the starting point for many other tax calculations.

**IRC Section 162(l):** The Internal Revenue Code provision that authorizes the self-employed health insurance deduction. "IRC" stands for Internal Revenue Code, the body of federal tax law.

**Earned income limitation:** The rule under IRC 162(l)(2)(A) that limits the self-employed health insurance deduction to the amount of your net profit (for Schedule C/F filers) or W-2 wages from the S-corporation (for S-corp shareholders) from the trade or business under which the insurance plan is established. The deduction cannot exceed that earned income amount.

**Form 7206:** The IRS form used to calculate the self-employed health insurance deduction. The result flows to Schedule 1 (Form 1040), Line 17. Partners and S-corp shareholders also use this form.

**Schedule 1 (Form 1040):** An attachment to Form 1040, the individual federal income tax return, that lists certain income and deductions not shown on the main form. The self-employed health insurance deduction appears on Schedule 1, Line 17.

**Schedule C:** The IRS form sole proprietors use to report business income and deductions. Net profit from Schedule C is the "earned income" the self-employed health insurance deduction is capped at, for sole proprietors.

**Schedule F:** The IRS form farmers use to report farm income and expenses. Net profit from Schedule F functions the same way as Schedule C net profit for this deduction.

**Plan "established under your business":** The IRS requirement that the health insurance plan must be set up (established) in connection with your self-employment activity. For sole proprietors and Schedule F farmers, the policy can be in the name of the business or in the individual's name. For partners, the partnership must pay or reimburse the premium and report it as a guaranteed payment (or the partnership pays it directly). For S-corp shareholders, the S-corporation must either pay the premium directly or reimburse the shareholder and include the amount as wages in Box 1 of Form W-2.

**S-corporation (S-corp):** A corporation that has elected to pass its income, deductions, and credits through to its shareholders for federal tax purposes. S-corp shareholders pay income tax on their share of S-corp income on their personal returns.

**More-than-2% S-corp shareholder:** Any person who directly or indirectly owns more than 2% of an S-corp's stock at any time during the year. These individuals are treated similarly to partners for fringe benefit purposes, which means the S-corp must include health insurance premiums in their W-2 Box 1 wages in order for the shareholder to then claim the self-employed health insurance deduction.

**High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP):** A health plan with a minimum annual deductible and a maximum annual out-of-pocket limit as defined by the IRS each year. HDHPs typically have lower premiums than traditional health plans. For 2026, an HDHP must have a minimum annual deductible of at least $1,700 (self-only) or $3,400 (family) and out-of-pocket maximums not exceeding $8,500 (self-only) or $17,000 (family), per IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-19.

**Health Savings Account (HSA):** A tax-advantaged savings account available only to people enrolled in a qualifying HDHP. Contributions are tax-deductible (or pre-tax if made through payroll), the money grows tax-free, and withdrawals used for qualified medical expenses are also tax-free -- a triple tax benefit. For 2026, the annual contribution limit is $4,400 for self-only HDHP coverage and $8,750 for family HDHP coverage. Individuals age 55 or older may make an additional $1,000 catch-up contribution. Source: IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-19.

**Qualified medical expenses (HSA):** Medical costs that the IRS allows you to pay with HSA funds tax-free. This includes most out-of-pocket medical, dental, and vision costs -- deductibles, co-pays, prescription drugs, glasses, dental work, and many others. After age 65, you may withdraw HSA funds for any purpose; if used for non-medical expenses before age 65, the withdrawal is taxable and subject to a 20% penalty.

**COBRA:** The Consolidated Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act, a federal law requiring most employer-sponsored group health plans to offer continuation coverage for a limited period after a qualifying event (such as leaving a job). COBRA allows you to keep the same employer health plan, but you pay the full premium (up to 102% of the plan's cost, including the employer's share plus a 2% administrative fee). COBRA continuation coverage typically lasts 18 months, or up to 29 months for certain disabled beneficiaries. Note on the disability extension: for months 19 through 29 of a disability-based extension, the plan may charge up to 150% of the plan's cost (not the standard 102%), so premiums rise noticeably during that period. Source: 29 U.S.C. Section 1162(3); DOL COBRA guidance.

**ACA Marketplace:** The Health Insurance Marketplace created by the Affordable Care Act (ACA), where individuals and families can shop for and enroll in health insurance plans. Marketplace plans are sold in four metal tiers (Bronze, Silver, Gold, Platinum) plus Catastrophic plans for eligible individuals. Coverage purchased on the ACA Marketplace can qualify as the "plan" for the self-employed health insurance deduction.

**Premium Tax Credit (PTC):** A federal tax credit under IRC Section 36B that helps eligible individuals and families afford ACA Marketplace health insurance. For 2026, the enhanced subsidies that were available from 2021-2025 under the American Rescue Plan and Inflation Reduction Act have expired (they were not extended by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025). As a result, the standard income eligibility rule applies for 2026: households with income between 100% and 400% of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL) may qualify for the PTC. Households above 400% FPL do not qualify under the standard rules. Confirm current eligibility with your advisor, as legislation may change.

**Federal Poverty Level (FPL):** A measure of income published annually by the Department of Health and Human Services. ACA premium tax credit eligibility is based on your household income as a percentage of the FPL for your family size. For 2026 coverage, eligibility is based on the 2025 FPL tables (the most recently published guidelines as of the 2026 Open Enrollment Period start date).

**Medicare:** The federal health insurance program primarily for people age 65 and older, as well as certain younger individuals with disabilities. Medicare has four main parts: Part A (hospital insurance), Part B (medical insurance), Part C (Medicare Advantage plans, an alternative to Original Medicare), and Part D (prescription drug coverage). Most people become eligible for Medicare at age 65. Most people receive Part A premium-free (because they or their spouse worked 40 or more quarters and paid Medicare taxes). Those who did not earn 40 quarters may buy into Part A: the 2026 full monthly premium is $565 (fewer than 30 quarters of coverage) or $311 (30-39 quarters of coverage). Source: CMS 2026 Parts A and B Premiums and Deductibles Fact Sheet.

**Medicare Part B premium:** The monthly premium you pay for Medicare Part B (medical insurance). The standard Part B premium for 2026 is $202.90 per month, per the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS). Higher-income beneficiaries pay an Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount (IRMAA) surcharge on top of the standard premium.

**IRMAA (Income-Related Monthly Adjustment Amount):** An additional monthly surcharge added to the Medicare Part B (and Part D) premium for beneficiaries whose modified adjusted gross income exceeds certain thresholds. For 2026, the IRMAA surcharge applies to individuals with MAGI above $109,000 (single filers) or $218,000 (joint filers), based on 2024 MAGI. Total Part B premiums with IRMAA range from $284.10 to $689.90 per month for 2026. Source: CMS 2026 Medicare Parts A and B Premiums and Deductibles Fact Sheet.

**Qualified long-term care insurance:** Insurance that covers nursing home care, home health care, or other long-term custodial or skilled care. Premiums for qualifying long-term care insurance can be included in the self-employed health insurance deduction, but only up to age-based limits set by the IRS each year.

**Guaranteed payment:** In a partnership, a payment to a partner for services that is determined without regard to the partnership's income. Health insurance premiums the partnership pays for a partner are typically reported as guaranteed payments on Schedule K-1.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before starting:

1. **Your entity type and tax filing status:** Are you a sole proprietor (Schedule C), farmer (Schedule F), partner in a partnership, or more-than-2% S-corp shareholder? The mechanics differ by entity.
2. **2026 net profit or W-2 wages from your business:** Your deduction is capped at net profit (for Schedule C/F/partnership filers) or W-2 wages from the S-corp (for S-corp shareholders). You need a projection or actual figure.
3. **Health insurance premiums paid for the year:** Total monthly premiums you paid or the S-corp paid on your behalf. Separate out any dental, vision, or long-term care premiums.
4. **Whether you are eligible for any subsidized employer plan:** For every month of 2026, were you eligible -- even if you declined -- to enroll in a health plan subsidized by an employer (yours or your spouse's)? Eligibility for any month disqualifies that month.
5. **Whether you received a Premium Tax Credit (PTC) for Marketplace coverage:** If you claimed or will claim a PTC, the deduction is reduced by the PTC amount attributable to the same premiums.
6. **Your Medicare enrollment status:** If you enrolled in any part of Medicare during 2026, the self-employed health insurance deduction still applies to Medicare premiums, but you cannot contribute to an HSA for any month in which you are enrolled in Medicare.
7. **Whether you are enrolled in a qualifying HDHP:** If you have a high-deductible health plan and want to contribute to an HSA, confirm the plan meets the 2026 HDHP deductible and out-of-pocket thresholds (see The Numbers section).
8. **Your age:** Age 55 or older allows an additional $1,000 catch-up HSA contribution. Age at year-end determines which long-term care insurance premium limit applies.
9. **Coverage period for each family member:** The deduction covers premiums for yourself, your spouse, dependents, and children under age 27 at year-end, even if they are not your tax dependent.
10. **State of residence:** Several states do not conform to the federal self-employed health insurance deduction or have separate rules. Confirm your state's treatment with your advisor.

## Required Documents And Records

### To claim the deduction

- **Monthly premium statements or invoices:** Documentation showing the amount of each monthly premium paid. For plans in your own name, keep your billing statements and proof of payment (bank statements, canceled checks, credit card statements).
- **Policy information:** The name of the insurance company, plan name, policy or group number, and the names and dates of birth of all covered individuals.
- **Form W-2 from your S-corporation (S-corp shareholders only):** Box 1 must include the health insurance premiums the S-corp paid or reimbursed. Keep a copy of your W-2. If premiums are not in Box 1, the S-corp must issue a corrected W-2 before you can claim the deduction.
- **Schedule K-1 (Form 1065) (partners only):** Health insurance premiums the partnership pays on your behalf are reported as a guaranteed payment. Confirm the amount matches what you paid or was paid on your behalf.
- **Form 1095-A (ACA Marketplace coverage):** If you purchased coverage through a Marketplace, you will receive Form 1095-A from the Marketplace by early February of the following year. You need this form to reconcile any Premium Tax Credit on Form 8962. Keep it with your tax records.
- **Completed Form 7206:** The form you use to calculate the deduction. Retain the completed form with your tax return records.

### For HSA contributions

- **Form 5498-SA:** Issued by your HSA trustee/custodian each May, confirming the total contributions made to your HSA during the year. Keep this form even though you do not file it.
- **Form 1099-SA:** Issued by your HSA trustee if you made withdrawals from your HSA. Required to prepare Form 8889.
- **HDHP documentation:** Evidence that your health plan qualifies as an HDHP for the months you contributed to your HSA. A copy of your insurance card and the Summary of Benefits and Coverage (SBC) showing the plan's deductible is sufficient.
- **Qualified medical expense receipts:** Keep receipts and explanations of benefits (EOBs) for every expense you pay from HSA funds. Retain these for as long as the statute of limitations may apply -- generally 3 years from the date you file the return for the year of the withdrawal, but keep indefinitely if you expect to use the same records to substantiate future HSA withdrawals.

### Retention period

Keep all of the above records for a minimum of 3 years from the date you file the tax return to which they relate (the standard statute of limitations under IRC Section 6501). For HSA records, retain receipts and EOBs indefinitely if you may claim reimbursement in a future year for expenses incurred now.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

### Phase 1: Confirm eligibility and choose coverage (do before coverage begins)

**Step 1 -- Confirm your deduction eligibility with your advisor.**
Before enrolling in any plan, confirm with your advisor: (a) your entity type and that a plan can be "established under your business," (b) your expected net profit or W-2 wages, and (c) whether you are eligible for any subsidized employer plan during any month of the year. Do not skip this step; the disqualification rules are month-by-month.

**Step 2 -- Decide on your coverage approach.**
You have several options for coverage before Medicare:

- **ACA Marketplace plan:** Enroll during Open Enrollment (November 1 through January 15 for coverage starting February 1, or through December 15 for coverage starting January 1). If you are losing employer-sponsored coverage, you have a Special Enrollment Period of 60 days from the date you lose coverage to enroll outside of Open Enrollment. Premiums are deductible under IRC 162(l) if the plan is established under your business.
- **COBRA continuation coverage:** Elect within 60 days of losing employer coverage. Lasts up to 18 months. You pay up to 102% of the full plan cost (both your old share and the employer's share, plus 2% administration fee). COBRA premiums may also be deductible, but the deductibility of COBRA specifically under IRC 162(l) depends on whether the plan can be considered "established under" your self-employed business. Confirm with your advisor; some practitioners treat COBRA as an itemized medical deduction only.
- **Private off-exchange individual plan:** Plans purchased directly from an insurance company outside the Marketplace. These may qualify for the self-employed health insurance deduction if the plan is established under your business.
- **Spouse's employer plan:** If your spouse is eligible for employer-sponsored coverage that covers you, you generally cannot claim the self-employed health insurance deduction for the months you are eligible for that plan, even if you pay the premiums yourself.

**Step 3 -- Determine whether you want an HDHP paired with an HSA.**
If you are generally healthy and want the combination of lower premiums plus tax-free savings, compare HDHP options. To open and contribute to an HSA:
- You must be enrolled in a qualifying HDHP (meeting the 2026 minimums: $1,700 deductible for self-only, $3,400 for family).
- You must not be enrolled in Medicare.
- You must not be claimed as a dependent on someone else's return.
- You must not have a general-purpose Flexible Spending Account (FSA) (or your spouse must not have one covering you).

Starting in 2026 (per the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, Public Law 119-21), Bronze and Catastrophic plans offered through the ACA Marketplace are also considered HSA-compatible, even if they do not technically meet the standard HDHP minimum deductible definition. This expands your options. Source: IRS Notice 2026-5.

**Step 4 -- For S-corp shareholders: set up the premium reporting correctly.**
The S-corporation must either:
- Pay the premiums directly to the insurance company and include the premium amounts in your W-2 Box 1 wages (but not in Boxes 3 and 5 -- the amounts are not subject to FICA taxes when paid under a plan covering a class of employees), OR
- Reimburse you for premiums you paid and include those amounts in your W-2 Box 1.

If Box 1 of your W-2 does not include the premiums, you cannot claim the self-employed health insurance deduction for those premiums. Work with your payroll provider to get this right before year-end.

**Step 5 -- For partners: confirm the guaranteed payment structure.**
If your partnership pays health insurance premiums on your behalf, confirm those amounts are reported as guaranteed payments on your Schedule K-1 (Form 1065), Box 4. If you paid the premiums personally, the partnership must reimburse you and report the reimbursement as a guaranteed payment for the deduction to work.

**Step 6 -- Open an HSA (if using an HDHP).**
Open an HSA with a bank, credit union, or investment custodian that offers HSA accounts. You can open one any time you are eligible. Contributions can be made up to the tax filing deadline (April 15, 2027 for the 2026 tax year, plus extensions) and still count for 2026.

### Phase 2: Fund the HSA and track premiums through the year

**Step 7 -- Contribute to your HSA throughout the year.**
You can contribute all at once or on any schedule. The 2026 annual limits are $4,400 (self-only HDHP) or $8,750 (family HDHP). Add $1,000 if you are age 55 or older by December 31, 2026. You can contribute the full annual limit even if you were only eligible for part of the year, but there is a "last-month rule" that requires you to remain eligible for the full following year -- confirm timing with your advisor.

**Step 8 -- Track all health insurance premiums paid each month.**
Keep a running log of premiums paid (or paid by the S-corp on your behalf). Note the month, amount, and name of who is covered. Flag any months in which you were eligible for a subsidized employer plan; those months must be excluded.

**Step 9 -- Track Medicare enrollment month-by-month (for those turning 65 during the year).**
If you turn 65 during 2026, you may become eligible for or enroll in Medicare. You can continue to deduct health insurance premiums under IRC 162(l) for the months before Medicare enrollment. However, once you enroll in Medicare, you cannot contribute to your HSA for any month in which you are enrolled in Medicare. You may still use existing HSA funds tax-free for qualified expenses.

**Step 10 -- Use HSA funds for qualified medical expenses as needed.**
Pay qualifying out-of-pocket medical, dental, vision, and prescription costs from your HSA. Keep every receipt or explanation of benefits (EOB). You can also pay now out of pocket and reimburse yourself from the HSA later -- there is no deadline for reimbursement, as long as the expense was incurred after the HSA was opened.

### Phase 3: Calculate and claim the deductions at tax time

**Step 11 -- Calculate the self-employed health insurance deduction using Form 7206.**
Complete Form 7206 to calculate the deduction. The form asks for:
- Total premiums paid for the year (medical, dental, vision, and qualified long-term care, subject to age-based limits for long-term care)
- Net profit from your business (Schedule C, Line 31; Schedule F, Line 34; or Schedule K-1, Box 14, Code A for partners) or W-2 wages from your S-corp
- Whether you received a Premium Tax Credit (which reduces the deductible amount)
- Months of Medicare enrollment (excluded from the calculation)

The deduction cannot exceed your earned income from the business where the plan is established.

**Step 12 -- Report the deduction on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), Line 17.**
Transfer the amount from Form 7206 to Schedule 1, Line 17. This reduces your AGI.

**Step 13 -- Complete Form 8889 for your HSA.**
Report all HSA contributions and distributions on Form 8889, which flows to your Form 1040. Deductible HSA contributions appear on Schedule 1, Line 13. Report distributions on the same form; distributions for qualified medical expenses are tax-free.

**Step 14 -- Reconcile the ACA Premium Tax Credit if applicable (Form 8962).**
If you enrolled in a Marketplace plan and received advance premium tax credits, use Form 1095-A (from the Marketplace) and Form 8962 to reconcile what you received in advance versus what you were eligible for based on your actual income. The self-employed health insurance deduction and the premium tax credit interact: see IRS Publication 974 for the calculation.

**Step 15 -- Confirm state treatment.**
Check whether your state allows the same deduction. Most states conform to federal treatment, but some do not. Your advisor or state Department of Revenue website can confirm.

**Recurring rhythm:**

- **Monthly:** Pay premiums on time and save your billing statement. If using an HSA, make your monthly contribution and retain receipts for any HSA distributions.
- **Mid-year (if S-corp):** Verify with your payroll provider that the correct premium amounts are accumulating in Box 1 of your W-2 for the year.
- **October/November:** Review your income projection for the year to estimate the deduction amount and to decide whether to adjust HSA contributions before year-end.
- **November 1 -- January 15:** ACA Open Enrollment window for 2026 coverage. Compare current plan against new plan options; update enrollment if needed. Note: CMS finalized a rule (2025 Marketplace Integrity and Affordability Final Rule) that shortens the federal exchange Open Enrollment Period for 2027 coverage and beyond, ending it on December 15 (no February 1 start date). Starting fall 2026, the enrollment window for 2027 coverage will end December 15; confirm the applicable dates with HealthCare.gov or your state's exchange each fall.
- **January (new year):** Confirm the new year's HDHP deductible and HSA limits from IRS publications. Adjust contribution amounts.
- **January 31:** Receive W-2 from S-corp; confirm Box 1 includes health insurance premiums.
- **Early February:** Receive Form 1095-A if enrolled in a Marketplace plan.
- **April 15:** Tax filing deadline; also the last day to make prior-year HSA contributions.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed July 4, 2025, did not extend them. For 2026, only the standard ACA premium tax credit rules under IRC Section 36B apply. If you are above 400% FPL, you do not qualify for a PTC under the standard rules. Confirm with your advisor whether any subsequent legislation has changed this.

**Note on Medicare Part A premium:** Most people who worked 40 or more quarters under Social Security receive Part A premium-free. Those who did not earn 40 quarters may buy into Part A: the 2026 full premium is $565/month for individuals with fewer than 30 quarters of coverage, and the 2026 reduced premium is $311/month for individuals with 30-39 quarters of coverage. Source: CMS 2026 Parts A and B Premiums and Deductibles Fact Sheet. Confirm your specific premium history with Social Security or Medicare.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

### Entity-specific caveats

**Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs taxed as sole proprietors:** The plan must be in the name of the business or in the individual's name. You claim the deduction on Form 7206 and Schedule 1. You cannot use an accountable plan to reimburse yourself for health insurance; the Form 7206 route is the correct mechanism.

**Partners in a partnership:** Health insurance premiums paid by the partnership on your behalf must be reported as guaranteed payments on Schedule K-1, Box 4 (other guaranteed payments), or Box 14, Code A (net earnings from self-employment). If you paid the premiums yourself, the partnership must reimburse you and report the amount as a guaranteed payment. The plan must be established under the partnership. Confirm the partnership's reporting with your tax preparer before filing.

**More-than-2% S-corp shareholders:** The S-corp must include health insurance premiums in Box 1 of your W-2. The amounts are NOT subject to Social Security or Medicare (FICA) taxes. If the S-corp fails to include premiums in Box 1, the IRS may disallow the self-employed health insurance deduction. Work with your payroll provider early in the year to ensure this is set up correctly; do not wait until December.

**Single-owner S-corps where the owner's spouse is also employed:** If the spouse (who is employed by the S-corp on a bona fide basis) is covered by a health plan provided by the S-corp as an employee benefit, the IRS may treat the 2% shareholder-spouse as eligible for an employer plan, disqualifying the self-employed health insurance deduction. Get specific guidance from your advisor.

### Payroll caveats (S-corp shareholders)

- Include premiums in Box 1 of W-2 but not in Boxes 3 and 5 (FICA boxes) -- confirm this with your payroll provider.
- The W-2 must be issued by January 31 of the following year.
- If premiums were paid monthly and added to payroll throughout the year, confirm that the annual W-2 amount matches the total premiums paid or reimbursed.

### State caveats

- Most states conform to the federal self-employed health insurance deduction under IRC 162(l), but a small number do not. Verify with your advisor or your state's Department of Revenue.
- HSA deductibility also varies by state. California and New Jersey, for example, do not recognize HSAs for state income tax purposes -- contributions are not deductible and earnings are not tax-free at the state level.
- If you moved between states during the year, your state tax treatment for each period may differ.
- If you enrolled in a Marketplace plan and your state operates its own state-based exchange (rather than using HealthCare.gov), confirm any state-specific enrollment deadlines.

### Current-year caveats (2026)

- **ACA enhanced subsidies expired:** As noted in The Numbers section, the American Rescue Plan / Inflation Reduction Act enhanced premium tax credits expired December 31, 2025, and the OBBBA did not extend them. For 2026, the 400% FPL ceiling on PTC eligibility is back in effect under standard law. This may significantly increase premiums for self-employed individuals with higher incomes who relied on enhanced subsidies.
- **OBBBA HSA expansions (effective 2026):** Bronze and Catastrophic ACA Marketplace plans are now HSA-compatible starting January 1, 2026. Direct Primary Care (DPC) arrangement fees can now be paid from an HSA. These are expansions that may benefit clients who could not previously use an HDHP/HSA combination.
- **Premium Tax Credit and self-employed deduction interaction:** If you receive an ACA Premium Tax Credit and also claim the self-employed health insurance deduction, the two interact: you cannot double-count. See IRS Publication 974 for the iterative calculation. This is complex and should be confirmed with your advisor.
- **Long-term care premium limits:** The limits in this playbook are from the 2025 Form 7206 instructions. The IRS publishes updated amounts annually; confirm the 2026 limits when they are published.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Claiming the deduction despite eligibility for a subsidized employer plan:** The deduction is disallowed for any month you were eligible for a subsidized health plan through an employer (yours or your spouse's), even if you didn't enroll. This is the single most common error. Review eligibility month by month.

2. **S-corp failing to include premiums in Box 1 of W-2:** If the premiums are not in Box 1, the IRS may disallow the deduction. Do not assume your payroll service handles this automatically -- confirm with them at the start of each year.

3. **Deducting more than net profit:** The deduction cannot exceed the net profit (or S-corp W-2 wages) from the business where the plan is established. Claiming the full premium when profit is less will result in the excess being disallowed.

4. **Contributing to an HSA while enrolled in Medicare:** Once you are enrolled in any part of Medicare, you cannot make HSA contributions for that month. If you enroll in Medicare mid-year, contributions must stop the month Medicare begins. Excess contributions carry a 6% penalty.

5. **Not establishing the plan "under the business" for S-corp shareholders:** Paying premiums personally and not running them through the S-corp payroll at all breaks the "established under your business" requirement. The S-corp must pay or reimburse and include in W-2 Box 1.

6. **Ignoring the Premium Tax Credit reconciliation:** If you received advance PTC payments from the Marketplace and your actual income was higher than expected, you may owe back part of the credit on Form 8962 -- with no repayment cap for 2026 under the OBBBA. Estimate income carefully and inform the Marketplace of material income changes during the year.

7. **Missing COBRA or Special Enrollment deadlines:** Both COBRA election and ACA Special Enrollment Period windows are 60 days and are not extended for lateness. Mark the date you lose coverage on your calendar and act immediately.

8. **Deducting COBRA premiums under 162(l) without confirmation:** Whether COBRA premiums qualify for the above-the-line self-employed health insurance deduction (rather than only the itemized medical deduction on Schedule A) depends on whether the plan can be considered "established under" your self-employed business. The IRS position is not clearly settled for all fact patterns. Get your advisor's guidance before claiming the above-the-line deduction for COBRA premiums.

9. **Not tracking which months are eligible and which are not:** If you changed jobs, had a period of employer-sponsored coverage, or had months of Medicare enrollment, you must exclude those months from the premium deduction calculation. A month-by-month log prevents errors.

10. **Using HSA funds for non-qualifying expenses before age 65:** If you withdraw HSA funds for anything other than qualified medical expenses before age 65, you owe income tax on the withdrawal plus a 20% penalty. Build a separate emergency fund rather than relying on your HSA for non-medical costs.

## Advisor Review Gates

Do not proceed through the following decisions without first confirming with your advisor:

1. **Before enrolling in a plan:** Confirm (a) whether you are eligible for any subsidized employer plan for any month, (b) whether the plan you are considering can be "established under your business" for the deduction, and (c) whether your expected net profit is sufficient to absorb the deduction.

2. **S-corp shareholders before the first payroll of the year:** Confirm that your payroll provider will include health insurance premiums in Box 1 (and not Boxes 3 and 5) of your W-2, and that the correct monthly amounts will be reported. Do not wait until year-end to discover a payroll error.

3. **Before opening an HSA:** Confirm that your chosen plan qualifies as an HDHP for 2026, that you are not enrolled in Medicare, and that you do not have a disqualifying general-purpose FSA.

4. **If you turn 65 during 2026:** Confirm the month your Medicare coverage begins and stop HSA contributions from that month forward. Confirm how to calculate the pro-rated HSA contribution limit and the pro-rated health insurance deduction for the pre-Medicare months.

5. **If you have a Marketplace plan and received advance Premium Tax Credits:** Have your advisor calculate the interaction between the PTC and the self-employed health insurance deduction under IRS Publication 974 before finalizing your return. Errors here are common and can be costly.

6. **If you pay COBRA premiums:** Confirm with your advisor whether those premiums qualify for the above-the-line deduction under IRC 162(l) in your specific situation, or whether they are limited to the itemized medical deduction (subject to the 7.5% AGI floor).

7. **State tax treatment:** Confirm whether your state conforms to the federal deduction and whether your state recognizes HSAs.

8. **Before claiming the last-month HSA rule:** If you were eligible for an HDHP only for part of the year but want to contribute the full annual HSA limit, confirm the "last-month rule" (which allows this but requires you to remain eligible for the full following year) with your advisor before contributing the maximum.

## Using The Download(s)

**Download: Health Insurance Before Medicare Implementation Worksheet**

Use this worksheet at the following points in the process:

- **Steps 1-3 (Eligibility and Coverage Decision):** The worksheet contains a month-by-month eligibility grid. For each month of the year, record whether you were eligible for a subsidized employer plan, your Medicare status, the plan type (HDHP or not), and the premium paid. This grid is the foundation for calculating the deductible amount and confirming HSA eligibility each month.

- **Step 7 (HSA Contributions):** The worksheet includes a running HSA contribution tracker. Enter each contribution as you make it and confirm you stay within the annual limit ($4,400 self-only or $8,750 family, plus $1,000 catch-up if age 55+). The tracker flags if you approach the limit.

- **Step 8 (Premium Log):** Record each month's premium in the premium log section, noting the insurer, plan name, amount, and covered individuals. This becomes your source document for Form 7206.

- **Step 10 (HSA Expense Log):** Record every qualified medical expense you pay from your HSA: date, provider, amount, and brief description. Attach or reference the receipt. This log is your audit file.

- **Step 11 (Form 7206 Calculation):** The worksheet walks you through the Form 7206 inputs: total premiums paid, long-term care limits by age, net profit or S-corp wages, and any PTC received. Complete this section before you or your tax preparer fill out the actual Form 7206.

- **Annual review:** Use the worksheet each year to confirm updated HDHP and HSA limits, compare plan options during Open Enrollment, and recalculate the expected deduction based on your income projection.

Keep completed worksheets in your tax file for the applicable year. Retain for at least 3 years after you file the return, and keep HSA expense records indefinitely.

## Before You Act

Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before:

- Enrolling in any health insurance plan and claiming it will qualify for the deduction
- Setting up the S-corp payroll treatment for health insurance premiums
- Opening or contributing to an HSA
- Claiming the self-employed health insurance deduction on your return
- Electing COBRA or a Special Enrollment Period plan under the assumption that those premiums qualify as an above-the-line deduction
- Making HSA contributions in a year when you may be eligible for or enrolled in Medicare

The strategy described in this playbook is well-established under federal tax law, but the eligibility rules are detailed and your specific situation -- entity type, income level, coverage options, spouse's employer benefits, state of residence, and age -- determines whether and how much of the strategy applies to you.
