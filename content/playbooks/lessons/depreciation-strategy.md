# Depreciation Strategy

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

## Overview

Depreciation is the tax code's way of letting your business recover the cost of long-lived assets -- equipment, vehicles, buildings, furniture, and more -- by deducting a portion of that cost each year over the asset's designated useful life. Two acceleration tools, the Section 179 deduction and bonus depreciation (also called the "additional first-year depreciation deduction"), allow you to deduct a large share of an asset's cost in the very first year you place it in service, rather than spreading the deduction over many years. Used intentionally, depreciation strategy can produce significant current-year deductions, reduce taxable income, and improve cash flow -- without any additional out-of-pocket spending beyond what you were already planning.

## Who This Applies To

This strategy is most valuable for:

- **Business owners purchasing or financing equipment, machinery, vehicles, furniture, or technology** for use in their trade or business in 2026.
- **Real estate investors and business property owners** who own commercial or residential rental property and have not yet had a cost segregation study performed.
- **S-corporation, C-corporation, partnership, and sole proprietorship owners** who place qualifying assets in service during the tax year and want to accelerate deductions into the current year.
- **Businesses that have recently expanded, upgraded equipment, or made tenant improvements** to commercial space they own or lease.
- **Owners who previously under-depreciated assets** and want to correct the error and claim a catch-up deduction through a change in accounting method.

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **Businesses with little or no taxable income in the current year:** Accelerating deductions when there is no income to offset them may produce a net operating loss (NOL) or suspended deduction that carries forward but provides no immediate cash benefit. The income limitation on Section 179 (explained in Key Terms) is a hard stop.
- **Businesses expecting much higher income in future years:** If your taxable income will be substantially higher next year, spreading the deduction over multiple years -- or waiting until next year to purchase -- may produce a greater tax benefit at a higher effective rate.
- **Assets primarily used for personal purposes:** Only the business-use percentage of an asset's cost can be depreciated. If a vehicle or computer is used less than 50 percent for business, listed property rules apply and both Section 179 and bonus depreciation are unavailable for that asset.
- **Intangible assets (goodwill, customer lists, non-compete agreements):** These are amortized over a fixed 15-year period under IRC Section 197, not depreciated under MACRS or expensed under Section 179.
- **Land:** Land is never depreciable. Only the building and improvements on land qualify.
- **Inventory held for sale:** Stock in trade is not a depreciable asset.
- **Assets placed in service and then quickly disposed of:** Disposing of a Section 179 or bonus-depreciation asset before the end of its MACRS recovery period can trigger recapture of previously deducted amounts as ordinary income.
- **Businesses subject to Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) with certain asset types:** Confirm AMT exposure with your advisor before making large first-year elections.

## Key Terms

**Depreciation:** The process of deducting the cost of a business asset over its useful life, as allowed by the tax code. It represents the "using up" of the asset's value in producing income.

**MACRS (Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System):** The standard depreciation system in the United States for assets placed in service after 1986. MACRS assigns each asset to a property class with a fixed recovery period (number of years) and uses an accelerated depreciation method (such as 200% declining balance) that front-loads larger deductions in the early years of an asset's life. Governed by IRC Section 168.

**Recovery period:** The number of years over which an asset is depreciated under MACRS. Common periods: 3 years, 5 years, 7 years, 15 years, 27.5 years, and 39 years.

**GDS (General Depreciation System):** The primary MACRS system most taxpayers use. GDS uses accelerated methods (200% or 150% declining balance) and the recovery periods prescribed by the tax code.

**ADS (Alternative Depreciation System):** A slower MACRS method that uses straight-line depreciation and often longer recovery periods. ADS is required for certain tax-exempt property, vehicles used 50 percent or less for business, and listed property not meeting the 50-percent business-use test. Taxpayers may also elect ADS voluntarily (for example, to produce more even deductions).

**Placed in service:** The date an asset is ready and available for use in your trade or business. This date -- not the date you ordered or paid for the asset -- triggers the start of depreciation and determines which tax year's rules apply.

**Section 179 deduction:** A provision of IRC Section 179 that allows a business to elect to deduct the full cost of qualifying property in the year it is placed in service, up to the annual dollar limit, instead of depreciating it over its recovery period. The deduction is limited to the business's taxable income from active business operations for the year.

**Business income limitation (Section 179):** Section 179 deductions cannot exceed the business owner's taxable income from the active conduct of their trade or business for the year. Any amount disallowed by this limit is carried forward to future years.

**Bonus depreciation (additional first-year depreciation deduction):** Under IRC Section 168(k), an additional first-year depreciation allowance on top of regular MACRS depreciation. As of 2026, bonus depreciation is 100 percent for qualifying property that was both acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025 (a permanent change made by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act). Property acquired on or before January 19, 2025 and placed in service in 2026 follows the prior TCJA phase-down schedule and qualifies for only 20% bonus depreciation (see The Numbers section). Unlike Section 179, bonus depreciation is NOT limited to business taxable income and can produce a net operating loss.

**One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA):** Federal legislation signed July 4, 2025 (Public Law 119-21) that, among other things, made the 100-percent bonus depreciation allowance under Section 168(k) permanent for qualifying property both acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025 (OBBBA Section 70301).

**Qualified property (for bonus depreciation):** Depreciable tangible property with a MACRS recovery period of 20 years or less, certain film and television productions, certain computer software, and qualified improvement property. The original-use requirement was expanded by TCJA (2017) to include used property that is new to the taxpayer (first time the taxpayer uses or acquires it), subject to certain related-party exclusions.

**Qualified improvement property (QIP):** Improvements made to the interior of a nonresidential building after the building was first placed in service, other than structural components, elevators, escalators, or the internal structural framework. QIP has a 15-year MACRS recovery period under GDS and therefore qualifies for bonus depreciation.

**Section 179 real property:** The following types of improvements to nonresidential real property qualify for Section 179 (even though real property generally does not): roofs, HVAC systems, fire protection and alarm systems, security systems, and qualified improvement property. These must be placed in service on or after the date the building was first placed in service by any taxpayer.

**Listed property:** Certain types of assets that the IRS monitors closely because of their potential for personal use: passenger automobiles (vehicles weighing 6,000 pounds or less), other transportation vehicles, computers not used only at a regular business establishment, and cellular phones. Listed property used 50 percent or less for business must be depreciated under ADS (straight-line, longer period) and cannot use Section 179 or bonus depreciation.

**50-percent business-use test:** The threshold that applies to listed property. If business use exceeds 50 percent, the property can use Section 179 and bonus depreciation. If business use is 50 percent or below, listed property rules restrict depreciation to ADS straight-line.

**Half-year convention:** The default rule under MACRS that treats all personal property as placed in service at the midpoint of the tax year, regardless of the actual date. This means you deduct a half year of depreciation in both the first and last years of the recovery period.

**Mid-quarter convention:** If more than 40 percent of your total depreciable personal property is placed in service during the last three months of the tax year, the half-year convention is replaced by the mid-quarter convention, which treats each quarter's additions as placed in service at the midpoint of that quarter. This reduces first-year deductions for property placed in service in Q4.

**Mid-month convention:** Applies only to real property (buildings). Real property is treated as placed in service at the midpoint of the month in which it was actually placed in service.

**200% declining balance method:** The accelerated depreciation method used for 3-year, 5-year, 7-year, and 10-year MACRS property under GDS. The annual deduction equals two times the straight-line rate applied to the remaining book value of the asset. The method switches to straight-line when straight-line produces a larger deduction.

**150% declining balance method:** The accelerated depreciation method used for 15-year and 20-year MACRS property under GDS. The annual deduction equals 1.5 times the straight-line rate applied to the remaining book value.

**Straight-line depreciation:** A method that deducts an equal amount each year over the recovery period. Required for real property (27.5-year residential, 39-year nonresidential) and for ADS property.

**Cost segregation study:** An engineering and tax analysis of a building or improvement that identifies components that can be reclassified from 39-year (nonresidential) or 27.5-year (residential) real property to shorter-lived personal property classes (5-year, 7-year, 15-year), allowing significantly faster depreciation. The IRS has published a Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide (IRS Publication 5653) governing these studies.

**Section 481(a) adjustment:** When you change a depreciation method (including correcting prior under-depreciation through a Form 3115 filing), you compute the cumulative difference between depreciation you actually claimed and depreciation you were allowed to claim. This difference is reported as a Section 481(a) adjustment, typically deducted in the year of the change (if a negative -- catch-up -- adjustment).

**Form 4562:** IRS Form 4562, Depreciation and Amortization. The form used to claim MACRS depreciation, make the Section 179 election, claim bonus depreciation, and disclose listed property information. Filed with your business tax return each year you place new assets in service or have continuing depreciation from prior years.

**Form 3115:** IRS Form 3115, Application for Change in Accounting Method. Filed to change a depreciation method or to correct prior depreciation errors (including claiming missed bonus depreciation or cost segregation reclassifications). Allows a catch-up deduction in a single year for all prior under-depreciation.

**Depreciation recapture:** If you sell a depreciable business asset for more than its adjusted tax basis (cost minus accumulated depreciation), the portion of the gain attributable to prior depreciation is "recaptured" and taxed as ordinary income (for personal property under IRC Section 1245) or potentially at a 25-percent rate (for real property under IRC Section 1250). Depreciation recapture is not a reason to avoid depreciation -- it only arises if you sell at a gain -- but it should factor into exit planning.

**NOL (Net Operating Loss):** When allowable deductions (including large bonus depreciation claims) exceed income in a tax year, the result is a net operating loss. NOLs generally carry forward indefinitely and can offset up to 80 percent of taxable income in future years under current law.

**Section 280F:** The IRC provision that caps depreciation deductions on passenger automobiles, regardless of their cost, to protect against abuse. Annual caps are set by IRS revenue procedure and adjusted for inflation.

**SUV (Sport Utility Vehicle) limitation:** A separate sub-limit within Section 179 that caps the deduction on SUVs with a gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR) between 6,001 and 14,000 pounds. Vehicles over 6,000 pounds GVWR are not passenger automobiles for Section 280F purposes but are subject to this separate Section 179 cap.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these facts before beginning:

1. **Complete asset list:** Every piece of equipment, machinery, furniture, technology, vehicle, and real property improvement placed in service or planned to be placed in service in the current tax year. Include: asset description, purchase price, date placed in service, and vendor or seller name.
2. **Entity type and tax return:** What type of entity is your business? (S-corp, C-corp, partnership, sole proprietorship, LLC with what tax election?) Which form does your business file? (1120-S, 1120, 1065, Schedule C?)
3. **Projected taxable income for the year:** An estimate of business taxable income before depreciation elections. This determines how much Section 179 you can use.
4. **Vehicle weight ratings:** For any vehicle you are depreciating, the GVWR (gross vehicle weight rating) from the vehicle's door sticker or manufacturer specification sheet. This determines whether Section 280F caps or the SUV limitation applies.
5. **Business-use percentages:** For any asset with mixed personal and business use (vehicle, computer, phone), the estimated percentage of actual use that is for business. You need a log or usage records to support this.
6. **Prior-year depreciation records:** Copies of Form 4562 from your prior tax returns, and a depreciation schedule showing every asset, its cost, accumulated depreciation, and remaining book value.
7. **Real property details:** For any building or commercial improvement, the original placed-in-service date, total cost, land value, and whether a cost segregation study has ever been performed.
8. **State of domicile and operating states:** Many states do not conform to federal Section 179 limits or bonus depreciation. Confirm state-level treatment with your advisor before assuming the federal deduction flows through to your state return.

## Required Documents And Records

**Gather before filing:**

- Purchase invoices, closing statements, or receipts for every depreciable asset, showing purchase price, seller, and date.
- Vehicle title, registration, or purchase agreement for all vehicles, plus the GVWR from the door sticker.
- Mileage log for every business vehicle (date, destination, business purpose, and miles -- required by IRC Section 274(d)).
- Written depreciation schedule from your bookkeeping or accounting software showing each asset's cost, prior accumulated depreciation, and current-year depreciation election.
- Completed Form 4562, prepared by your tax preparer and signed by you as part of your business return.
- Form 3115 (if changing a depreciation method or making a catch-up correction) and the related Section 481(a) computation worksheet.
- Cost segregation study report (if applicable), signed by the engineering firm, noting the methodology and IRS guidance relied upon.

**Keep after filing (retention guidance):**

- Asset records, purchase invoices, and depreciation schedules: Retain for at least 3 years after the asset is sold or fully depreciated, whichever is later, and for at least 7 years if there is any chance of audit (the 6-year lookback applies when the IRS alleges a 25-percent income understatement). Because recapture is determined by the entire depreciation history, many advisors recommend keeping depreciation records permanently for real property.
- Mileage logs and vehicle records: Retain for at least 3 years after the return due date for the year covered.
- Form 4562 copies: Retain with the corresponding year's tax return, minimum 7 years.
- Cost segregation study: Retain permanently (it supports reclassifications that affect every future tax year for that building).
- Form 3115 and Section 481(a) worksheets: Retain at least 7 years.

## Step-By-Step Implementation

**Before you start:** Pull your prior-year Form 4562 and depreciation schedule (or ask your bookkeeper or advisor for them). You cannot complete this process without knowing what you have already claimed.

---

**Step 1: Compile your asset list for the current tax year.**

List every depreciable business asset placed in service in 2026 (or that you plan to place in service before December 31, 2026). For each asset, record:
- Description and model/serial number
- Total cost (including delivery, installation, and any necessary setup costs, but not financing interest)
- Date placed in service (the day it was ready and available for business use)
- GVWR (for vehicles)
- Business-use percentage (for assets with mixed use)

Use the "Asset Inventory" tab of the Depreciation Resource Guide download to organize this list.

---

**Step 2: Classify each asset into its MACRS property class.**

Using the property-class reference table in the Depreciation Resource Guide, assign each asset a GDS recovery period:

| Recovery Period | Common Examples |
|---|---|
| 3-year | Racehorses, certain manufacturing tools |
| 5-year | Computers, office machinery, vehicles (cars, light trucks), appliances used in residential rental property |
| 7-year | Office furniture, desks, file cabinets, most equipment not assigned elsewhere |
| 10-year | Certain water transportation, fruit/nut-bearing trees and vines |
| 15-year | Land improvements (fences, sidewalks, parking lots), qualified improvement property (QIP) to nonresidential buildings |
| 27.5-year | Residential rental buildings (structures only; land excluded) |
| 39-year | Nonresidential commercial buildings and structural components (land excluded) |

Source: IRS Publication 946 (2025), Appendix B (Table of Class Lives and Recovery Periods).

For most equipment and technology purchases, the answer is 5-year or 7-year property. When in doubt, confirm with your advisor.

---

**Step 3: Identify assets that qualify for bonus depreciation or Section 179.**

An asset qualifies for 100% bonus depreciation if ALL of the following are true:
- It has a MACRS recovery period of 20 years or less (personal property classes 3- through 15-year, plus QIP).
- It was both acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025 (OBBBA Section 70301 requires both conditions for the permanent 100% rate; property acquired on or before January 19, 2025 but placed in service in 2026 follows the pre-OBBBA TCJA phase-down schedule and qualifies for only 20% -- see The Numbers section).
- It is the taxpayer's first use of the property (used property acquired for the first time by your business qualifies, subject to related-party rules).
- The property is used more than 50 percent for business.

An asset qualifies for Section 179 if:
- It is tangible personal property used in a trade or business, OR
- It is qualified Section 179 real property (roofs, HVAC, fire/alarm systems, security systems on nonresidential buildings, or QIP), AND
- It is used more than 50 percent for business.
- Section 179 is also limited to your business taxable income for the year (Step 4).

Land, inventory, property used outside the U.S., and property held for investment do NOT qualify.

---

**Step 4: Estimate your taxable income before depreciation elections and apply the Section 179 business-income limit.**

Ask your advisor or bookkeeper to provide an estimate of your business taxable income before any Section 179 or bonus depreciation deductions. This figure caps your Section 179 deduction. Bonus depreciation has no income cap.

Example: If your projected net income before depreciation is $80,000, your Section 179 deduction is capped at $80,000. Any excess Section 179 beyond that cap carries forward to next year.

---

**Step 5: Decide between Section 179, bonus depreciation, or regular MACRS for each asset.**

Your advisor will help optimize the mix, but here is the logic:

- **Use Section 179 first** for assets you want fully deducted this year when you have sufficient taxable income. Section 179 reduces taxable income dollar for dollar.
- **Use bonus depreciation** for remaining qualifying assets, or when Section 179 is capped by the income limitation, or when creating an NOL is acceptable or beneficial. Bonus depreciation applies automatically unless you elect out.
- **Use regular MACRS** for assets where you want to spread deductions over future years (for example, to avoid recapture risk on short-held assets or to plan for a higher-income year ahead).

You may use all three methods on different assets in the same year.

To elect out of bonus depreciation for a particular property class, your tax preparer must attach a written statement to your timely filed return (including extensions) stating the class of property for which the election is being made. The election to opt out of bonus depreciation must be made on a class-by-class basis, not asset by asset.

---

**Step 6: Address vehicles separately.**

Vehicles require additional steps because of listed-property and Section 280F caps.

- **Vehicles weighing 6,000 pounds GVWR or less (passenger automobiles):** Capped by Section 280F annual limits regardless of cost (see The Numbers section for 2026 figures). Even 100% bonus depreciation cannot push your first-year deduction above the cap.
- **Vehicles weighing more than 6,000 pounds GVWR and 14,000 pounds or less (heavy SUVs):** Not subject to Section 280F caps, but Section 179 is capped at $32,000 for 2026. Bonus depreciation is not subject to the $32,000 SUV cap.
- **Vehicles weighing more than 14,000 pounds GVWR (heavy trucks, large cargo vans):** Not subject to either Section 280F or the SUV cap. May qualify for full bonus depreciation.

For any vehicle, maintain a mileage log. The IRS consistently audits vehicle deductions.

---

**Step 7: For real property, consider a cost segregation study.**

If you own a commercial building (or have made significant leasehold improvements) and have never had a cost segregation study, ask your advisor whether one is warranted. A cost segregation study identifies personal property components buried inside a building's cost -- flooring, electrical, plumbing, cabinetry, specialty lighting, parking areas -- that can be reclassified to 5-year, 7-year, or 15-year property. Those reclassified components are then eligible for bonus depreciation.

A study typically pays for itself on buildings or improvements costing $500,000 or more. The IRS audits cost segregation studies; only use a qualified engineering firm that follows the IRS Cost Segregation Audit Techniques Guide (IRS Publication 5653, updated February 2025).

If the building was placed in service in a prior year and no study was done, a retroactive study is possible. You would file Form 3115 to implement the reclassifications and claim a catch-up deduction in the year of change.

---

**Step 8: Correct any prior under-depreciation.**

If your prior tax returns did not claim the full depreciation you were allowed (for example, bonus depreciation was missed, or an incorrect recovery period was used), you may be able to claim all missed depreciation in a single year by filing Form 3115. This creates a negative Section 481(a) adjustment that flows through to your tax return as a deduction.

This is one of the highest-return steps for business owners who have owned equipment or real property for several years. Confirm with your advisor whether prior returns are candidates for this correction.

---

**Step 9: Complete Form 4562 and file with your business return.**

Your tax preparer will prepare Form 4562. The form has three main sections you should understand:

- **Part I (Section 179):** Lines 1-12. Your election, the dollar limit, the phase-out calculation (if total qualifying purchases exceed $4,090,000), the income limitation, and the final deduction.
- **Part II (Bonus Depreciation):** Line 14. Reports the total additional first-year depreciation for all qualifying assets.
- **Part III (MACRS Depreciation):** Lines 17-22. Reports regular MACRS depreciation for all assets by property class.
- **Part V (Listed Property):** Section V covers vehicles and other listed property, including the business-use percentage for each vehicle and whether the 50-percent test is met.

Form 4562 is attached to your federal business tax return. If your business is a partnership or S-corp, the depreciation flows to partners or shareholders through Schedule K-1.

---

**Step 10: Track assets going forward.**

After the return is filed, update your depreciation schedule (or confirm your bookkeeper has done so) with:
- Every new asset added this year, its cost, recovery period, method, and depreciation claimed
- The remaining book value of every prior-year asset
- Any assets sold or disposed of, and the resulting recapture calculation

Use the "Depreciation Schedule Tracker" tab of the Depreciation Resource Guide to maintain this record year to year.

## Timing And Deadlines

**Year-end purchase deadline:** An asset must be placed in service (ready and available for business use) on or before December 31 of the tax year to generate any depreciation deduction for that year. Ordering and paying for equipment in December but not receiving and installing it until January means zero deduction for the current year. Coordinate purchases early enough to allow for delivery and setup.

**Mid-quarter convention watch:** If more than 40 percent of your total depreciable personal property purchases for the year are placed in service in Q4 (October through December), the mid-quarter convention replaces the half-year convention, reducing Q4 asset deductions significantly. Review the timing of planned purchases by mid-year.

**Tax return due dates (when elections take effect):**
- S-corporation (Form 1120-S): March 15 (calendar-year filers). Extension available to September 15.
- C-corporation (Form 1120): April 15 (calendar-year filers). Extension available to October 15.
- Partnership (Form 1065): March 15 (calendar-year filers). Extension available to September 15.
- Sole proprietor (Schedule C with Form 1040): April 15. Extension available to October 15.

The Section 179 election and the election out of bonus depreciation must both be made by the due date of the return, including extensions. Once made, a Section 179 election can only be revoked with IRS consent.

**Form 3115 (catch-up/change):** For automatic consent depreciation method changes, Form 3115 is generally filed with the timely filed tax return for the year of change (including extensions). It must also be sent to the IRS National Office separately. Confirm procedural requirements with your advisor, as Revenue Procedure 2024-23 governs many automatic method changes.

**Cost segregation study:** The study must be completed and a Form 3115 filed for the year in which you want to implement the reclassification. There is no deadline per se for retroactive studies, but the further back the original placed-in-service date, the fewer remaining years there are to benefit from accelerated deductions.

**Annual record review:** Review your depreciation schedule every year in Q4. Identify assets that may need recapture calculations (disposals), assets reaching the end of their recovery period, and new purchases eligible for elections.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**S-corporations and partnerships:** Section 179 passes through to shareholders and partners on Schedule K-1. Each partner or shareholder applies the business-income limitation at their own level, not the entity level. Bonus depreciation also passes through and reduces each owner's basis in the entity. Track basis carefully -- deductions in excess of basis are suspended.

**Sole proprietors:** Report Section 179 and depreciation on Form 4562 attached to Schedule C. The Section 179 business income limitation is your net profit from self-employment before the Section 179 deduction.

**C-corporations:** The Section 179 deduction is taken at the corporate level. The business income limitation is the corporation's taxable income before the Section 179 deduction and NOL deduction. C-corps may benefit from bonus depreciation creating an NOL that carries forward at the corporate level.

**Passive activity rules:** If a business activity is a passive activity for you (you do not materially participate), depreciation deductions are passive losses. Passive losses generally can only offset passive income; they cannot offset W-2 wages or active business income. This is a common trap for real estate investors. Confirm your material participation status with your advisor.

**Real estate professional status:** A taxpayer who qualifies as a real estate professional under IRC Section 469(c)(7) (spending more than 750 hours per year and more than 50 percent of their working time in real property trades or businesses in which they materially participate) may treat rental real estate losses -- including large depreciation deductions -- as non-passive, allowing them to offset active income. This status requires careful documentation.

**Basis limitations:** In an S-corporation or partnership, your share of losses (including depreciation) cannot exceed your tax basis in the entity (your investment). Claim only deductions you have sufficient basis to support.

**State non-conformity examples (verify for your specific state with your advisor):**
- California: Does not conform to federal bonus depreciation at all for most property.
- New York: Conforms to federal Section 179 limit but has historically required an addback of federal bonus depreciation with a phase-in recovery over later years.
- New Jersey: Does not conform to bonus depreciation; has its own Section 179 limit.
- Texas: No corporate income tax; depreciation is primarily a federal issue.
- Florida: Generally conforms to federal depreciation rules for C-corporations.

Confirm your specific state's depreciation rules with your advisor before projecting state tax savings.

**Property acquired before January 19, 2025:** If you are placing in service in 2026 an asset that was acquired (purchased or ordered) on or before January 19, 2025, the OBBBA's permanent 100% bonus depreciation does not apply. Under the TCJA pre-OBBBA phase-down schedule, such property placed in service in 2026 qualifies for only 20% bonus depreciation (the phase-down was 40% for property placed in service in 2025, 20% for property placed in service in 2026, and 0% thereafter). Do not confuse this 20% phase-down rate with the separate voluntary 40% election available under IRC Section 168(k)(10), which allows taxpayers to elect 40% instead of 100% for qualifying OBBBA property placed in service in the first tax year ending after January 19, 2025 -- that election applies to new 100%-eligible property, not to pre-OBBBA phase-down property. Verify the acquisition date for any asset with a long lead time between order and delivery.

**Recapture planning:** If you plan to sell your business or any major depreciable asset within the next few years, large first-year deductions now mean more recapture income later. Your advisor should model both scenarios (claim full deductions now vs. spread over years) before you commit to a heavy front-loaded strategy.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing "placed in service" with "purchased" or "paid for."** Depreciation begins when an asset is ready and available for use, not when you ordered it or paid the deposit. A piece of equipment sitting in a crate that has not been installed does not qualify.

2. **Missing the mid-quarter convention trap.** If you buy a large piece of equipment in Q4 and it represents more than 40 percent of your year's depreciable additions, the mid-quarter convention applies to ALL personal property placed in service that year, not just the Q4 purchase. This dramatically reduces first-year deductions.

3. **Failing to maintain a mileage log for vehicles.** The IRS requires a contemporaneous log for every business vehicle. "I drove mostly for business" is not enough. Without a log, the deduction is vulnerable in audit.

4. **Claiming 100% business use on a vehicle without a log.** Every vehicle used partially for personal driving must reflect that in the business-use percentage. Overstating business use is a common audit trigger.

5. **Applying Section 179 or bonus depreciation to land or inventory.** Neither qualifies. The cost of land (versus the building on it) is never depreciable. Inventory held for sale is not a business asset subject to depreciation.

6. **Forgetting the Section 179 income limitation.** Owners sometimes plan their entire tax strategy around a Section 179 deduction they cannot use because taxable income is too low. Model income before making the election.

7. **Assuming all states follow federal depreciation rules.** Taking a large federal bonus depreciation deduction and assuming the same flows to your state return can result in a large state tax surprise. Verify state conformity.

8. **Not tracking depreciation historically in a schedule.** When you sell an asset, you need its entire depreciation history to compute the gain or loss and the recapture amount. Bookkeeping systems that do not maintain a running depreciation schedule create expensive reconstruction problems later.

9. **Skipping Form 3115 when prior depreciation was missed.** Many owners simply leave the money on the table. If your prior returns under-depreciated assets (wrong recovery period, missed bonus depreciation, never did a cost segregation study), a Form 3115 filing can unlock years of missed deductions in a single year.

10. **Disposing of a fully expensed asset in the same year it was purchased.** Claiming 100% bonus depreciation or Section 179 on an asset and then selling it before year end can trigger full recapture of the deduction as ordinary income in the same year, eliminating the tax benefit.

## Advisor Review Gates

Stop and review with your advisor before acting if any of the following apply:

1. **Your total 2026 depreciable asset purchases will exceed $1,000,000.** The interaction of Section 179, bonus depreciation, income limitations, and NOL carryforward rules becomes complex at scale and requires deliberate planning.

2. **You own a commercial building or have made leasehold improvements and have never had a cost segregation study.** The cost-benefit analysis and the Form 3115 mechanics require professional preparation.

3. **You are placing in service assets acquired before January 19, 2025.** The applicable bonus depreciation percentage (20% vs. 100%) depends on the acquisition date and the rules are different from the permanent OBBBA provision. Property acquired on or before January 19, 2025 and placed in service in 2026 qualifies for only 20% bonus depreciation under the TCJA pre-OBBBA phase-down schedule. Confirm before filing.

4. **You operate in multiple states.** State depreciation conformity varies widely and can produce unexpected state tax liabilities if the federal strategy is applied without a state-by-state analysis.

5. **You plan to sell the business, a major asset, or real property within the next 3 years.** Front-loaded depreciation today creates recapture exposure on exit. Model both paths with your advisor.

6. **Your business has a net loss before considering depreciation, or your Section 179 deduction would exceed your taxable income.** Understand how the excess carries forward and whether an NOL is beneficial in your specific situation.

7. **You are claiming depreciation on a vehicle and your business-use percentage is below 75%.** Borderline business use invites scrutiny; confirm your log supports the percentage before reporting it.

8. **You are considering a retroactive cost segregation study or a Form 3115 catch-up deduction.** These involve procedural requirements and potential audit risk if not properly executed.

9. **Your entity is an S-corporation or partnership with owners near their basis limit.** Confirm each owner has sufficient basis before passing through large depreciation deductions.

## Before You Act

Do not make Section 179 elections, claim bonus depreciation, file Form 3115, place major assets in service, or commit to a cost segregation study without first reviewing the specifics with your advisor. Depreciation elections directly affect your taxable income, your state tax liabilities, your basis in the entity, and your recapture exposure on future sales. The numbers in this playbook are verified for federal tax year 2026 and are intended to educate you on the strategy, not to constitute advice specific to your situation. Your entity type, state of filing, income level, prior depreciation history, and plans for your business all affect which elections make sense for you. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before filing.
