# Estimated Tax 30-Day Review Kit

> Educational guidance for Beyond Freedom Financial clients. Not personalized tax, legal, payroll, or state-law advice. Confirm your specific facts with your advisor before acting.

---

## Overview

Estimated taxes are quarterly payments you make directly to the IRS (and often your state) throughout the year to cover income tax and self-employment tax that no employer is withholding for you. Business owners who do not pay enough by each quarterly deadline owe a per-quarter underpayment penalty on top of any tax they owe at filing. This 30-day review kit walks you through calculating whether you are on track, making or adjusting any required payments, and setting a recurring quarterly calendar so you never miss a deadline or overpay unnecessarily.

---

## Who This Applies To

Use this kit if you fall into any of the following categories for tax year 2026:

- **Self-employed individuals (sole proprietors or single-member LLC owners filing on Schedule C):** You receive business income with no employer withholding, so you are responsible for both income tax and self-employment tax via quarterly estimated payments.
- **S-corporation owners who receive pass-through income above their W-2 salary:** Your W-2 wages have withholding, but your share of S-corp profit (reported on Schedule K-1) does not. If K-1 income pushes your total tax bill above the withholding on your wages, you likely owe estimated payments.
- **Partners in a partnership:** Partnership profit flows to you on Schedule K-1 with no withholding; you pay that tax through estimated payments.
- **Individuals with significant non-wage income:** Freelance earnings, rental income, capital gains, interest, dividends, alimony received, or any other income that is not subject to employer withholding can create an estimated tax obligation.
- **W-2 employees with a side business:** Your employer withholding covers your W-2 wages, but your side income still requires separate estimated payments (or an adjusted W-4 to increase wage withholding to cover both).

---

## When This Is Not A Fit

- **You expect to owe less than $1,000 in total tax after subtracting your withholding and any refundable credits on your 2026 return.** Below that threshold, no estimated payments are legally required and no underpayment penalty applies. (IRS Topic 306; IRC Section 6654(e)(1).)
- **Your W-2 withholding already covers all your expected tax.** If you have a job with high wages and your employer already withholds enough, you may have nothing left to pay quarterly. Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator to confirm.
- **You are a farmer or commercial fisherman.** Special estimated tax rules apply to farming and fishing income (different payment dates and a single-payment option). This kit covers the standard rules; ask your advisor about the farm/fish special rules.
- **You are a calendar-year corporation.** Corporations have separate estimated tax rules (Form 1120-W, 25% per quarter installments, different thresholds). This kit is for individuals, sole proprietors, S-corp shareholders, and partners.
- **Your prior-year tax liability was zero.** If you owed zero tax for 2025 and were a U.S. citizen or resident for the entire year, no estimated payments are required for 2026, regardless of how much you earn. (Publication 505, Chapter 2.)

## Key Terms

**Estimated tax:** A prepayment of income tax (and self-employment tax, if applicable) made in four quarterly installments during the tax year, because no employer is withholding those amounts from your pay. Governed by IRC Section 6654.

**Underpayment penalty:** A charge assessed by the IRS when you have not paid enough tax during the year through withholding and/or estimated payments. The penalty is calculated separately for each quarterly period -- you can owe a penalty for Q1 even if you overpay in Q3. Governed by IRC Section 6654.

**Safe harbor:** A rule that guarantees you will not owe an underpayment penalty, even if you end up owing tax at filing. There are three ways to reach safe harbor for 2026 (see "The Numbers" section).

**Required annual payment:** The minimum total you must pay (through withholding plus estimated payments combined) during 2026 to stay within safe harbor and avoid the underpayment penalty.

**Prior-year tax liability:** The total federal income tax shown on your 2025 Form 1040 (line 24, which is tax before credits, minus any credits that reduce final liability -- specifically your total tax after all credits on line 24, or the amount on Schedule 2). For safe harbor purposes, this is the figure from your most recently filed return. Ask your advisor or your tax software to confirm which line to use.

**Adjusted gross income (AGI):** Your total gross income minus specific adjustments listed on Schedule 1 of Form 1040 (such as the deductible portion of self-employment tax, health insurance premiums for self-employed individuals, and contributions to retirement accounts). AGI appears on line 11 of Form 1040. It is used to determine which safe harbor percentage applies to you.

**Self-employment (SE) tax:** The 15.3% tax that replaces the Social Security and Medicare taxes an employer would normally split with you. It applies to 92.35% of your net self-employment earnings (net profit multiplied by 0.9235 before applying the 15.3% rate). You may deduct half of the SE tax you owe as an above-the-line deduction when computing AGI. Computed on Schedule SE. (IRC Section 1401; see "The Numbers" for 2026 wage base.)

**Net self-employment earnings:** Your gross business revenue minus allowable business deductions (from Schedule C or your share of partnership or S-corp income treated as SE income), then multiplied by 92.35% before applying the SE tax rate.

**Additional Medicare Tax:** A 0.9% surtax on wages and self-employment income above certain thresholds (see "The Numbers"). It must be included in your estimated tax calculation if you expect to exceed the threshold. Reported on Form 8959.

**Annualized income installment method:** An alternative calculation that allows you to base each quarterly estimated payment on the income you actually earned in that specific quarter rather than an even 25% split. It can reduce or eliminate penalties for taxpayers whose income is uneven across the year. Requires completing Schedule AI of Form 2210.

**Form 1040-ES:** IRS form titled "Estimated Tax for Individuals." Contains the worksheet for calculating your estimated tax and the payment vouchers (paper option). Available at IRS.gov.

**Form 2210:** IRS form titled "Underpayment of Estimated Tax by Individuals, Estates, and Trusts." Used to calculate whether you owe a penalty and its amount. Submitted with your annual Form 1040 if a penalty applies.

**Schedule AI:** A schedule within Form 2210 used when you elect the annualized income installment method.

**Schedule SE:** The IRS schedule (attached to Form 1040) where self-employment tax is calculated.

**EFTPS:** Electronic Federal Tax Payment System, the U.S. Treasury's free online payment portal for federal taxes. Previously a separate system; as of 2026, new individual accounts are set up through IRS Online Account instead. Current EFTPS users can still use that system.

**IRS Direct Pay:** A free IRS online tool at IRS.gov/payments that lets you make a one-time federal tax payment directly from your bank account without registering for an account. Payments can be scheduled up to 365 days in advance.

**IRS Online Account:** The IRS's account portal (irs.gov/account) where you can view your tax balance, payment history, transcripts, and make payments.

**IRS Tax Withholding Estimator:** A free IRS online tool at IRS.gov/W4app that projects your full-year tax liability and tells you whether your current withholding and estimated payments are on track.

**Form W-4:** Employee's Withholding Certificate, submitted to your employer to set how much federal income tax is withheld from each paycheck. You can use line 4(c) of Form W-4 to request additional withholding to cover non-wage income, which is an alternative to making quarterly estimated payments.

**Period of limitations:** The window during which the IRS can audit your return and you can claim a refund. The standard period is three years from the date you filed (Publication 552 / Topic 305). Records supporting estimated payments should be kept for at least three years after you file the return for that tax year.

## Required Client Inputs

Gather these items before starting the 30-day review:

1. **Your 2025 Form 1040 (or your most recently filed return).** You need the total tax line (line 24 before credits, or line 24 total tax after credits -- ask your advisor which to use for safe harbor) to calculate your prior-year safe harbor amount.
2. **Your 2026 income projection, broken down by type:**
   - W-2 wages (from each employer)
   - Net profit from self-employment (Schedule C businesses)
   - S-corp K-1 ordinary income (Box 1) and any SE income from the entity
   - Partnership K-1 income
   - Rental income (net)
   - Capital gains expected (sales of investments, property, business assets)
   - Other taxable income (interest, dividends, alimony, etc.)
3. **Your expected business deductions** that will offset self-employment income.
4. **Year-to-date W-2 withholding** from each employer's most recent pay stub (year-to-date federal income tax withheld, Box 2 equivalent from stub).
5. **Any estimated tax payments already made for 2026**, with the date and amount of each.
6. **Your 2025 AGI** (line 11 of your 2025 Form 1040), to determine whether the 100% or 110% prior-year safe harbor applies to you.
7. **Your 2026 filing status** (single, married filing jointly, married filing separately, head of household).

## Required Documents And Records

**Collect before each quarterly payment:**

- Profit and loss statement for your business(es), updated through the month before the payment due date.
- Current pay stubs showing year-to-date withholding for any W-2 income.
- Brokerage or bank statements if you have capital gains or investment income to include.
- Prior payment confirmation (screenshot, bank record, or EFTPS/Direct Pay confirmation number) for any estimated payment already made in 2026.

**Keep after each payment:**

- Payment confirmation number or bank withdrawal record for every estimated tax payment made. Keep these with your tax files.
- The worksheet (the attached "Estimated Tax 30-Day Review Worksheet" or the Form 1040-ES worksheet) you used to calculate each payment, so you can show your reasoning if questioned.
- Your income projection spreadsheet or printout for each quarter.

**Retention:** Keep estimated tax records for a minimum of three years after the filing date of the return for the year the payment applies to. For 2026 payments, that means retain through at least April 2030 (or later if your return is extended or amended). If your income is understated by more than 25% of the gross income shown on your return, the IRS has six years to audit; keep those records six years to match. (IRS Topic 305; Publication 552.)

## Step-By-Step Implementation

Complete these steps within your 30-day review window. The review should occur in the month before each quarterly due date (March, May, August, and December).

**Step 1: Pull your prior-year tax figure.**
Open your 2025 Form 1040 and locate the total tax amount on line 24. Write that number on the attached worksheet in the "Prior-Year Tax" field. This is your baseline for the prior-year safe harbor calculation.

**Step 2: Calculate your prior-year safe harbor payment for the full year.**
If your 2025 AGI (Form 1040, line 11) was $150,000 or less (or $75,000 or less if you will file married filing separately for 2026), multiply the prior-year tax by 1.00 (100%). If your 2025 AGI exceeded those thresholds, multiply by 1.10 (110%). The result is the minimum total you must pay through withholding and estimated payments combined during 2026 to guarantee you avoid the underpayment penalty using the prior-year method. Divide this amount by 4 to get the equal quarterly installment amount.

Example (illustration only, not a tax projection): Prior-year tax $20,000 x 110% = $22,000 required annual payment. Divided by 4 = $5,500 per quarter.

**Step 3: Project your current-year tax liability.**
Using your income projections gathered in "Required Client Inputs," estimate your total 2026 federal tax as follows:
  - a. Estimate adjusted gross income: total income minus adjustments (half of SE tax, self-employed health insurance, retirement contributions).
  - b. Subtract the standard deduction (or your estimated itemized deductions if they exceed the standard deduction). Standard deduction amounts for 2026 are listed in "The Numbers."
  - c. Apply the 2026 tax brackets (also in "The Numbers") to compute estimated income tax.
  - d. Add estimated self-employment tax (net SE earnings x 92.35% x 15.3%).
  - e. Add Additional Medicare Tax if your total wages and SE income will exceed the thresholds in "The Numbers."
  - f. Subtract any tax credits you expect (child tax credit, education credits, etc.).
  - g. The result is your projected total 2026 tax liability.

**Step 4: Calculate your required annual payment using the current-year method.**
Multiply the projected 2026 tax liability from Step 3 by 90%. This is the alternative safe harbor target: if your total payments (withholding plus estimated) will equal at least 90% of your actual 2026 liability, you avoid the penalty.

**Step 5: Choose the lower of the two safe harbor targets.**
Compare the prior-year safe harbor amount (Step 2) with the 90%-of-current-year amount (Step 4). You only need to meet the lower of the two. Use that lower number as your required annual payment target.

**Step 6: Subtract withholding already paid.**
From your year-to-date pay stubs, total all federal income tax already withheld in 2026. If you will receive additional withholding before year-end, project the full-year withholding amount. Subtract total expected withholding from your required annual payment target. The remainder is how much you must pay through estimated payments.

**Step 7: Subtract estimated payments already made.**
Total all estimated tax payments you have already submitted for 2026 (with confirmation of receipt). Subtract from the amount calculated in Step 6. The result is the remaining estimated tax you still need to pay across the remaining quarters.

**Step 8: Allocate remaining payments evenly across remaining quarters.**
Divide the remaining balance from Step 7 by the number of payment periods that have not yet passed. That is your target payment for each upcoming quarter. Enter each amount and due date on the worksheet's payment schedule section.

**Step 9: Make or schedule your next payment.**
Submit your payment using one of these IRS methods (all free):
  - **IRS Direct Pay** (directpay.irs.gov): No account needed. Select "Estimated Tax," tax year 2026. Schedule up to 365 days in advance.
  - **IRS Online Account** (irs.gov/account): Log in and pay under "Make a Payment."
  - **EFTPS** (eftps.gov): If you already have an EFTPS account, you can still use it to schedule payments.
  - **Paper check with Form 1040-ES voucher:** Mail the payment voucher from Form 1040-ES with a check payable to "United States Treasury," noting "2026 Form 1040-ES" and your Social Security number on the memo line. Mail to the address in the Form 1040-ES instructions for your state.

Record the confirmation number or check number on your worksheet and in your tax file.

**Step 10: Set a calendar reminder for the next quarter.**
Add a recurring reminder approximately 30 days before each remaining 2026 due date (March 15, May 15, August 15, and December 15) to run this review again. Each quarterly review uses the same steps, updated with your most recent income and withholding data.

**Step 11: Consider adjusting W-4 withholding instead of (or in addition to) estimated payments.**
If you or your spouse have W-2 wages, you can eliminate the need to make manual estimated payments by requesting additional withholding on Form W-4, line 4(c). Withholding is treated as paid evenly throughout the year for penalty purposes, even if increased mid-year, which can help cure an underpayment from earlier quarters. Talk to your advisor about whether a W-4 adjustment is simpler than quarterly estimated payments for your situation.

**Step 12: Flag large income changes to your advisor before the next due date.**
If you close a large deal, sell property, receive an unexpected distribution, or lose a major revenue source between now and the next due date, that is a trigger for an off-cycle recalculation. Do not wait for the scheduled quarterly review.

## The Numbers

All figures are for tax year 2026 and verified against the sources cited in the "## Sources" section.

**Estimated tax threshold (must pay if you expect to owe this much after withholding and credits):**
$1,000 (IRC Section 6654(e)(1); IRS Topic 306)

**Safe harbor options to avoid the underpayment penalty -- meet any one of these three:**

1. **Owe less than $1,000 at filing:** Your 2026 return shows less than $1,000 remaining after subtracting withholding and refundable credits. (IRS Topic 306.)
2. **90% of current-year tax:** Your total payments (withholding plus estimated payments) equal at least 90% of your actual 2026 federal tax liability. (IRS Topic 306; Publication 505, Chapter 2.)
3. **Prior-year safe harbor:** Your total payments equal at least 100% (or 110% for higher-income taxpayers, see below) of the total tax shown on your 2025 Form 1040. (IRS Topic 306; Publication 505, Chapter 2.)

**Prior-year safe harbor percentage:**
- **100%** if your 2025 AGI (line 11 of your 2025 Form 1040) was $150,000 or less, or $75,000 or less if your 2026 filing status is married filing separately.
- **110%** if your 2025 AGI exceeded $150,000 (or $75,000 for married filing separately).
(IRS Publication 505, Chapter 2; IRS search results confirming the $150,000/$75,000 thresholds.)

**Self-employment (SE) tax rate:** 15.3% total -- 12.4% Social Security portion plus 2.9% Medicare portion. (IRS Topic 554; IRS Topic 751.)

**SE tax applies to:** 92.35% of net self-employment earnings (that is, net profit multiplied by 0.9235 before applying the 15.3% rate). (IRS Topic 554.)

**Social Security wage base for 2026:** $184,500. The 12.4% Social Security portion of SE tax applies only to net SE earnings up to this amount. The 2.9% Medicare portion has no upper limit. (IRS Topic 751; SSA Contribution and Benefit Base.)

**Additional Medicare Tax:** 0.9% on wages and/or net SE income above:
- $200,000 for single filers and heads of household
- $250,000 for married filing jointly
- $125,000 for married filing separately
(IRS Topic 560; IRC Section 3103.)

Reported on Form 8959, filed with Form 1040. Must be factored into estimated tax payments if you expect to exceed the applicable threshold.

**Deduction for half of SE tax:** You may deduct 50% of your total self-employment tax as an above-the-line deduction (Schedule 1, Part II) when computing AGI. (IRC Section 164(f).) This reduces your income tax and also reduces your AGI, which matters for the safe harbor calculation.

**2026 standard deduction amounts:**
- Single / Married filing separately: $16,100
- Married filing jointly: $32,200
- Head of household: $24,150
(IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32; IRS newsroom "IRS releases tax inflation adjustments for tax year 2026.")

**2026 federal income tax brackets (for estimated tax projection):**

Single filers:
- 10% on taxable income up to $12,400
- 12% on income from $12,401 to $50,400
- 22% on income from $50,401 to $105,700
- 24% on income from $105,701 to $201,775
- 32% on income from $201,776 to $256,225
- 35% on income from $256,226 to $640,600
- 37% on income above $640,600

Married filing jointly:
- 10% on taxable income up to $24,800
- 12% on income from $24,801 to $100,800
- 22% on income from $100,801 to $211,400
- 24% on income from $211,401 to $403,550
- 32% on income from $403,551 to $512,450
- 35% on income from $512,451 to $768,700
- 37% on income above $768,700

(IRS newsroom "IRS releases tax inflation adjustments for tax year 2026, including amendments from the One, Big, Beautiful Bill.")

**2026 underpayment penalty interest rate (federal short-term rate + 3 percentage points; set quarterly):**
- Q1 2026 (January 1 -- March 31): 7% per year, compounded daily
- Q2 2026 (April 1 -- June 30): 6% per year, compounded daily
- Q3 2026 (July 1 -- September 30): 7% per year, compounded daily
- Q4 2026 (October 1 -- December 31): [confirm with your advisor -- could not verify against a public source for Q4 at time of drafting; IRS publishes quarterly rates in the Internal Revenue Bulletin approximately 3 months before each quarter begins]

(IRS newsroom Q1 2026 interest rate announcement; IRS IRB 2026-22.)

**Payment limits for IRS Direct Pay:** $10 million per transaction. No single-payment limit for IRS Online Account or EFTPS.

## Entity, Payroll, State, And Current-Year Caveats

**S-corporation owners -- wages vs. distributions:** Your W-2 salary from the S-corp has federal income tax withheld, which counts toward your required annual payment. Your S-corp profit distributions (cash pulled out beyond your salary) have no withholding and do not carry SE tax, but they do increase your individual income tax bill. If your K-1 profit is large relative to your salary, estimated payments are almost always required. Do not confuse the S-corp's own estimated tax obligations (S-corps are pass-through entities and generally do not pay federal corporate income tax themselves) with your personal obligation.

**Reasonable compensation requirement:** If you are an S-corp owner, the IRS requires you to pay yourself a reasonable W-2 wage for services you perform before taking profit distributions. Estimated tax planning cannot substitute for, or be used to reduce, your required reasonable compensation. Get your advisor's input on the correct salary level.

**State estimated taxes:** Most states that have income taxes also require quarterly estimated payments on a similar schedule to the federal system, but the thresholds, safe harbor percentages, and due dates often differ. Some states have no income tax. Several states (California, for example) have a different Q1 due date and higher income-based safe harbor requirements. This kit covers federal estimated taxes only. Ask your advisor about your state's requirements in the same 30-day review window.

**Self-employment tax is in addition to income tax:** Many first-year business owners are surprised that SE tax adds up to 15.3% on top of income tax. On net self-employment profit of $100,000, SE tax alone is approximately $14,130 (calculated as $100,000 x 0.9235 x 0.153). Include this in your projections. Failing to account for SE tax is one of the most common causes of underpayment penalties for self-employed individuals.

**Uneven income -- use the annualized method if needed:** If most of your income arrives in one or two quarters (common for commission-based, consulting, or seasonal businesses), the equal-installment safe harbor can be inefficient -- you may pay too much early and still technically underpay in a later quarter. In that case, use the annualized income installment method (Form 2210, Schedule AI). Talk to your advisor about whether this method applies to your income pattern.

**Capital gains:** If you sell appreciated property, stock, or a business during 2026, the resulting capital gain can dramatically increase your tax liability for that quarter. Make an estimated payment in the quarter in which the gain occurs; do not wait until year-end.

**Alternative minimum tax (AMT):** The estimated tax worksheet in Form 1040-ES includes an AMT line. If you have significant ISO (incentive stock option) exercises, large accelerated depreciation, or other AMT preference items, your advisor should review whether you are subject to AMT in 2026 and whether it changes your estimated tax calculation.

**Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT):** A 3.8% tax applies to net investment income (interest, dividends, capital gains, rental income, and similar passive income) for taxpayers whose modified AGI exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). If applicable, include it in your current-year liability estimate. Reported on Form 8960. (IRC Section 1411.)

**Fiscal-year taxpayers:** If your business uses a fiscal year other than the calendar year, your due dates and payment periods are different. This kit assumes a calendar tax year. Consult your advisor.

**Overpayment credit election:** If you overpaid in 2025 and elected to apply your refund to 2026 estimated taxes on your 2025 return, that credit counts toward your 2026 required annual payment as of the original due date of the 2025 return (April 15, 2026). Include it in your calculation in Step 6.

## Common Mistakes

**1. Using gross revenue instead of net profit for SE tax.** SE tax is based on net self-employment earnings (after business deductions), not top-line revenue. Overstating the SE tax base leads to overcalculating required payments; understating it (by not tracking deductions) leads to underpayment.

**2. Forgetting the 92.35% multiplier.** The SE tax rate of 15.3% applies to only 92.35% of your net SE earnings, not 100%. Calculate correctly: net profit x 0.9235 x 0.1530.

**3. Paying four equal installments of 25% without updating projections.** If your income changes significantly during the year and you keep paying the same flat amount, you may overpay (costing you the use of your money) or underpay in later quarters (triggering a penalty for those quarters only). Rerun the worksheet each quarter.

**4. Applying the wrong prior-year safe harbor percentage.** If your 2025 AGI exceeded $150,000 ($75,000 for married filing separately), you need to base your prior-year safe harbor on 110%, not 100%, of your 2025 tax. Using 100% when 110% is required leaves you exposed to the penalty.

**5. Not counting withholding from a W-2 job or from an IRA distribution.** Withholding from wages (Box 2 of W-2), IRA distributions, and other sources all counts toward your required annual payment. Forgetting to include this withholding causes you to overpay estimated taxes unnecessarily.

**6. Missing one quarter and thinking it does not matter.** The underpayment penalty is computed per quarter. A missed Q1 payment results in a Q1 penalty even if Q2-Q4 payments are large. Do not skip a quarter intending to "make it up later."

**7. Not paying state estimated taxes on the same schedule.** Federal and state estimated taxes are separate obligations. Focusing only on federal payments and ignoring state requirements results in state underpayment penalties and interest.

**8. Confusing the S-corp's quarterly payroll deposits with personal estimated taxes.** The S-corp's employer portion of FICA and the federal income tax withheld from your W-2 are deposited separately (via EFTPS as a business payment for the S-corp). Your personal estimated income tax on K-1 income is a separate personal payment under your own Social Security number.

**9. Failing to account for a large capital gain in the quarter it occurs.** If you sell property in Q2 and wait until the Q4 payment to account for the gain, you will owe an underpayment penalty for Q2 and Q3.

**10. Not confirming that a Direct Pay or EFTPS payment actually cleared.** Save every payment confirmation number. Payments that fail due to an incorrect bank account number or insufficient funds do not count, and you remain liable for the tax, the penalty, and possibly a returned-payment fee.

## Advisor Review Gates

Pause and contact your advisor before acting if any of the following applies:

1. **Your income or circumstances changed significantly since your last annual return.** Triggers include: you started or closed a business, you changed entity type (e.g., elected S-corp status), your income is more than 20% above or below last year, or you got married, divorced, or became a dependent.

2. **You had a large capital gain or unusual income event in 2026.** A business sale, real estate sale, large stock sale, or receipt of a legal settlement changes your quarterly liability calculation in the quarter it occurs.

3. **Your prior-year AGI was above $150,000 (or $75,000 MFS) and you are not sure which safe harbor percentage to use.** Using 100% when you need 110% will leave you exposed to the penalty.

4. **You are subject to the Additional Medicare Tax or NIIT.** These taxes require additional calculation steps and may increase your quarterly payment amounts.

5. **You earn income in multiple states or relocated during 2026.** State estimated tax rules vary substantially. Your advisor needs to review obligations in each applicable state.

6. **Your income is highly uneven across quarters.** Before electing the annualized income installment method (Form 2210, Schedule AI), confirm with your advisor that the method applies to your pattern and that you have adequate records to support each quarter's annualized calculation.

7. **You want to adjust your W-4 withholding rather than make estimated payments.** A Form W-4 change requires employer action, and the timing affects how much withholding is credited to each quarter. Your advisor can confirm whether adjusting withholding or making estimated payments is better for your situation.

8. **You are already in a Q1 or Q2 underpayment.** If you missed an earlier quarter entirely and are catching up now, your advisor should run the Form 2210 calculation to quantify the penalty you already owe and determine the optimal path forward.

## Using The Download(s)

**Estimated Tax 30-Day Review Worksheet**

Use this worksheet during Step 1 through Step 10 of the implementation above. The worksheet is organized in the same numbered sequence as the steps.

- **Section 1 (Prior-Year Tax):** Enter your 2025 Form 1040 total tax figure. The worksheet will prompt you to indicate your 2025 AGI and automatically flag whether the 100% or 110% safe harbor percentage applies.
- **Section 2 (Prior-Year Safe Harbor Target):** The worksheet multiplies your prior-year tax by 100% or 110% (based on your input) and divides by 4 to show your per-quarter target using this method.
- **Section 3 (Current-Year Projection):** Enter your income projections, estimated deductions, and expected credits. The worksheet calculates estimated income tax, SE tax, Additional Medicare Tax (if applicable), and total current-year liability.
- **Section 4 (90% Current-Year Target):** The worksheet multiplies Section 3 by 90% and shows the alternative safe harbor target.
- **Section 5 (Payments Made):** Enter each estimated payment already made (date, method, amount, confirmation number) and your projected full-year withholding. The worksheet totals these amounts and subtracts them from your required annual payment.
- **Section 6 (Remaining Balance and Quarterly Schedule):** The worksheet divides the remaining balance across upcoming quarters, showing how much to pay and by which due date.
- **Section 7 (Payment Log):** Record each payment confirmation number as you make payments. Keep this worksheet with your tax records for the three-year retention period.

Run the worksheet once per quarter (approximately 30 days before each due date) and retain each completed version as a dated record of your calculation.

## Before You Act

Before making or skipping any estimated tax payment, confirm your income projections, prior-year tax figure, withholding credits, and applicable safe harbor percentage with your advisor. The numbers you use determine whether you owe a penalty. If you are unsure about any figure, mark it as uncertain on the worksheet and ask your advisor to review it before you finalize the payment amount.

Do not base your estimated payments on this worksheet alone if your income is significantly different from last year, if you had a large capital event, if you are subject to AMT, NIIT, or Additional Medicare Tax, or if you operate in multiple states. Those situations require personalized analysis.

This page teaches you the process and the rules. The correct numbers for your specific situation require review by your advisor.
